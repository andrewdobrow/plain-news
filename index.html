5:46 PM ET

More than 245 people are suspected to have died from Ebola in the Democratic Republic of Congo, with 246 suspected cases reported in Ituri province. The outbreak has prompted international health organizations to mobilize emergency response teams to support local health workers in the region.

The World Health Organization director visited the worst-affected area as the medical charity Doctors Without Borders described the spread as deeply alarming. The WHO estimates the death rate at between 30 percent and 50 percent based on confirmed cases. Health workers in the area face significant challenges, lacking adequate protective equipment and treatment supplies to handle the outbreak effectively.

The virus has spread across multiple communities in eastern Congo, with cases reported mainly in the Mongwalu and Rwampara health zones as well as the neighboring city of Bunia. The outbreak represents one of the deadliest Ebola events in recent years, with transmission continuing despite containment efforts.

Ituri province presents particular difficulties for disease control. The region is a mining hub where thousands of people work in close proximity, and ongoing armed conflict has displaced populations into overcrowded camps. This combination of factors creates conditions that make controlling transmission extremely challenging. The virus has already spread beyond the DRC, with Uganda confirming cases after a 59-year-old man died in a Kampala hospital after traveling from the affected region.

A significant concern is the strain of Ebola involved in this outbreak. The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention confirmed that samples tested positive for the Bundibugyo strain, for which no licensed vaccine exists. This differs from the more common Zaire strain found in previous DRC outbreaks. Only two previous outbreaks of Bundibugyo virus occurred, in 2007 and 2012.

International health organizations are working to establish treatment centers and trace contacts of infected individuals. Medical teams are focusing on isolating patients, implementing safe burial practices, and vaccinating at-risk populations to slow the virus's spread. The Africa CDC convened an urgent meeting with authorities from the DRC, Uganda, and South Sudan, along with the WHO and pharmaceutical companies, to coordinate regional response efforts.

Ebola is a severe viral disease spread through direct contact with body fluids of infected people or dead bodies, particularly during funeral preparations. The virus has a high fatality rate, as demonstrated by the 2014-16 west African outbreak that resulted in approximately 28,000 cases and 11,000 deaths.

The WHO's visit and the convening of regional coordination meetings signal international recognition of the outbreak's severity. Officials have emphasized that rapid coordination across borders is essential given the population movement between affected areas and neighboring countries.

A New Glenn rocket owned by Blue Origin exploded on a launchpad at Cape Canaveral in Florida on Thursday evening, damaging NASA's plans to establish a lunar base and return astronauts to the moon within the next two years.

The explosion occurred at 9 p.m. ET during a static fire test, when the rocket remains anchored to the ground. A massive fireball destroyed the launchpad at Kennedy Space Center, creating an orange sky visible as far south as Fort Pierce, 115 miles away. Shockwaves from the blast were felt across Florida's space coast, with residents in nearby Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach reporting that their homes shook. Emergency officials confirmed there was no threat from fumes or other hazards.

Blue Origin founder Jeff Bezos said all company employees were accounted for and safe. He described the incident as "a very rough day" and said the company was investigating the cause, calling it an "anomaly."

The timing of the explosion creates significant complications for NASA. Just hours before the blast, Blue Origin had won a contract to launch the first of three planned missions this year to begin construction of the $20 billion moon base. The New Glenn rocket is intended to launch landers to the lunar surface for NASA's Artemis program.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said a full evaluation of mission timelines would be conducted following the explosion. "Spaceflight is unforgiving, and developing new heavy-lift launch capability is extraordinarily difficult," he wrote on social media.

The setback strengthens SpaceX's position in the competition for lunar contracts. SpaceX's Starship Human Landing System is already competing with Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander to determine which will transport the Artemis IV crew to the lunar surface in 2028. NASA had been working to diversify its launch options to avoid depending on any single company, but the explosion now leaves SpaceX as the only operational option for the agency's near-term moon exploration plans.

This is the latest in a series of problems for Blue Origin. Last month, a payload from the company's third New Glenn flight ended up in the wrong orbit, triggering a temporary grounding by the Federal Aviation Administration. Thursday's static fire test was the first since the FAA cleared the rocket to return to flight.

Elon Musk, SpaceX's founder and owner of the social media platform where much commentary appeared, simply wrote "Most unfortunate" and "Rockets are hard."

Blue Origin and SpaceX have both constructed large facilities near Cape Canaveral to support crewed and cargo missions in partnership with NASA.

3:52 PM ET

The U.S. military attacked and seized an Iranian-flagged container ship attempting to breach an American blockade near the Strait of Hormuz on Sunday, marking the first such interception since the blockade began last week.

According to U.S. Central Command, marines departed the USS Tripoli assault ship by helicopter and rappelled onto the vessel, named Touska. President Trump posted on social media that a U.S. Navy guided missile destroyer had warned the ship to stop in the Gulf of Oman, but the vessel refused to comply.

"Our Navy ship stopped them right in their tracks by blowing a hole in the engine room," Trump said. He confirmed that U.S. marines now had custody of the vessel and noted it was under U.S. Treasury sanctions due to prior illegal activity.

Video footage released by the U.S. defense department shows the USS Spruance firing on the Touska. Audio in the video captures a U.S. sailor warning the cargo ship's crew: "Vacate your engine room. We're prepared to subject you to disabling fire."

The ship had departed Port Klang, a major port in Malaysia, before attempting to move past the U.S. blockade near Iran's border with Pakistan, according to tracking information.

Iran's joint military command swiftly condemned the action, calling it piracy that violated the ceasefire in place since April 8. A spokesperson for Khatam al-Anbiya, Iran's military command center, warned that "the armed forces of the Islamic Republic of Iran will soon respond and retaliate against this armed piracy and the US military."

The seizure creates uncertainty around diplomatic efforts. Trump had previously announced that U.S. negotiators would travel to Pakistan on Monday for another round of talks with Iran aimed at extending the fragile ceasefire set to expire Wednesday. However, Iran has not confirmed it will attend the negotiations.

Iranian state media reported that Tehran had rejected new peace talks, citing the ongoing blockade, threatening rhetoric, and what officials described as Washington's shifting positions and excessive demands.

The escalating standoff threatens to worsen the global energy crisis and push the two countries toward renewed conflict. The current tensions have already resulted in significant casualties, with at least 3,000 deaths in Iran, nearly 2,300 in Lebanon, 38 in Israel, and more than a dozen in Gulf Arab states. Thirteen U.S. service members have also been killed.

The incident represents the latest enforcement action as the U.S. maintains its naval blockade in the region as part of its broader Gulf strategy. The Strait of Hormuz remains a critical waterway, with roughly one-fifth of the world's petroleum passing through it under normal conditions.
