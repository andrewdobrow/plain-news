Five people died and 37 others were injured when a bus struck multiple vehicles on Interstate 95 in Stafford County, Virginia. The collision occurred on the southbound lanes of the highway, one of the most heavily traveled routes on the East Coast.

Among those killed were a 13-year-old and a 7-year-old, according to state police. The identities of the three other victims have not been released publicly.

Emergency responders arrived at the scene in Stafford County, located approximately 50 miles south of Washington, D.C. Injured passengers were transported from the scene, though authorities have not specified the severity of injuries sustained by those hurt in the crash.

The collision forced authorities to shut down portions of I-95, disrupting traffic on a major transportation corridor that connects Washington, D.C. and Richmond and serves as a critical artery for both commuters and long-distance travelers moving along the eastern seaboard.

State police are investigating the circumstances that led to the bus striking the other vehicles. As of now, officials have not released details about what caused the collision, the bus operator's identity, the type of bus involved, or where it was traveling.

The crash represents one of the deadliest traffic incidents in Virginia in recent months. Interstate 95 carries significant daily traffic volumes, making safety incidents particularly concerning given the number of people who use the route regularly.

Authorities continue to gather information about the incident. State police have not yet provided additional details about the vehicles involved beyond confirming the bus struck multiple cars, or about the circumstances surrounding the collision.

The incident underscores the dangers present on busy interstate highways, where high speeds and heavy traffic volumes can result in devastating consequences when crashes occur. The I-95 corridor has been the site of numerous serious traffic incidents over the years.

Investigators are working to piece together exactly what happened in the moments leading up to the collision. Witnesses or anyone with information about the crash are being asked to contact state police as the investigation continues.

The crash has drawn attention to highway safety issues, particularly involving large vehicles like buses on major interstate routes. The significant number of injuries and fatalities highlights the potential for mass casualty incidents on heavily traveled highways during peak traffic periods.

A Russian drone struck a residential apartment building in the Romanian city of Galati on Friday, injuring two people and triggering swift condemnation from NATO and European Union officials. The incident marks a significant escalation in tensions between the alliance and Moscow.

Romanian authorities said the drone was likely hit by Ukrainian air defenses while operating over Ukraine, which altered its trajectory before it crashed into the building. The impact sparked a fire and forced the evacuation of several residents. Emergency services responded to the scene, and the Romanian military scrambled two F-16 fighter jets and a helicopter.

NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte responded forcefully to the incident. "Russia's reckless behaviour is a danger to us all," Rutte wrote on social media following a call with Romanian President Nicusor Dan. "I affirmed that Nato stands ready to defend every inch of allied territory."

Romania's foreign minister, Oana Toiu, declared the strike a "grave and irresponsible escalation" and a serious breach of international law. She announced that Romania would formally communicate consequences for the incident and stated the country would pursue diplomatic repercussions and European-level sanctions against Russia.

The Romanian government formally requested NATO support and indicated it would invoke Article 4 of the NATO treaty, which allows member states to request consultations when they believe their territorial integrity or security is threatened. Unlike Article 5, which triggered automatic collective defense only once in NATO history following the September 11 attacks, Article 4 provides a forum for members to discuss security concerns without automatic military response.

Romania also asked NATO to accelerate the transfer of anti-drone capabilities to strengthen its defenses. The government instructed the foreign ministry to prepare a package of measures regarding Romania's relationship with Moscow, with President Dan saying his country would not accept the Ukraine war being transferred to its citizens.

Vladimir Putin suggested without evidence that the drone might have been a stray Ukrainian weapon and claimed he had only just learned of the incident. He called for the drone wreckage to be handed over to Russia for an independent investigation.

European leaders united in condemning the strike. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyder stated that Russia's "war of aggression has crossed yet another line," while France's foreign minister Jean-Noel Barrot denounced what he called an irresponsible act. Ukraine's foreign minister said the incident demonstrated that Russian aggression poses a threat to the entire European continent.

General Gheorghe Maxim, a senior commander of Romania's armed forces, characterized the strike as evidence that Russia represents a security threat to countries in the region, though he stopped short of calling it a direct attack on Romania itself.

A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded during a static fire test at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station's Launch Complex 36 in Florida on Thursday night at 9pm ET. The massive fireball destroyed the launchpad seconds after the scheduled test began, with the orange glow visible as far south as Fort Pierce, 115 miles away. Shockwaves from the blast were felt across Florida's space coast, with residents in nearby Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach reporting that their homes shook. People hundreds of miles north in South Carolina also reported seeing the glow in the sky.

Blue Origin confirmed that all employees were accounted for and safe, with no personnel injured in the incident. The company described the explosion as "an anomaly" and has not yet disclosed the root cause. Company founder Jeff Bezos acknowledged it was "a very rough day" but said the team was already working to identify what went wrong and would "rebuild whatever needs rebuilding and get back to flying."

The explosion represents a significant setback for NASA's Artemis program, which depends on commercial partners including Blue Origin. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman stated that a full evaluation of the program's timeline would be conducted following the incident. "Spaceflight is unforgiving, and developing new heavy-lift launch capability is extraordinarily difficult," Isaacman said, adding that NASA would work with partners to support a thorough investigation and assess impacts to Artemis and the lunar base program.

The New Glenn rocket is a heavy-lift vehicle designed to be partially reusable, with the first stage intended to land back on Earth after launch. Blue Origin had planned to use New Glenn rockets to launch landers to the moon for NASA, including those that would carry astronauts to the lunar surface. Just days before the explosion, NASA had awarded Blue Origin a contract to launch the first of three planned missions this year to begin construction of a $20 billion moon base.

This explosion marks another setback for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA contracts. Last month, a payload from the third flight of New Glenn ended up in the wrong orbit, leading the Federal Aviation Administration to temporarily ground the rocket. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared New Glenn to return to flight the previous week.

The incident adds to the challenges facing commercial space companies developing reliable launch systems. Both Blue Origin and SpaceX have built large facilities near Cape Canaveral to support crewed and cargo missions in partnership with NASA. Artemis III, planned for 2027, is scheduled to test both Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander and SpaceX's Starship Human Landing System to determine which will transport the Artemis IV crew to the lunar surface in 2028.

The Federal Communications Commission has warned all broadcasters to comply with its orders or face consequences similar to those imposed on ABC, according to the network.

ABC says the FCC granted early license renewals for all of its broadcast stations in what the network describes as an unprecedented move with no legitimate purpose. The network has challenged the action, questioning both its timing and justification.

The warning from the Trump-appointed FCC leadership represents a shift in how the commission is approaching broadcaster oversight. License renewals typically follow a standard schedule and process, but the ABC situation marks a departure from those norms.

The commission has positioned the ABC case as a template for how it will handle broadcaster compliance going forward. The nature of the original orders ABC allegedly violated, and the specific punishments the network faces, remain part of an ongoing dispute between the broadcaster and the regulatory agency.

Broadcasters operate under FCC licenses that must be renewed periodically. The commission has authority to impose conditions on those licenses or deny renewals if stations fail to meet regulatory requirements. However, ABC contends the early renewal process it experienced falls outside normal regulatory procedures.

The development adds to tensions between the current administration and media companies. The FCC, as an independent agency, has five commissioners appointed by the president and confirmed by the Senate. The Trump administration's appointees now control the majority of seats on the commission.

Industry observers note that using one broadcaster's case as a public warning to others marks an unusual approach to regulatory enforcement. Traditional FCC practice involves addressing individual cases on their merits rather than holding them up as examples to influence other licensees' behavior.

ABC has not disclosed whether it plans legal action beyond its current challenge to the license renewal process. Other major broadcasters have not publicly commented on the FCC's warning or indicated how they might adjust their operations in response.

Brazilian teenager Joao Fonseca produced a stunning comeback to defeat Novak Djokovic in a five-set match in the third round of the French Open, ending the tennis legend's pursuit of a record 25th Grand Slam singles title.

Fonseca, 19, won the epic encounter 4-6, 4-6, 6-3, 7-5, 7-5 after four hours and 53 minutes on Court Philippe-Chatrier. The victory is particularly remarkable given that Djokovic had established a commanding two-set lead, appearing to be in full control of the match.

The physical toll of the contest became evident as the match progressed. After four hours of intense play, the 39-year-old Djokovic was visibly struggling. The passage of time worked in Fonseca's favour as daylight faded and the court slowed down, allowing him to find his rhythm and mount his extraordinary comeback.

Fonseca's achievement places him in rare historical company. He became only the second player ever to defeat Djokovic after the Serbian champion won the opening two sets. Djokovic's previous only loss from a 2-0 set advantage came against Jürgen Melzer at the 2010 French Open, when Fonseca was just three years old.

Speaking after his victory, Fonseca expressed his disbelief at the outcome. "I didn't, really," he said when asked if he believed he could win from two sets down. "It's my first time stepping on court with him, he's a legend. I'm just really happy."

The teenager credited his powerful serving and aggressive approach to turning the match around. "I wasn't thinking about the occasion, I was just trying to hit the ball as hard as I could," Fonseca explained. He was also delighted with his serving display, remarking that he felt like John Isner as he landed three consecutive aces. "I had no idea I could hit them like this," he said.

Fonseca paid tribute to his supporters after the match, thanking his mother, whose birthday it was, and the Brazilian fans who backed him throughout the contest. The Brazilian teenager has now advanced to the fourth round, where he will face either Casper Ruud or Tommy Paul.

The victory marks a significant milestone in Fonseca's young career and demonstrates the depth of emerging talent in men's professional tennis. Despite Djokovic's vast experience and his reputation for finding victories from difficult positions, the teenager's courageous and headstrong performance ultimately denied the veteran any path back into the match.

This result ensures that Djokovic's quest for a record-breaking 25th Grand Slam title will have to continue at future tournaments.

A few minutes ago

A federal judge ruled that the Kennedy Center's decision to add President Donald Trump's name to the building was illegal and ordered the removal of all signage bearing his name within two weeks.

U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper directed the Trump administration to take down physical signs and eliminate any references to a "Trump Kennedy Center" from official materials. Cooper stated the iconic Washington D.C. performing arts venue "cannot bear any other formal name or public memorial" without an act of Congress.

The Kennedy Center's board of trustees, which Trump had restructured after taking office, voted to rename the facility the Trump Kennedy Center. The president appointed himself chair of the board and oversaw the addition of Trump's name to the building's exterior facade. However, legal experts and lawmakers argued that renaming a national memorial requires congressional approval.

The ruling also blocked the Kennedy Center's planned two-year closure for renovation that was announced in connection with the naming change. This closure would have disrupted programming and shuttered the facility for an extended period while renovations were completed.

The controversy sparked significant backlash from the arts community. Prominent musicals including Hamilton canceled performances at the venue. Actors like Issa Rae and author Louise Penny withdrew from scheduled appearances. Musicians and consultants including Ben Folds and singer Renée Fleming resigned from advisory roles. The executive director of the National Symphony Orchestra, Jean Davidson, departed to head the Los Angeles-based Wallis Annenberg Center for the Performing Arts.

The Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts was originally established as the National Cultural Center under legislation signed by President Dwight D. Eisenhower in 1958. It was renamed to honor President John F. Kennedy following his assassination and serves as the nation's official memorial to him.

Trump had made the Kennedy Center a focal point of his second term, particularly in what he characterized as his campaign against "woke" culture. He ousted the center's previous leadership and installed a hand-picked board. The venue became an extension of the White House, hosting events including a FIFA World Cup draw in December and the Kennedy Center Honors, the first such event hosted by a sitting president.

The fallout from renaming efforts prompted Trump to replace Ric Grenell, the Republican foreign policy adviser who oversaw changes at the center, with Matt Floca, vice president of operations. According to Trump, Grenell had done a poor job managing publicity during his tenure, contributing to artists canceling appearances.

Kennedy Center officials have not yet commented on how they will comply with the court order or whether they plan to appeal the decision. The two-week deadline requires the Trump name to be physically removed from the building.

Judge orders Trump's name removed from Kennedy Center, blocks closure

A federal judge on Friday ruled that President Trump's name must be removed from the Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, finding that the performing arts venue cannot be renamed without an act of Congress. U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper sided with Rep. Joyce Beatty, a Democratic member of Ohio who serves as an ex officio board member. Beatty challenged the decision...

A few minutes ago

A federal judge on Friday ruled that President Trump's name must be removed from the Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, finding that the performing arts venue cannot be renamed without an act of Congress.

U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper sided with Rep. Joyce Beatty, a Democratic member of Ohio who serves as an ex officio board member. Beatty challenged the decision to rename the iconic Washington DC institution after the president.

In his 94-page ruling, Cooper concluded that the Kennedy Center's board had overstepped its legal authority. "Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name, and only Congress can change it," the judge wrote.

The court order also halted the planned temporary closure of the Kennedy Center, which had been scheduled to begin in July to allow for renovation work. The ruling paused both the closure timeline and any associated construction plans.

Cooper directed the Trump administration to remove all physical signage bearing Trump's name from the venue and eliminate any references to a "Trump Kennedy Center" from official materials within 14 days.

The decision represents a significant obstacle to the president's renovation plans for the performing arts institution, which has been a prominent cultural venue in the nation's capital for decades. The board had voted to close the Kennedy Center temporarily and rename it to include Trump's name, but the judge found these actions violated the center's statutory framework.

Beatty brought the legal challenge against the board's decisions in her capacity as a member with authority granted through her congressional position. Her challenge questioned the legality of both the renaming and the closure plan.

The ruling came down Friday afternoon, immediately affecting the timeline for the closure and renovation work. The Kennedy Center will now remain open and continue its operations under its original name while the legal dispute proceeds.

The case hinges on the scope of the board's authority under the Kennedy Center's governing statutes. Cooper's extensive written order addressed whether the board possessed the power to unilaterally rename an institution that Congress itself had established and named.

The judge's decision aligns with principles of congressional oversight and the separation of powers. By requiring congressional approval for any name change, the ruling ensures that major decisions affecting the federally chartered institution remain subject to legislative review.

The Trump administration has the option to pursue congressional approval if the president wishes to proceed with renaming the Kennedy Center. However, any such effort would require action by lawmakers to change the existing statutory framework that governs the venue.

The Kennedy Center remains a focal point of cultural activity in Washington, hosting performances and events throughout the year. The court's decision preserves the institution's current operations while legal questions about governance authority continue to be resolved.
