A few minutes ago

The Trump administration announced it will temporarily pause and reconsider its $1.8 billion "Anti-Weaponization Fund" following a court ruling and pushback from Republican congressional leaders. The Justice Department said it would comply with a court decision that temporarily halted the fund, which was created through an out-of-court settlement in Trump v the Internal Revenue Service.

The fund has drawn significant criticism from within the Republican Party, with more than a dozen Republican senators reportedly urging the administration to change course. The administration signaled to GOP congressional leaders that it would back away from the program in its current form. The Justice Department characterized its decision as complying with judicial oversight rather than abandoning the initiative entirely, while stating it "disagrees strongly" with the court's ruling.

The Anti-Weaponization Fund was designed to provide payouts to individuals who claimed they were subjected to politically motivated investigations or prosecutions. The administration had positioned the fund as a remedy for what it described as past abuses of federal law enforcement powers. The settlement agreement gave the administration discretion in distributing the funds.

However, the fund quickly became a source of tension within the party and generated broader public opposition. Critics argue the arrangement smacks of cronyism and corruption, contending it amounts to a scheme to reward political allies while potentially benefiting the Trump family indirectly. Polling by YouGov found a majority of both Democrats and Republicans opposed the fund.

The backlash has been described as unrelenting since details of the settlement emerged two weeks before the pause announcement. The controversy has intensified questions about the administration's priorities and spending decisions. Republican lawmakers have pressed for fiscal restraint, creating pressure on the White House to justify major expenditures.

The temporary pause comes as the administration faces broader scrutiny over federal spending priorities and governance practices. The Justice Department has not specified how long the pause will last or what changes might be made to the fund's structure during the reconsideration process.

The administration's retreat on the fund represents a setback for one of its signature initiatives. The outcome of the reconsideration will determine whether the fund moves forward in a modified form or is abandoned entirely. The court ruling that prompted the pause provides a legal mechanism for opponents to challenge the fund's implementation, adding uncertainty to its future.

The rare pushback from within Trump's own party underscores the political vulnerability of the fund despite the administration's framing it as addressing legitimate grievances about past law enforcement actions. How the administration responds to the combined pressure from courts and Republican colleagues will likely signal the trajectory of other controversial initiatives.

6:06 PM ET

A meteor streaked across the northeastern United States over the weekend, triggering reports of loud booms and bright flashes from Delaware to Montreal. NASA confirmed the event occurred on Saturday at 2:06pm as the meteor entered the atmosphere near the New Hampshire-Massachusetts border, north of Boston.

The American Meteor Society reported that the meteor measured about 3 feet wide as it entered Earth's atmosphere. According to Robert Lunsford, the society's program monitor, the organization received dozens of reports from people who either heard a double boom, felt the ground shake, or witnessed the fireball in the daytime sky. He described the meteor as looking like a shooting star but noticeably larger than a typical fireball.

"It was definitely bigger than a normal fireball, about a yard wide," Lunsford said.

NASA estimates the meteor was traveling at approximately 75,000 miles per hour and likely fragmented about 40 miles above the ground. When meteors travel faster than the speed of sound, they create pressure waves that produce loud sonic booms audible from the ground. The agency calculated that the energy released when the meteor broke apart was equivalent to about 300 tons of TNT.

The double boom reportedly shook buildings across Massachusetts and Rhode Island. Several people in multiple states reported on social media that they felt their buildings vibrate. A dashboard camera in New York captured footage of the meteor streaking across the sky.

The shaking was significant enough that several people filed reports with the US Geological Survey's National Earthquake Information Center. The agency received enough "Did you feel it?" reports to open an event page on its website. However, spokesman Steve Sobie confirmed that no event registered on the agency's seismographs, ruling out an earthquake as the cause of the vibrations.

Whether the meteor struck the ground remains uncertain. Lunsford noted that most meteors burn up completely before reaching Earth's surface. He explained that determining if this particular meteor hit land would require more information about its trajectory and speed. "If it didn't burn up, then it would have landed in the ocean," he said.

NASA confirmed that the meteor was natural material rather than a satellite or space debris. The Atlantic Ocean location of the likely impact meant the explosion occurred over water, away from populated areas. The widespread visibility of the event across New England and into Canada indicates the meteor was at a significant altitude when it fragmented, allowing people across a broad geographic area to witness or hear the event.
