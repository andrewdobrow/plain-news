The United States conducted new military strikes on Iranian targets on Wednesday, marking the second such operation in three days. A U.S. official confirmed the attacks targeted four one-way attack drones and a drone ground-control station in southern Iran, describing them as defensive measures.

The strikes occur as a fragile cease-fire between the two countries persists into its third month, with ongoing negotiations to formally end the conflict. Both nations remain engaged in diplomatic discussions even as military operations continue, reflecting the delicate balance between armed action and peace efforts.

The latest attacks raise questions about the durability of the current arrangement. U.S. officials have maintained that their forces are responding to immediate threats while talks proceed. The dual approach of conducting strikes alongside negotiations demonstrates the precarious nature of the existing truce.

In separate comments, President Trump addressed the diplomatic situation, stating he feels no political pressure to reach an agreement with Iran. He suggested he could afford to wait out Iranian leadership rather than accept unfavorable terms, dismissing concerns about political consequences related to upcoming elections. Trump also made unverified claims about Iranian government actions, stating that 32,000 people were killed during recent protests, though independent monitoring groups have documented significantly lower figures.

According to Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi, the two sides have reached an understanding on main guiding principles during indirect talks held in Geneva this week. Araghchi indicated that Iran could have a draft counterproposal ready within two or three days for senior officials to review, with additional U.S.-Iran negotiations possible within about a week. He emphasized that there is no military solution to Iran's nuclear program, noting that previous attacks on facilities and scientists had not succeeded in halting the program.

Araghchi also addressed uranium enrichment discussions, stating that current negotiations focus on ensuring Iran's nuclear program remains peaceful rather than seeking zero enrichment. A White House official responded by reiterating that the president has been clear Iran cannot have nuclear weapons or the capacity to build them, and cannot enrich uranium.

The intensification of U.S. strikes occurs amid escalating regional tensions. Fighting between Israel and Hezbollah has intensified in southern Lebanon, with few Lebanese citizens expressing confidence that any U.S.-Iran agreement would bring peace to their territory.

Iran recently began restoring internet access to its population after 88 days of near-total shutdown. The government had cut connectivity at the conflict's onset, blocking news and communication platforms. Access remains limited and uneven across the country.

A federal court has ordered the customs chief to appear at a hearing to address the Trump administration's compliance with refunding tariffs that were illegally imposed. The demand signals new judicial concern about efforts to repay the full $166 billion owed to businesses and importers.

The court's action represents an escalation in oversight of the refund process. The administration has been required to return tariffs collected under trade policies that were later ruled illegal. The $166 billion figure represents the total amount collected from importers before courts invalidated the tariff programs.

The hearing will require the customs chief to explain the status of refunds and detail steps being taken to ensure full compliance with court orders. The directive suggests judges are not satisfied with the pace or completeness of the repayment effort.

The refund obligation stems from tariff policies implemented during the Trump administration that courts subsequently found violated trade law. Importers paid the tariffs under protest, and successful legal challenges established their right to reimbursement.

The $166 billion liability represents one of the largest financial obligations resulting from overturned trade policies in recent history. The funds were collected from businesses across multiple industries that import goods into the United States.

The court's order to compel testimony from the customs chief indicates potential concerns about delays or incomplete refunds. Such hearings are typically ordered when courts believe government agencies may not be fully complying with judicial mandates.

The customs chief will need to provide detailed accounting of how much has been refunded, how much remains outstanding, and what timeline exists for completing the payments. The hearing will also likely address any administrative obstacles preventing full compliance.

The appearance requirement underscores judicial authority to enforce its orders through direct testimony from senior officials. Courts can hold agencies in contempt if they fail to comply with refund mandates or cannot adequately explain delays in implementation.

8:45 PM ET

Federal authorities charged a Google engineer with insider trading after he made $1.2 million on the prediction market platform Polymarket. The engineer allegedly used confidential company information to place profitable bets.

According to the criminal complaint, the engineer risked over $2.7 million on wagers related to Google's 2025 Year in Search campaign. The betting activity centered on internal company information about the annual report, which Google traditionally releases to summarize the year's most popular search trends.

The case represents one of the first major insider trading prosecutions involving a cryptocurrency-based prediction market. Polymarket allows users to bet on the outcomes of real-world events using cryptocurrency. The platform has grown rapidly in recent years, attracting both retail traders and sophisticated investors.

Prosecutors allege the engineer had advance knowledge of the Year in Search results through his position at Google. He allegedly placed bets on Polymarket that would profit if certain search terms or trends appeared in the official report. The complaint suggests he made these trades before the information became public.

The $1.2 million profit came from successful predictions tied to the internal campaign data. The engineer's total wagers exceeded $2.7 million, indicating he placed multiple bets across different potential outcomes. Some of those bets apparently did not pay off, but his overall profit still reached seven figures.

Insider trading laws prohibit using confidential corporate information for personal financial gain. While these cases typically involve traditional stock trading, prosecutors have increasingly applied the laws to newer financial instruments including cryptocurrency markets and prediction platforms.

Google has not publicly commented on the charges. The company maintains internal policies restricting employees from using confidential information for personal benefit. Violations can result in termination and, as this case demonstrates, criminal prosecution.

The engineer faces federal charges that could result in significant prison time if convicted. Insider trading convictions typically carry sentences of several years, along with financial penalties and disgorgement of illegal profits. The case remains in early stages as prosecutors build their evidence.

A few minutes ago

The Trump administration has launched a criminal investigation into E. Jean Carroll, the writer who accused President Trump of sexual assault, according to reports from CNN and the New York Times on Wednesday.

Federal prosecutors are examining whether Carroll, 82, committed perjury during a 2022 deposition in her civil lawsuits against Trump. The specific focus concerns statements Carroll made under oath about whether she received outside financial support for her legal battles.

During the 2022 deposition, Carroll stated she did not accept outside financial backing. However, nearly six months later, before the trial began, her attorneys informed the judge and Trump's legal team that a nonprofit funded by Reid Hoffman, the LinkedIn co-founder, had paid some of her legal fees and expenses.

Trump's lawyers argued that Carroll deliberately concealed Hoffman's financial support, claiming this deception undermined her credibility. Carroll's legal representatives responded that she had never met or spoken with anyone from the nonprofit organization. The judge allowed Trump's attorney, Alina Habba, to question Carroll again in a second deposition.

In 2024, a three-judge federal appeals court panel in New York dismissed Trump's claim that Carroll had lied during her deposition.

Carroll is a longtime advice columnist who claimed in her 2019 book "What Do We Need Men For?" that Trump forced himself upon her in a New York department store dressing room decades ago. Trump has denied her allegations, stating she was "totally lying" and that she was "not my type."

Carroll filed a federal civil lawsuit against Trump for sexual abuse and defamation. A jury found Trump liable on both counts and awarded Carroll five million dollars in damages. A year later, another jury ordered Trump to pay eighty-three million dollars in a separate defamation case. Trump has appealed both judgments.

According to reports, Todd Blanche, the acting attorney general, has recused himself from the investigation due to his previous role representing Trump in Carroll's civil cases.

The investigation represents the latest in a series of criminal probes launched by Trump's Justice Department into political opponents. Federal prosecutors have investigated former FBI Director James Comey, New York Attorney General Letitia James, and Democratic members of Congress including Adam Schiff and Ilhan Omar. None of these investigations have resulted in convictions.

The Justice Department and Carroll's legal representatives did not immediately respond to requests for comment.
