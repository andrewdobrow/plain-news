The lawsuit alleges that 3M concealed information about the harmful effects of PFA...

4:52 AM ET

The Australian government filed a $1.4 billion lawsuit against 3M, marking the largest legal action the government has ever brought against a private company. The case centers on contamination from PFAS chemicals, commonly known as forever chemicals, at military defense sites across the country.

The lawsuit alleges that 3M concealed information about the harmful effects of PFAS used in firefighting foam at these locations. PFAS, or per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, are synthetic chemicals that persist in the environment and human body for extended periods, earning them the forever chemicals designation.

The contamination originated from firefighting foam containing these chemicals, which was deployed at Australian defense facilities. The government claims 3M knew about the dangers these substances posed but failed to disclose critical safety information.

The legal action represents an escalation in global efforts to hold manufacturers accountable for environmental damage linked to PFAS. These chemicals have been detected in water supplies and soil around military bases and airports worldwide, where firefighting foam was regularly used for training and emergency response.

3M has faced similar litigation in other countries over PFAS contamination. The chemicals have been linked to various health concerns and environmental damage, though they were widely used for decades in firefighting applications due to their effectiveness in suppressing fuel-based fires.

The Australian case seeks compensation for cleanup costs and environmental remediation at affected defense sites. The $1.4 billion figure, converted from 2 billion Australian dollars, reflects the scale of contamination and projected costs for addressing the pollution.

The lawsuit comes as governments worldwide grapple with legacy contamination from PFAS chemicals. Many countries have since restricted or banned the use of PFAS in firefighting foam, but existing contamination continues to pose environmental and public health challenges at sites where the substances were previously deployed.

Polymarket allows users to bet real money on the outcomes of future events, from political elections to corporate announcements. The platform has grown in popularity as a tool for gauging public sentiment on various topics, but it also presents opportunities for abuse when participants have access to privileged information.

The case marks a notable intersection of technology industry employment and prediction market gambling. Insider trading laws prohibit trading securities or other financial instruments based on material nonpublic information, and prosecutors appear to be applying those principles to prediction market platforms.

The complaint did not specify exactly what information the engineer accessed or how the bets were structured to generate the profit. Google has not commented on the case or whether the employee remains with the company.

Federal prosecutors have increasingly scrutinized activities on prediction market platforms, particularly as these services have gained mainstream attention. Polymarket has faced regulatory questions in the past about whether its operations comply with commodity trading laws.

The engineer now faces criminal charges that could result in significant fines and prison time if convicted. Insider trading cases typically require prosecutors to prove that the defendant knowingly used confidential information for personal gain and that the information was material to the trading decisions.

The investigation remains ongoing. Additional charges or defendants have not been announced.
