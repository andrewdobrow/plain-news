A new study has found that the National Guard's presence in Washington, D.C. has had no measurable impact on reducing violent crime in the city. The Guard has been deployed since August as part of a federal task force aimed at addressing crime, and their numbers are scheduled to double in the coming weeks.

The deployment represents a significant federal intervention in local public safety operations, with Guard members working alongside the Metropolitan Police Department. As of recently, nearly 2,400 National Guard troops are stationed in Washington, with the operation costing approximately one million dollars daily.

The Guard's presence in the capital was extended through at least February by Pete Hegseth, the Defense Secretary, after the initial order was set to expire in November. This extension comes as the Trump administration has expanded its use of federal law enforcement to address crime in major cities.

The deployment has already drawn legal challenges. The District of Columbia's attorney general, Brian Schwalb, sued the Trump administration over the Guard's presence, characterizing it as an "involuntary military occupation" and an illegal use of the military for domestic law enforcement purposes. A federal judge previously ruled that Trump's deployment of National Guard troops to Los Angeles following immigration-related protests in June had been illegal, though that ruling does not directly apply to Washington, where the president has greater control over Guard operations.

The study's findings come as the administration has escalated its use of federal forces in American cities. Beyond Washington, Guard troops have been sent to Chicago, and officials have threatened deployments to Democratic-led cities including San Francisco, Portland and New York.

The Pentagon has moved to formalize these operations. In October, a top military official ordered National Guard units in all 50 states, the District of Columbia and U.S. territories to establish "quick reaction forces" trained in riot control tactics, including the use of batons, body shields, Tasers and pepper spray. According to an internal Pentagon directive, most states are required to train 500 National Guard members in these tactics, amounting to roughly 23,500 troops nationwide. States are required to report monthly on their progress.

Despite the study's conclusions about ineffectiveness, federal officials have not commented on the findings or indicated whether they will reconsider the strategy. The planned expansion of Guard personnel is proceeding as scheduled.

The deployment raises questions about resource allocation and the appropriate role of military forces in addressing urban crime. As Washington continues to grapple with public safety concerns, residents and policymakers are watching to see whether the expanded Guard presence will produce different results or whether alternative approaches to crime reduction will need to be considered.

Yesterday, 10:46 PM ET

The United States Department of Agriculture has confirmed the presence of New World screwworm in South Texas, marking a significant breakthrough in the parasite's containment. The detection comes after decades of successful efforts to keep the flesh-eating insect south of the US-Mexico border.

The New World screwworm is a parasitic fly whose larvae burrow into wounds on warm-blooded animals, including humans and livestock. Once inside, the larvae feed on living tissue using sharp mouths to create severe infections that can be fatal if untreated. Female flies lay eggs in any open wound, and hundreds of larvae can emerge from a single infestation.

For much of the 20th century, screwworm infestations devastated the American livestock industry. In the 1950s, scientists developed an elegant solution using radiation to sterilize fly eggs. Because female flies typically mate only once, encounters with sterile males meant eggs would not fertilize, and populations began declining. This sterile-fly program proved so successful that it gradually pushed the screwworm southward, eventually establishing a containment zone at the Darién Gap between Panama and South America.

However, the parasite has been creeping northward again in recent years, probably assisted by cross-border livestock trade. About 89,000 cases have been detected in animals across Central America over the past three years, according to FDA chief Marty Makary. The screwworm now sits only a few hundred miles south of the United States border.

Recent budget cuts to scientific research and international aid programs have complicated containment efforts. Some global screwworm-fighting initiatives were reduced during the Trump administration, shaking international cooperation that had been crucial to the program's decades of success. Yet in response to rising case numbers, the USDA announced in June that it would reopen a Texas breeding facility for sterile flies, followed by an ambitious $850 million plan in August to combat the screwworm.

A Maryland resident diagnosed with screwworm in August after traveling to El Salvador provided a warning signal. Medical experts emphasized that human transmission risk remains very low, as the infection was caught before the insect could mature. However, the case highlighted how closely the parasite has approached US territory and underscored the importance of international cooperation on research and prevention.

The USDA and Texas animal health officials are expanding monitoring programs to trap flies and continuing inspections of livestock headed for slaughter. Officials worry that wildlife could also carry the parasite across the border, citing a 2016 infestation among Florida Keys deer that required releasing sterile flies to control.

Experts stress the real threat involves livestock populations, particularly in Texas with its large cattle industry. Timothy Schell, acting director of the Center for Veterinary Medicine at the FDA, warned that screwworm could cause serious economic damage to American livestock production if it becomes established.

Marjane Satrapi, the French-Iranian artist and filmmaker known for her autobiographical graphic novel and animated film Persepolis, has died at age 56. A statement from close friends and family said Satrapi died of sadness a little over a year after the death of her husband, Mattias Ripa.

Satrapi became internationally recognized for Persepolis, which chronicled her childhood in Iran during the Islamic Revolution and her adolescence in Europe. The graphic novel series was adapted into an animated feature film in 2007, which she co-directed. The film received an Oscar nomination and won the Jury Prize at the Cannes Film Festival.

Born in Iran, Satrapi left the country as a teenager and eventually settled in France, where she built her career as an artist, author, and filmmaker. Her work combined stark black-and-white illustrations with personal storytelling that addressed political upheaval, cultural identity, and displacement. Persepolis was published in multiple languages and became widely used in schools and universities.

Beyond Persepolis, Satrapi directed live-action films and continued to create graphic novels. Her work as an animator and director expanded her influence in both literature and cinema, making her one of the most prominent voices bridging the two mediums.

Satrapi's death follows the loss of her husband, whom friends and family described as the love of her life. She leaves behind a body of work that shaped how graphic novels are perceived as a serious literary and cinematic form.

A few minutes ago

The House of Representatives delivered a significant rebuke to President Trump on Wednesday by voting 215 to 208 in favor of a war powers resolution that would force him to seek congressional approval before continuing military operations against Iran or withdraw U.S. forces from the conflict.

Four Republicans joined all Democrats to support the measure. The dissident Republicans were Thomas Massie of Kentucky, Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania, Warren Davidson of Ohio, and Tom Barrett of Michigan.

The vote represents a notable moment of Republican defection on a key foreign policy issue. It demonstrates that some members of Trump's own party are willing to challenge his approach to military conflict, despite the president's significant influence over the GOP.

The resolution now moves to the Senate, where lawmakers must promptly take up the measure under the war powers law. A handful of Senate Republicans previously joined Democrats in advancing a similar resolution last month, indicating potential support in the upper chamber as well.

This marks the fourth time the House has voted on a resolution designed to limit Trump's authority to continue the Iran conflict, which has now lasted more than 90 days. House Republicans had previously cancelled an earlier scheduled vote, recognizing they lacked sufficient numbers to block the measure's passage.

The timing of the vote comes as diplomatic efforts to negotiate a settlement have stalled. Trump administration officials have repeatedly claimed that a deal is close and that Iran is eager to reach an agreement, but concrete progress remains elusive.

The war powers resolution is largely symbolic in nature, as significant procedural hurdles remain before Congress could actually compel the president to end military operations. The measure would require Senate passage and potentially a presidential veto override to become binding policy. Still, the House vote signals growing frustration among lawmakers across the aisle regarding the administration's handling of the conflict.

The four Republican defectors represent a minority within their party, but their willingness to break ranks highlights the genuine concerns some GOP members hold about executive power in military matters. Historically, questions about the balance between presidential authority and congressional oversight in military conflicts have periodically united lawmakers across party lines.

The vote reflects evolving dynamics within Congress as members grapple with the ongoing tension between supporting a president of their own party and asserting institutional congressional powers over matters of war and peace. The resolution's passage, despite Republican control of the chamber, indicates that some lawmakers prioritize constitutional checks and balances over partisan loyalty on this particular issue.
