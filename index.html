8:56 PM ET

A meteor exploded over the Atlantic Ocean off the coast of Massachusetts, creating a sonic boom heard across Southern New England. Reports of the loud noise and sightings of the meteor came from areas stretching from Delaware to Montreal.

The Boston Herald reported that authorities identified the mysterious noise as a sonic boom, possibly caused by a meteor that exploded in mid-air. CBS News confirmed the meteor detonated off the Massachusetts coast, generating the loud boom that alarmed residents throughout the region.

According to the Worcester Telegram, the explosion released energy equivalent to 300 tons of TNT. The meteor disintegrated over the ocean rather than reaching the ground, which likely prevented any damage or injuries.

Sonic booms from meteors occur when the space rock travels through the atmosphere faster than the speed of sound, creating a shock wave. When meteors explode in mid-air, the sudden release of energy amplifies the sound, making it audible over wide areas.

Residents across Southern New England reported hearing the boom and took to social media to share their experiences. The widespread reports helped authorities piece together what had occurred and rule out other potential causes for the noise.

The event took place over the Atlantic Ocean, away from populated areas. No injuries or property damage were reported following the explosion. Meteors entering Earth's atmosphere are relatively common, though most burn up at high altitudes without creating noticeable effects on the ground.

The sightings and reports spanned a large geographic area, indicating the meteor was visible from multiple states and into Canada. The explosion occurred at an altitude high enough that the sonic boom could travel across the region but low enough to be clearly heard by people on the ground.

An Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has killed 65 people, with health officials reporting 246 suspected cases so far. The outbreak is concentrated in Ituri province, a conflict-affected region that borders Uganda and South Sudan.

The strain identified in this outbreak is the Bundibugyo variant, according to DRC health officials. The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention said the DRC's national research laboratory detected the Ebola virus in 13 of 20 samples tested. This strain is particularly concerning because no licensed vaccine currently exists for it, unlike the Zaire strain that has caused most previous Ebola outbreaks in the DRC. The Bundibugyo strain has appeared only twice before, in 2007 and 2012.

Uganda has also confirmed cases linked to the outbreak. A 59-year-old man died in a Kampala hospital after traveling from the DRC, marking Uganda's first confirmed death from the current outbreak.

The World Health Organization estimates the death rate from confirmed cases at between 30 and 50 percent. Anaïs Legand, from the WHO's high threat pathogens team, described this as significant, noting that up to five out of ten infected people are likely to die. The head of the WHO has visited the region to support containment efforts.

Ituri province presents particular challenges for disease control. The region contains mining towns with constant population movement, making it difficult to track and contain the spread of infectious disease. The vast geography of the DRC and ongoing security challenges in some areas further complicate surveillance and response efforts.

Health workers in the affected areas face significant obstacles. Medical facilities lack sufficient resources to manage the increasing number of cases, and workers struggle to access remote communities. Maintaining the cold chain required for vaccines and treatments adds another layer of difficulty in this region.

The medical charity Médecins Sans Frontières described the situation as deeply alarming, highlighting the strain on local health systems. Aid organizations are working to support underequipped health workers as they respond to the outbreak.

The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention convened an urgent meeting with authorities from the DRC, Uganda, and South Sudan, along with the WHO and pharmaceutical companies. Dr Jean Kaseya, the director general of Africa CDC, emphasized the need for rapid regional coordination given the high population movement between affected areas and neighboring countries.

The DRC has experienced 16 Ebola outbreaks since the virus was first identified there in 1976. Cases in the current outbreak have been reported mainly in the Mongwalu and Rwampara health zones, with suspected cases also appearing in the neighboring city of Bunia.

The U.S. military attacked and seized an Iranian-flagged container ship that tried to pass through an American blockade near the Strait of Hormuz, marking the first such interception since the blockade began last week.

U.S. Central Command said marines from the USS Tripoli assault ship rappelled onto the vessel, called the Touska, by helicopter on Sunday. President Trump announced the action on social media, saying a U.S. Navy guided missile destroyer had warned the Touska to stop in the Gulf of Oman, but the ship refused to comply. "Our Navy ship stopped them right in their tracks by blowing a hole in the engine room," Trump said. He noted that U.S. marines now had custody of the vessel.

Video released by the U.S. Defense Department showed the USS Spruance firing on the Touska. Audio from the interception captured a U.S. sailor telling the crew to vacate the engine room, warning that disabling fire would follow if they did not comply. The ship had previously departed Port Klang in Malaysia before attempting to breach the blockade near Iran's border with Pakistan.

Trump said the ship was under U.S. Treasury sanctions due to prior illegal activity and appeared on the Treasury Department's list of sanctioned vessels.

Iran's joint military command rejected the seizure, calling it an act of piracy that violated a ceasefire in place since April 8. A spokesperson for Iran's military said the armed forces would "soon respond and retaliate against this armed piracy." Iranian state media reported that Tehran had rejected new peace talks, citing the ongoing blockade, threatening rhetoric, and what officials described as excessive U.S. demands and shifting positions.

The incident throws uncertainty over planned negotiations. Trump had announced that U.S. negotiators would travel to Pakistan on Monday for another round of talks with Iran, but Iran has not confirmed its attendance. The ceasefire is scheduled to expire Wednesday.

The confrontation threatens to deepen the global energy crisis and push the two countries toward renewed conflict. The war has killed at least 3,000 people in Iran, nearly 2,300 in Lebanon, 23 civilians and 15 soldiers in Israel, and more than a dozen in Gulf Arab states. Thirteen U.S. service members have also been killed.

The blockade of Iranian ports has been central to U.S. military operations during the conflict. The seizure of the Touska demonstrates the continued military enforcement of the blockade even as diplomatic efforts continue, reflecting the dual approach of maintaining military pressure while pursuing negotiations.

Shai Gilgeous-Alexander, the reigning NBA MVP, was brilliant for Oklahoma City with 35 points and nine assists. The Spurs sealed the series with a breakaway dunk in the final moments, ending any comeback hopes for the defending champions. Coach Mark Daigneault acknowledged the defeat while looking forward for his team. "You have to grow from every experience, including the tough ones," Daigneault said. Yesterday, 9:04 PM ET

President Trump has asked for amendments to a tentative agreement that his envoys negotiated with Iranian counterparts, according to senior administration officials and sources briefed on the matter.

During a Friday Situation Room meeting, Trump indicated he wants to finalize the deal but is seeking to strengthen certain provisions he considers important, particularly those addressing Iran's nuclear material. The requested changes have triggered another round of negotiations between the parties that officials say could extend several days.

The tentative agreement, which both sides reached earlier this week, would extend the current ceasefire and reopen the Strait of Hormuz if both the United States and Iran approve it. Trump stated he will only sign a deal "where we get everything we want," according to reports from his conversations with media outlets.

Trump made a public announcement on Friday that an agreement has been "largely negotiated" after calls with leaders from Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Egypt, Jordan, Turkey and Israel. He indicated that "final aspects and details" of the deal were still being discussed and would be announced shortly.

However, Iran's state-aligned news agency contested Trump's characterization, stating that his assertion about the deal being nearly final was "inconsistent with reality." The agency also reported that Iran insists the Strait of Hormuz would remain under Iranian control, including determining passage routes, timing and methods.

According to details reported by international media outlets, the proposed agreement would include opening the strait with no tolls during a 60-day ceasefire extension. Iran would be permitted to freely sell oil while negotiations occur on its nuclear program. The United States would lift its blockade on Iranian ports in return.

Additional reported provisions include an official declaration ending the war, negotiations on Iran's nuclear program within 30 to 60 days, and potentially releasing approximately 25 billion dollars in frozen Iranian assets.

Trump convened meetings with his negotiating team, including special envoy Steve Witkoff, adviser Jared Kushner, and Vice President JD Vance to discuss the latest proposals.

The potential deal has generated strong reactions from Republican hawks who have long advocated for military action against Iran. Former Secretary of State Mike Pompeo criticized the rumored terms, claiming they resembled the approach taken by the Obama administration in the 2015 nuclear agreement that Trump withdrew from in 2018.

However, some officials involved in previous negotiations suggested the deal could still represent progress toward peace. The timing for a final agreement remains uncertain, though Trump has expressed his desire to complete negotiations quickly.
