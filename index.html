A few minutes ago

American and Iranian negotiators have reached a tentative agreement that could extend a ceasefire and restart discussions about Iran's nuclear program, according to multiple sources. President Donald Trump must approve the deal before it takes effect.

The framework of the agreement has been negotiated, though details remain limited and some aspects could still change before final approval. U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the deal could achieve "a completely open" Strait of Hormuz "without tolls" if successful.

Under the proposed agreement, a 60-day ceasefire extension would allow time for negotiations on Iran's nuclear program. During this period, the Strait of Hormuz would reopen for shipping, enabling Iran to freely sell oil without restrictions. Iran would agree to clear mines it has deployed in the strait and not impose tolls on passing ships.

In exchange, the United States would lift its blockade on Iranian ports that has been in place since April 13. The deal would also unfreeze some Iranian assets held in banks outside Iran. Hostilities would halt on all fronts, including between Israel and Lebanon.

However, a senior Iranian source told Reuters that Tehran has not agreed to surrender its highly enriched uranium stockpile. The source stated that Iran's nuclear issue was not part of the preliminary agreement with the U.S., contradicting earlier reports from the New York Times that Iran had expressed willingness to give up its stockpile.

The deal does not appear to address Iran's ballistic missile program or efforts to curb Iran's support for regional allies such as Hezbollah in Lebanon or the Houthis in Yemen. Iranian media has also reported that the Strait of Hormuz would remain under Iranian control, potentially conflicting with U.S. descriptions of the arrangement.

Neither Tehran nor the Trump administration had provided official public comments on the tentative agreement as of the latest reports. U.S. officials told the BBC that the framework had been agreed pending approval from Trump and Iran's leadership.

The timing of the announcement indicates negotiators have made progress following extended discussions. The tentative nature of the agreement means specifics could still change before presentation to Trump for final approval.

If approved, the deal would represent a significant shift in U.S. relations with Iran and could resolve a major regional conflict. The reopening of the Strait of Hormuz would restore a critical route for international commerce and global energy supplies. The nuclear talks component indicates both sides are willing to address longer-term concerns about Iran's nuclear capabilities while implementing the immediate ceasefire extension.

Yesterday, 10:21 PM ET

A New Glenn rocket operated by Blue Origin exploded during a static fire test at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida on Thursday evening, destroying the launchpad and threatening NASA's ambitious plans to build a lunar base. The explosion occurred at 9pm ET seconds after the scheduled hotfire test began, creating a massive fireball visible from over 100 miles away.

The blast sent shockwaves across Florida's Space Coast, with residents reporting shaking homes in Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach. An orange glow was visible in Fort Pierce, 185 kilometers to the south, and reports came from South Carolina of observers seeing the sky light up hundreds of miles away. Blue Origin founder Jeff Bezos confirmed that all company employees were safe and accounted for, though he acknowledged it was "a very rough day." The company described the incident as "an anomaly" without providing immediate details about the cause.

The explosion arrives at a critical moment for Blue Origin's competitive position in the commercial spaceflight industry. Just two days before the test, NASA had awarded Blue Origin a contract to conduct the first of three planned uncrewed lunar missions this year to support construction of a $20 billion moon base. The company is also competing with SpaceX to provide a lunar lander for the Artemis IV mission scheduled for 2028, which would mark the first crewed lunar landing since 1972.

The setback further complicates NASA's timeline for returning humans to the moon. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman stated that the agency would conduct a full evaluation of the Artemis program's schedule following the explosion. He emphasized that developing heavy-lift launch capability is extraordinarily difficult and pledged to work with partners on investigating the anomaly and assessing impacts to lunar missions.

This explosion represents the latest in a series of difficulties for Blue Origin's New Glenn program. Last month, a payload from the rocket's third flight ended up in the wrong orbit, leading the Federal Aviation Administration to temporarily ground the vehicle. The static fire test on Thursday was the first conducted since the FAA cleared the rocket to resume operations the previous week.

The incident will likely delay New Glenn's path to operational status and may require NASA to adjust its mission planning and payload deployment strategies. The space agency has been working to diversify its launch capabilities beyond SpaceX to ensure competition and redundancy in the commercial spaceflight market.

Flames were still burning at the launchpad more than two hours after the explosion. Emergency officials reported no threats from hazardous fumes or other dangers. Blue Origin indicated the company would investigate the root cause and rebuild whatever equipment was damaged before resuming flight operations.
