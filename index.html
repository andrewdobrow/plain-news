12:30 PM ET

Applications for unemployment benefits climbed to their highest point since February, according to data released by the Labor Department. The increase signals a potential shift in the labor market after months of relative stability.

Weekly jobless claims rose to 225,000, marking a four-month high. The figure represents the number of Americans filing for unemployment assistance for the first time and serves as a proxy for layoffs across the economy. The data covers the week that included the Memorial Day holiday, which can affect reporting patterns.

The rise in claims comes as the Federal Reserve continues to monitor economic indicators for signs of weakness or strength in the labor market. Worker productivity data was also revised downward in separate government figures, adding to concerns about economic momentum. The labor market has remained a key focus for policymakers as they balance inflation concerns against economic growth.

The spike in gold prices accompanied the jobless claims report, with spot gold reclaiming the $4,500 per ounce level. Precious metals often attract investor interest during periods of economic uncertainty as traders seek safe-haven assets. The movement suggests market participants are reacting to signs of labor market softening.

The current claims level represents the highest since February when geopolitical tensions escalated in the Middle East. This baseline for comparison underscores how recent labor market conditions have evolved over the past four months. The data will likely influence upcoming Federal Reserve decisions on monetary policy as officials assess whether the labor market is cooling enough to ease inflationary pressures without tipping into recession.

Economic analysts will be watching closely to determine whether this increase represents a temporary spike tied to seasonal reporting variations or a genuine shift in employer hiring decisions. The Memorial Day holiday timing adds some complexity to interpreting the numbers, as holiday weeks can create distortions in weekly filings.

The jobless claims data provides important context for understanding broader labor market health. While 225,000 claims remain relatively moderate by historical standards, the upward movement from recent weeks has caught the attention of investors and policymakers alike. The combination of rising jobless claims and downward productivity revisions suggests the economy may be facing some headwinds that warrant closer monitoring.

The Labor Department will continue releasing weekly claims data, which economists view as among the most timely indicators of labor market conditions. These figures often move before broader employment statistics become available, making them valuable for spotting emerging trends. As the Federal Reserve weighs its next steps on interest rates, this pattern of rising claims will likely feature prominently in policy discussions.

12:21 PM ET

NASA officially concluded the MAVEN mission after the spacecraft spent more than a decade orbiting Mars. The Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution orbiter, which launched in 2013 and arrived at Mars in 2014, stopped communicating with Earth earlier this year. NASA held a media call to formally announce the end of the mission.

Over 11 years, MAVEN studied the Martian atmosphere and investigated how the planet lost much of its air and water over billions of years. The spacecraft was equipped with instruments designed to measure atmospheric composition, track interactions between solar wind and the planet's upper atmosphere, and observe how gases escaped into space. This research helped scientists understand the dramatic transformation of Mars from a potentially habitable world with liquid water on its surface to the cold, dry planet visible today.

The mission team experienced the conclusion with deep emotion. One team member described the loss in personal terms, saying the group felt as though they had lost a loved one when the mission ended. MAVEN significantly exceeded its original planned lifespan, continuing to transmit valuable data for years after its primary mission phase concluded.

The spacecraft fell silent months before NASA announced the official end. Ground controllers lost contact with MAVEN and were unable to restore communication despite repeated attempts. Scientists believe the spacecraft either exhausted its fuel or experienced a critical system failure that prevented it from properly orienting its antenna toward Earth.

MAVEN's contributions fundamentally transformed how scientists understand planetary atmospheres and climate processes on other worlds. The mission provided essential data about how solar radiation and solar wind gradually strip away atmospheric particles, a mechanism that thinned Mars's atmosphere over geological timescales. This understanding has broader implications for studying atmospheric loss on other planets and evaluating the long-term habitability of worlds throughout our solar system and beyond.

The loss of MAVEN reflects the reality of space exploration, where spacecraft eventually reach the end of their operational lives despite the dedication of mission teams. However, the data MAVEN collected during its 11 years in orbit will continue to inform Mars research for decades to come. Scientists will analyze the observations to refine models of planetary atmospheres and improve understanding of how worlds lose their protective air.

MAVEN's legacy demonstrates how sustained observation from orbit can reveal fundamental processes shaping planetary evolution. The spacecraft's instruments captured detailed measurements that ground-based observations and brief orbital passes could never provide. As NASA continues exploring Mars with other missions, MAVEN's findings will serve as a foundation for future discoveries about the red planet's past and its potential for past habitability.

2:49 PM ET

Marjane Satrapi, the French-Iranian artist and graphic novelist whose memoir Persepolis transformed international understanding of Iran, has died at 56. Her family said she "died of sadness" following the death of her husband, Swedish producer Mattias Ripa, who passed away on April 8 of the previous year.

Satrapi was born in 1969 in Rasht, Iran, and grew up in Tehran with her father, an engineer, and her mother, a dress designer. As a teenager, her parents sent her to Europe to continue her education and protect her from restrictions under the Islamic Republic. She eventually settled in France, arriving in 1994 and becoming a French citizen in 2006.

In 2000, while still an arts student in Strasbourg with limited professional comics experience, Satrapi published Persepolis. She initially had modest hopes for the work, telling El País in 2020: "With Persepolis, I didn't even think I'd find a publisher. I thought I'd make 50 photocopies for my friends to read." Instead, the graphic memoir became an international publishing phenomenon, selling millions of copies and establishing her as one of the most widely read Iranian authors worldwide.

The autobiographical work depicted her childhood during Iran's Islamic Revolution, following a rebellious young girl as she navigated the country's violence and ideological control before being sent alone to Europe at age 14. Satrapi explained to the Guardian in 2024 that she aimed to help Western readers recognize "Oh, they're actually human beings like us" when thinking about Iranian people.

President Emmanuel Macron paid tribute, saying Satrapi was "a great artist who turned her Iranian childhood into a universal tale," noting her "childlike perspective, her irony, her tenderness, her inner demons."

Satrapi co-directed the 2007 animated film adaptation of Persepolis, becoming the first woman nominated for an Academy Award for best animated feature. She later directed five feature films, including Radioactive in 2019, starring Rosamund Pike as scientist Marie Curie.

Regarding her chosen medium, Satrapi said in 2012: "Drawing – it's the first language of human beings, before writing, before even talking, before words." She explained that her comics aimed to reassure young Iranians they were heard and supported globally, stating: "If they kill you and the whole world doesn't care, how is that? This is the whole thing I'm asking: just recognise this."

After stepping away from comics for years, Satrapi returned to the medium in 2024 with Woman, Life, Freedom, a collaborative graphic work examining the protest movement following the death of Kurdish-Iranian woman Mahsa Amini in 2022. She told the Guardian: "The only thing I can do is cultural work. This book is a message to the Iranian people to say, listen, you are not alone."

A majority of Americans believe the democratic system in the United States is not functioning properly, according to a new Quinnipiac University poll. The survey found that 57 percent of respondents said the system of democracy is not working, while 37 percent said they believe it is working. Seven percent did not express an opinion.

The poll reflects broader public sentiment about the health of American democratic institutions during a period marked by political tension across multiple branches of government. The survey measures confidence in the overall functioning of democracy rather than approval of any specific politician or party.

Quinnipiac University regularly conducts national polls examining political and social issues. The organization surveys registered voters across the country through telephone interviews to gauge public opinion on key topics affecting the nation.

The finding that a majority of Americans view democracy as not working represents a significant indicator of public dissatisfaction with how government institutions are operating. Research over recent years has shown that confidence in various democratic institutions, including Congress, the presidency, and the courts, fluctuates considerably based on the political climate and current events.

Public trust in government has varied widely depending on what issues dominate the national conversation at any given time. While specific confidence levels rise and fall, concerns about whether democratic institutions are functioning effectively have appeared across different segments of the population at various points.

The available information from this poll does not include breakdowns of responses by political party affiliation or demographic categories. However, previous polling on similar questions about the state of democracy has typically shown differences in how Republicans, Democrats, and independents assess the current state of democratic function. Despite these differences in perspective, worries about democratic functioning have crossed party lines at different moments in recent history.

The results come as Americans grapple with multiple ongoing controversies involving the executive branch, Congress, and the judiciary. These institutional tensions have contributed to broader questions about whether the democratic system is operating as intended.

The survey adds to a growing body of polling data examining public confidence in government and democratic institutions. As Americans continue to express concerns about how well democracy is working, these measures provide insight into the public's assessment of the nation's political system and its ability to address the challenges facing the country.

NASA officially concluded the MAVEN mission after the spacecraft spent more than a decade orbiting Mars. The Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution orbiter, which launched in 2013 and arrived at Mars in 2014, stopped communicating with Earth earlier this year. NASA held a media call to formally announce the end of the mission.

Over 11 years, MAVEN studied the Martian atmosphere and investigated how the planet lost much of its air and water over billions of years. The spacecraft was equipped with instruments designed to measure atmospheric composition, track interactions between solar wind and the planet's upper atmosphere, and observe how gases escaped into space. This research helped scientists understand the dramatic transformation of Mars from a potentially habitable world with liquid water on its surface to the cold, dry planet visible today.

The mission team experienced the conclusion with deep emotion. One team member described the loss in personal terms, saying the group felt as though they had lost a loved one when the mission ended. MAVEN significantly exceeded its original planned lifespan, continuing to transmit valuable data for years after its primary mission phase concluded.

The spacecraft fell silent months before NASA announced the official end. Ground controllers lost contact with MAVEN and were unable to restore communication despite repeated attempts. Scientists believe the spacecraft either exhausted its fuel or experienced a critical system failure that prevented it from properly orienting its antenna toward Earth.

MAVEN's contributions fundamentally transformed how scientists understand planetary atmospheres and climate processes on other worlds. The mission provided essential data about how solar radiation and solar wind gradually strip away atmospheric particles, a mechanism that thinned Mars's atmosphere over geological timescales. This understanding has broader implications for studying atmospheric loss on other planets and evaluating the long-term habitability of worlds throughout our solar system and beyond.

The loss of MAVEN reflects the reality of space exploration, where spacecraft eventually reach the end of their operational lives despite the dedication of mission teams. However, the data MAVEN collected during its 11 years in orbit will continue to inform Mars research for decades to come. Scientists will analyze the observations to refine models of planetary atmospheres and improve understanding of how worlds lose their protective air.

MAVEN's legacy demonstrates how sustained observation from orbit can reveal fundamental processes shaping planetary evolution. The spacecraft's instruments captured detailed measurements that ground-based observations and brief orbital passes could never provide. As NASA continues exploring Mars with other missions, MAVEN's findings will serve as a foundation for future discoveries about the red planet's past and its potential for past habitability.
