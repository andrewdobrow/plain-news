7:38 PM ET

Nine employees have been confirmed dead following a chemical tank rupture at the Nippon Dynawave Packaging facility in Longview, Washington. Recovery crews have retrieved the remains of six victims, with two workers still unaccounted for.

The disaster occurred on Tuesday morning when an industrial tank ruptured and released white liquor, a highly caustic chemical solution used in paper manufacturing. The tank held approximately 600,000 gallons of the liquid, which is primarily made of sodium hydroxide and sodium sulfide. White liquor is used with heat to break down wood into kraft paper, a durable material used in packaging and other products.

The initial death toll was one confirmed fatality, with nine people injured. One hospitalized worker later died from injuries sustained in the incident. Seven workers remain hospitalized, along with a firefighter who responded to the scene.

Washington Governor Bob Ferguson described the incident as potentially "the deadliest industrial tragedy in modern Washington state history." Recovery operations began Wednesday after initial delays due to safety concerns about the structural integrity of the damaged tank. Officials indicated there was no hope of finding additional survivors and shifted operations from rescue to recovery efforts.

The U.S. Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board launched an investigation into the tank rupture. The cause of the failure remained unknown as of Wednesday.

Among the missing workers were two brothers who worked at the facility together, according to reports. The sprawling Longview plant employs about 1,000 people and produces material for tissues, printing paper, cups, plates and cartons.

After the rupture, white liquor spilled into a drainage ditch and contaminated the Columbia River. Dead carp were found following the spill, and authorities urged the public to avoid ditches and dikes while the area undergoes water testing. Officials said there were no issues with air quality or drinking water in Longview.

The tank still contained approximately 25,000 gallons of white liquor, which slowly leaked following the rupture. The Washington state department of ecology and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency are overseeing operations to determine environmental impacts.

The Nippon facility had faced safety complaints in March and May. According to Washington's labor and industries department, those complaints were unrelated to the tank that ruptured. One complaint involved a different valve. Since 2021, Nippon Dynawave has been fined $3,400 for three separate health and safety violations.

Brian Wood with Nippon Dynawave stated that the company was "focused on the victims and helping first responders recover the missing." He said the company would "cooperate to the maximum extent" with the investigation.

6:58 PM ET

The US government has proposed new funding rules that would allow officials to cancel research grants at any time, according to reports. The changes would also make peer review optional for grant applications.

Under the proposed rules, political staff would screen grant applications for forbidden topics before approval. The changes represent a significant shift in how federal research funding operates, moving away from traditional peer review processes that have guided scientific grant awards.

The proposal affects how billions of dollars in federal research funding would be distributed across universities, research institutions, and individual scientists. Grant recipients currently operate under agreements that provide funding stability for approved project durations.

Peer review has served as the standard method for evaluating scientific merit in federal grant competitions. The process involves independent experts assessing proposals based on scientific quality, methodology, and potential impact. Making this step optional removes a key safeguard in the grant approval process.

The addition of political staff screening introduces a new layer of review focused on identifying forbidden topics. This screening would occur before scientific merit evaluation, changing the order in which grant applications are assessed.

Researchers typically rely on multi-year grants to conduct experiments, hire staff, and publish findings. The ability to cancel grants at any time introduces uncertainty into long-term research planning. Scientists working on active grants could see their funding terminated without the protections currently built into grant agreements.

Federal agencies including the National Institutes of Health and National Science Foundation distribute research funding through competitive grant programs. These agencies have historically used peer review to maintain scientific standards and ensure taxpayer dollars support high-quality research.

The proposed rules would apply across federal agencies that award research grants. Implementation would require agencies to develop screening criteria and train political staff to review applications for prohibited subject matter.

The changes come as research institutions manage existing grant portfolios worth billions of dollars. Universities and research centers structure their budgets around expected grant funding, hiring personnel and purchasing equipment based on multi-year award commitments.

No timeline for implementing the proposed rules has been announced. The rules would need to go through a formal rulemaking process before taking effect.
