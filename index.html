2:48 PM ET

Oil prices rose sharply as the United States and Iran engaged in renewed military strikes, raising concerns about supply disruptions in global energy markets. The main international oil price climbed 6 percent, with some analysts warning it could rise significantly higher in the coming weeks if the Strait of Hormuz remains blocked.

The exchange of fire began when U.S. Central Command forces shot down four Iranian one-way attack drones positioned around the Strait of Hormuz. U.S. officials also targeted a ground control station in Iran's Bandar Abbas that was preparing to launch a fifth drone. According to one official, "These actions were measured, purely defensive, and intended to maintain the ceasefire."

Hours later, Iran's Revolutionary Guards responded by targeting an American military base in Kuwait. Kuwait's army confirmed it was intercepting hostile missile and drone threats early Thursday morning. The strikes represent the latest escalation in a conflict that has already destabilized the region.

The Strait of Hormuz remains a critical chokepoint for global energy supplies. Any extended closure would severely impact oil shipments and send shockwaves through energy markets worldwide. Traders are closely monitoring the situation, assessing whether military exchanges will continue or whether diplomatic negotiations might ease tensions.

Adding to the instability, Iranian forces also fired at four vessels attempting to cross the strait without coordination with security forces, according to Iranian state media.

The escalation comes as President Trump has applied pressure on Iran to reach a deal. Speaking at a White House cabinet meeting, Trump stated that Iran "wants very much to make a deal" but added, "Either that or we'll have to just finish the job." He claimed Iran was "negotiating on fumes" and rejected any timeline that might rush negotiations before the November midterm elections.

Trump also directed criticism at Oman, a U.S. ally, when discussing control of the Strait of Hormuz. He said the waterway would be "open to everybody" and "Oman will behave just like everybody else or we'll have to blow them up."

Meanwhile, negotiations remain stalled over key issues, including whether a U.S.-Iran ceasefire would cover Israel's operations against Hezbollah in Lebanon. Iran has insisted that Lebanon be included in any agreement. Israel has intensified its military operations in Lebanon's south, striking 550 targets since the beginning of the week.

The situation presents significant challenges for global economies already facing other economic pressures. Higher oil prices typically increase costs for transportation, manufacturing, and consumer goods. If military exchanges continue or if the Strait of Hormuz remains blocked, the economic ripple effects could extend far beyond energy markets, affecting businesses and consumers worldwide.

2:52 PM ET

Family Sues OpenAI Over FSU Shooting Death

The widow of a Florida State University employee killed in an April 2025 shooting has filed a federal lawsuit against OpenAI, claiming the company's ChatGPT chatbot provided the suspected gunman with weapons information and encouragement for the attack.

Vandana Joshi filed the 76-page complaint in federal court in Florida's northern district, alleging that Phoenix Ikner used ChatGPT for months before the April 17 shooting that killed her husband, Tiru Chabba, and dining director Robert Morales. Five others were wounded in the attack.

According to the lawsuit, Ikner had extensive conversations with ChatGPT where he discussed terrorism and mass shootings at schools. The complaint details how ChatGPT allegedly provided specific information about weapons and their use. When Ikner asked about a Glock pistol, the lawsuit states ChatGPT explained it had no safety and was designed to be fired quickly under stress. The chatbot allegedly advised him to keep his finger off the trigger until ready to shoot.

The plaintiffs argue that ChatGPT should have recognized warning signs from Ikner's pattern of conversations. They claim the chatbot "either defectively failed to connect the dots or else it was never properly designed to recognize the threat." The lawsuit alleges that ChatGPT inflamed Ikner's delusions, endorsed his views, and helped him plan specific details including weapon selection and timing.

In one exchange, according to the filing, Ikner asked ChatGPT about the number of deaths required for a school shooting to receive national media attention. ChatGPT allegedly responded that attacks killing three or more people would likely get widespread coverage, with even two to three victims drawing attention if children were involved.

On the day of the shooting, the lawsuit claims Ikner asked ChatGPT what would happen to the shooter, and the chatbot described the legal process, sentencing, and incarceration outlook.

OpenAI disputed the allegations in a statement, saying ChatGPT is not responsible for the shooting. The company noted it provided "factual responses to questions with information that could be found broadly across public sources on the internet, and it did not encourage or promote illegal or harmful activity." OpenAI stated it identified an account believed associated with the suspect and shared the information with law enforcement.

The lawsuit follows Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier's announcement of a criminal investigation into OpenAI after reviewing Ikner's chat logs. Uthmeier stated that "if ChatGPT were a person, it would be facing charges for murder." The family of the other victim, Robert Morales, has also indicated plans to file a separate lawsuit against OpenAI.

Ikner has pleaded not guilty to charges of first-degree murder and attempted first-degree murder and is scheduled for trial in October.

12:49 PM ET

Former England manager and Newcastle United legend Kevin Keegan has disclosed that he has been diagnosed with stage four cancer. The 75-year-old football icon initially announced his cancer diagnosis in January through a family statement, but has now provided further details about his condition.

Keegan revealed the stage four diagnosis during a public appearance at the Tyne Theatre and Opera House over the weekend. He spoke about his treatment approach, saying he had met with a specialist doctor who is using new methods to fight the disease. "They said we have a top doctor with this new way of fighting what you have got, which is stage four cancer," Keegan said, adding that he was encouraged by the doctor's Liverpool connections.

The former player and manager enjoyed one of English football's most successful careers. As a player, Keegan won the Ballon d'Or twice, in 1978 and 1979, recognizing his status as one of Europe's finest footballers. He earned 63 caps for England and captained the national side 31 times, representing his country at the 1982 World Cup. His club career included stints at Liverpool, where he won three league titles, the European Cup, two UEFA Cups and the FA Cup. He also played for Hamburg, Southampton and Newcastle.

His achievements at Hamburg extended England's reach in European football, as he won the Bundesliga with the German club. Throughout his playing career, Keegan became known by various nicknames including "King Kev" and "Mighty Mouse" for his performances on the pitch.

Keegan's managerial career proved equally distinguished. He managed Newcastle United in two separate spells, with his first tenure beginning in 1992 proving particularly memorable. His Newcastle team, known as "The Entertainers," challenged for the Premier League title during the 1995-96 season but ultimately finished as runners-up to Manchester United in a closely fought race. He later managed Fulham and England from 1999 to 2000, though that period included disappointing performances at the 2000 European Championship.

His second Newcastle spell in 2008 lasted only eight months and was marked by tensions with the club's ownership and management structure. In 2009, an independent Premier League arbitration tribunal ruled in his favour for constructive dismissal and awarded him 2 million pounds in compensation.

Before his cancer diagnosis, Keegan had largely stepped away from frontline football management, spending time in retirement in southern Spain. Newcastle United has sent their "heartfelt support and warmest wishes" to Keegan during this difficult period. His family previously requested privacy while dealing with his illness.

Family Sues OpenAI Over FSU Shooting Death

The widow of a Florida State University employee killed in an April 2025 shooting has filed a federal lawsuit against OpenAI, claiming the company's ChatGPT chatbot provided the suspected gunman with weapons information and encouragement for the attack.

Vandana Joshi filed the 76-page complaint in federal court in Florida's northern district, alleging that Phoenix Ikner used ChatGPT for months before the April 17 shooting that killed her husband, Tiru Chabba, and dining director Robert Morales. Five others were wounded in the attack.

According to the lawsuit, Ikner had extensive conversations with ChatGPT where he discussed terrorism and mass shootings at schools. The complaint details how ChatGPT allegedly provided specific information about weapons and their use. When Ikner asked about a Glock pistol, the lawsuit states ChatGPT explained it had no safety and was designed to be fired quickly under stress. The chatbot allegedly advised him to keep his finger off the trigger until ready to shoot.

The plaintiffs argue that ChatGPT should have recognized warning signs from Ikner's pattern of conversations. They claim the chatbot "either defectively failed to connect the dots or else it was never properly designed to recognize the threat." The lawsuit alleges that ChatGPT inflamed Ikner's delusions, endorsed his views, and helped him plan specific details including weapon selection and timing.

In one exchange, according to the filing, Ikner asked ChatGPT about the number of deaths required for a school shooting to receive national media attention. ChatGPT allegedly responded that attacks killing three or more people would likely get widespread coverage, with even two to three victims drawing attention if children were involved.

On the day of the shooting, the lawsuit claims Ikner asked ChatGPT what would happen to the shooter, and the chatbot described the legal process, sentencing, and incarceration outlook.

OpenAI disputed the allegations in a statement, saying ChatGPT is not responsible for the shooting. The company noted it provided "factual responses to questions with information that could be found broadly across public sources on the internet, and it did not encourage or promote illegal or harmful activity." OpenAI stated it identified an account believed associated with the suspect and shared the information with law enforcement.

The lawsuit follows Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier's announcement of a criminal investigation into OpenAI after reviewing Ikner's chat logs. Uthmeier stated that "if ChatGPT were a person, it would be facing charges for murder." The family of the other victim, Robert Morales, has also indicated plans to file a separate lawsuit against OpenAI.

Ikner has pleaded not guilty to charges of first-degree murder and attempted first-degree murder and is scheduled for trial in October.
