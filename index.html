During the roughly four-hour session, Bondi declined to answer numerous questions. She directed responsibility to acting Attorney General Todd Blanche regarding how the files were handled.

The release of the transcript came after a contentious period between Bondi and the committee. The House panel had initially scheduled Bondi for a deposition on April 14, but the Justice Department informed the committee that she would not appear. The department argued that because Bondi no longer held the position of attorney general, the subpoena no longer applied since it had been issued in her official capacity.

The committee's Democrats responded by filing a civil contempt resolution against Bondi for not appearing at the scheduled deposition. Representative Robert Garcia, the top Democrat on the committee, announced the action and stated that Bondi had "illegally defied our committee, skipped her deposition, and has refused to cooperate."

However, shortly after the Democrats announced the contempt charges, the oversight committee revealed that Bondi would appear before the panel on May 29. Republicans on the committee said the contempt action was "completely unnecessary" given that Bondi was now scheduled to testify. The oversight Democrats noted the timing, writing that the appearance was announced just 45 minutes after they filed the contempt charges.

Bondi had been subpoenaed by the committee while still serving as attorney general. The committee's chair, Republican Representative James Comer, wrote in the subpoena letter that there were questions regarding the Justice Department's handling of the Epstein investigation and its compliance with the Epstein Files Transparency Act.

The Justice Department faced criticism over its compliance with that law after failing to meet a December 19 deadline to release relevant files. The department instead released what it said were the full files on January 31. Survivors of Epstein's abuse raised concerns that sensitive personal information was improperly disclosed in the files, while several lawmakers criticized some of the redactions included in the documents.

Thousands of ultra-Orthodox Jews gathered in Jerusalem on Tuesday to protest a law requiring them to serve in the military. The demonstration turned tragic when a bus struck a crowd, killing a 14-year-old boy who became trapped underneath the vehicle. Police detained the bus driver and launched an investigation into the incident.

Video footage showed the bus driving directly into the assembled crowd of ultra-Orthodox men. According to police, the driver claimed he was assaulted by rioters before the incident occurred. Officers said a small group of protesters had begun violently disrupting public order by blocking traffic routes, damaging buses, setting fires to trash bins, and throwing objects at police and journalists at the scene.

The protests underscore deepening divisions within Israeli society as the country faces ongoing military manpower shortages following two years of conflict on multiple fronts. Ultra-Orthodox party leaders have consistently opposed conscription of religious students who pursue full-time Jewish text studies. Under a system established at Israel's founding in 1948, these students have traditionally received exemptions from mandatory military service.

This longstanding arrangement has become increasingly controversial among other Israelis, particularly as tens of thousands of conscripts and reservists have been mobilized across several military fronts despite a fragile ceasefire that ended the Gaza conflict.

The ultra-Orthodox population comprises 14 percent of Israel's Jewish population, and keeping their political parties in the government coalition remains crucial for Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's right-wing coalition survival. Netanyahu faces pressure from opposition members and some coalition partners to expand military recruitment, but he relies on ultra-Orthodox parties for his parliamentary majority.

A new draft law was proposed in parliament's foreign affairs and defense committee after a previous conscription bill failed to pass in July. The United Torah Judaism party, a major ultra-Orthodox faction, withdrew from the government over that failed bill, reducing Netanyahu's coalition to 60 seats in the 120-seat parliament. Ministers from Shas, the other significant ultra-Orthodox party, resigned from cabinet positions over the conscription issue, though the party has not formally left the coalition.

Mass demonstrations against the conscription law have become regular occurrences in recent months as the debate intensifies. These protests highlight the competing priorities within Israeli society between military necessity and religious community autonomy, a tension that has grown more acute as security pressures mount and the need for soldiers increases.

A flesh-eating parasite not seen in the United States for nearly 60 years has been confirmed in a calf in south Texas, about 50 miles from the Mexico border. The detection of the New World screwworm has alarmed agriculture officials and raised concerns about potential threats to the nation's cattle industry.

Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins announced the discovery late Wednesday, marking the first confirmed case in Texas since 1966. The parasite's larvae feed on the blood of warm-blooded animals and can spread rapidly through livestock populations if not controlled.

The screwworm was eradicated from the United States in the 1970s after causing tens of millions of dollars in losses to the livestock industry. Its return comes at a particularly sensitive time, as beef prices across the country have reached record highs. Officials worry that an outbreak could worsen food shortages and further increase prices.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture and Texas officials have been warning livestock owners about the parasite's progression through Mexico for over a year. The screwworm has advanced more than 1,100 miles from southern Mexico northward despite efforts to contain it.

Female screwworm flies lay eggs in open wounds on cattle and other animals. When the larvae hatch, they consume blood and living flesh from the host animal. Without treatment, infected animals will die.

Rollins emphasized that the situation remains manageable. "There is no threat of mass infestation," she said, noting that this is the only confirmed case so far. "There is no reason to believe this incursion will result in establishment of the pest in our country."

However, Texas Agriculture Commissioner Sid Miller expressed frustration with federal efforts, stating that the USDA has missed important components of its containment strategy. He called for increased deployment of the screwworm adult suppression system, or Swass, which uses attractants, bait, and targeted insecticides to reduce adult populations before releasing sterile flies to prevent reproduction.

Miller noted that Swass was successfully used to eradicate the screwworm in Mexico and Texas previously. "USDA already owns the playbook," he said, "the only question is whether USDA will use it before this situation gets worse."

In March, the USDA announced a partnership with the Army Corps of Engineers and Mortenson Construction to build a new sterile fly production facility at Moore Airbase in Edinburg, Texas. This facility aims to support ongoing containment efforts.

The parasite poses no threat to humans or food safety directly. However, the risk to wildlife in affected areas remains a concern. Previous outbreaks, including cases in Florida in 2016 and a Maryland case in 2025, were contained without spreading further.

Helion, a fusion energy startup supported by Sam Altman, has secured $465 million in funding to advance construction of a power plant intended to deliver electricity to Microsoft. The investment provides the company with crucial resources as it works toward completing the facility by 2028.

Fusion energy technology attempts to generate power by forcing atomic nuclei together, mirroring the energy production process of the sun. This approach differs fundamentally from nuclear fission, which splits atoms and generates radioactive waste. Fusion technology promises cleaner energy with reduced environmental impact, though the field has struggled for decades to achieve sustained commercial-scale energy production.

Helion has entered into an agreement with Microsoft to supply power from its planned fusion plant. The 2028 completion target is ambitious for an industry where similar projects typically require years or decades to reach operational status. The company must successfully demonstrate that its fusion technology operates reliably, generates electricity at scale, and can deliver power to Microsoft's facilities.

The funding round reflects increasing investor appetite for alternative energy solutions as organizations seek carbon-free power sources. Technology companies face mounting pressure to lower their environmental footprint while managing rising electricity consumption from data centers and artificial intelligence infrastructure. Microsoft has announced aggressive climate commitments and is investigating multiple clean energy pathways, including nuclear and renewable options.

Helion operates within a growing field of fusion startups attempting to bring the technology to market. Other companies pursuing fusion development have secured backing from major investors and government support, though none has yet successfully operated a fusion facility that produces more energy than it consumes on a sustained basis. The $465 million funding round positions Helion to advance its technological approach, despite the company still facing substantial technical and regulatory obstacles before achieving power delivery to Microsoft by 2028.

The broader energy sector is experiencing renewed interest in fusion technology as demand for clean electricity accelerates. Large technology and AI companies including Google's parent Alphabet and OpenAI have invested in fusion ventures. This wave of investment comes as the artificial intelligence industry strains existing power grids, creating urgency around developing new energy sources.

The Trump administration has also signaled support for fusion development. Since taking office in January, the government has pursued deregulation of the nuclear energy industry and promoted technology advancement, including executive orders designating fusion development as a national security priority. The Department of Energy has established a new Office of Fusion aimed at preparing the technology for commercial deployment as rapidly as possible.

Despite investor enthusiasm and government backing, fusion energy remains commercially unproven. No utility-scale fusion plant currently operates, and some scientists remain skeptical about achieving commercial viability. Helion's aggressive timeline and substantial funding position it as a significant contender in the race to commercialize fusion energy technology.

Liverpool identified Iraola as the ideal fit for their desired playing style and moved decisively to appoint him ahead of competition from other top clubs. Milan, Bayer Leverkusen and Crystal Palace had all expressed interest in the former Bournemouth manager. While other coaches including Stuttgart's Sebastian Hoeness and Pierre Sage of Lens were considered, Iraola was Liverpool's favored option from the outset and the only candidate formally spoken to about the position.

Sporting director Richard Hughes led Liverpool's approach for Iraola, having previously worked with him at Bournemouth in 2023. Hughes and Michael Edwards, chief executive of football for Liverpool's owners Fenway Sports Group, conducted a review following the team's poor title defense under Slot. They concluded that a change was necessary to implement a more aggressive, attacking style of play.

Negotiations progressed smoothly after formal talks began earlier in the week. Iraola made clear from the beginning that Liverpool were his only target after his contract with Bournemouth expired. The straightforward process was aided by Hughes's close working relationship with Iraola's agent Iñaki Ibáñez, who also represents Xabi Alonso.

Speaking about his appointment, Iraola stated: "At the beginning when you arrive at any club, I think you need to kind of prove a little bit yourself. You need to earn the right also to belong. I want to do this as quickly as possible so I can also celebrate with them."

He added on his ambitions: "I think Liverpool gives me the chance to coach top players, and top players give you the chance to fight for titles. To win titles. Obviously when you arrive at a place, you cannot promise everything. But it's true that I understand where I'm coming and what is expected. I'm ready for the challenge."

Iraola has signed a contract that runs until the end of the 2027/28 season. The two-year length reflects his preferred approach to managing his career, following the pattern he established at both Bournemouth and Rayo Vallecano.

The new manager intends to bring his backroom team from Bournemouth to Anfield, including assistants Tommy Elphick and Shaun Cooper, analyst Tom Webber and fitness coach Pablo de la Torre. Liverpool have not yet finalized all additions to their coaching staff.

Liverpool believe Iraola is one of the best coaches available and well-suited to evolving their team's style. His track record at Bournemouth of developing players and building an exciting, high-pressing team without significant financial resources particularly appealed to the club's leadership.

Marjane Satrapi, the French-Iranian artist and graphic novelist whose memoir Persepolis transformed international understanding of Iran, has died at age 56. Her family stated that she "died of sadness" following the death of her husband, Swedish producer Mattias Ripa, who passed away on April 8 of the previous year. In the weeks after his death, Satrapi posted on Instagram: "For I lost the love of my life."

Born in 1969 in Rasht, Iran, near the Caspian Sea, Satrapi grew up in Tehran with her father, an engineer, and her mother, a dress designer. As a teenager, her parents sent her to Europe to continue her education and shield her from the restrictions of the Islamic Republic. She eventually settled in France, arriving in 1994 and becoming a French citizen in 2006.

Satrapi published Persepolis in 2000 as a comic book memoir that became an international publishing sensation. The work chronicled her rebellious childhood during Iran's revolution and its aftermath, following her experience of the country's violence and ideological control before she was sent alone to Europe at age 14. The memoir sold millions of copies and established Satrapi as one of the world's most widely read Iranian authors.

When asked about her intentions for the work, Satrapi told the Guardian in 2024 that she wanted Western readers to recognize Iranian people as human beings like themselves. In a 2020 interview with El País, she recalled having minimal expectations for publication, saying: "With Persepolis, I didn't even think I'd find a publisher. I thought I'd make 50 photocopies for my friends to read."

The animated film adaptation, which Satrapi co-directed, became an international success and made her the first woman nominated for an Academy Award for Best Animated Feature. She explained that her purpose in creating comics was to reassure young Iranians they were heard and supported globally, asking: "If they kill you and the whole world doesn't care, how is that?"

Beyond comics, Satrapi directed five feature films, including Radioactive (2019), which starred Rosamund Pike as scientist Marie Curie. After stepping away from comics for years, she returned to the medium in 2024, coordinating Woman, Life, Freedom, a collaborative graphic work created by 17 Iranian and international comic artists alongside academics. The book examined the protest movement following the 2022 death in custody of Mahsa Amini, a Kurdish-Iranian woman. Satrapi stated: "The only thing I can do is cultural work. This book is a message to the Iranian people to say, listen, you are not alone."

Tributes came from across French politics and culture. President Emmanuel Macron described her as "a great artist who turned her Iranian childhood into a universal tale." Margaret Atwood told the Guardian that Persepolis made an enormous impact, noting that while she only wrote about oppression in theocratic regimes, Satrapi lived it.
