Israeli troops have captured Beaufort Castle, a Crusader fortress in southern Lebanon, in what marks the country's deepest military incursion into Lebanese territory in more than 26 years. The castle, also known as Qalaat al-Shakif, sits on a mountain ridge near the city of Nabatiyeh and commands sweeping views across southern Lebanon and northern Israel.

The fortress dates back to the 1100s and has changed hands multiple times throughout history. Sultan Baibars captured it in 1268, and it has proven valuable in modern conflicts between Israel and Lebanon. Israeli forces previously took control of the castle in 1982 during their occupation of southern Lebanon, which lasted until 2000.

Defense Minister Israel Katz announced the capture on his Telegram channel, writing: "Forty-four years after the heroic Battle of Beaufort, and on this day commemorating the soldiers who fell in the First Lebanon War (1982), our troops have returned to the summit of Beaufort and once again raised the Israeli flag there." Photographs showed Israeli troops outside the castle with the Israeli flag raised above the citadel.

The operation came after intense fighting and airstrikes in surrounding villages where Israeli forces engaged with Hezbollah members. The Israeli military stated that Hezbollah had launched hundreds of projectiles from the ridge toward Israeli civilians and soldiers, and that troops were operating against launch infrastructure in the area.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu visited troops along Israel's northern border on Friday, accompanied by Katz, where they received briefings on military activities and met with field brigade commanders. The capture followed one of the heaviest days of Hezbollah fire toward northern Israel since an April ceasefire, prompting school closures and restrictions on Saturday.

Israeli forces have expanded operations beyond previous boundaries, crossing the Litani River, which previously served as a de facto boundary between the two countries. The military designated the area between the Litani and Zahrani Rivers as a combat zone. Israeli troops have also been operating near Nabatiyeh, a major Hezbollah stronghold, with the group reporting clashes in several towns north of the river.

The Lebanese Health Ministry reported that 3,371 people have died in the country since March, including both civilians and combatants. The Israeli army announced that 25 Israeli military personnel have been killed since early March, with one soldier killed by a Hezbollah explosive drone in southern Lebanon.

The advance takes place despite a nominal ceasefire in place since April and just before planned talks at the US State Department scheduled for June 2 and 3. Some residents have already evacuated the area due to intense strikes, though people remain in many towns across the region.

US Strikes Kill Scores of Children at Iranian School

A US Tomahawk missile struck a primary school in Minab, Iran, killing more than 168 people, mostly children, according to video evidence and investigative analysis. The strike occurred on February 28 when US-Israeli operations against Iran began.

The missile hit the Shajareh Tayyebeh primary school as it targeted an adjacent Islamic Revolutionary Guards naval compound. The strike demolished approximately half the school building, killing scores of seven- to 12-year-old girls attending morning classes.

Video footage released by the Iranian news agency Mehr and verified by the investigative collective Bellingcat shows a Tomahawk missile striking the Minab compound. Munitions experts confirmed the weapon's origin. NR Jenzen-Jones, director of Armament Research Services, stated: "The video shows a Tomahawk missile striking a target. Given the belligerents, that indicates it is a US strike, as Israel is not known to possess Tomahawk missiles."

Jenzen-Jones added that the weapon was clearly not an Iranian Soumar missile, which has a distinctive external engine located toward the rear on the underside. He noted: "Despite various claims circulating online, the munition in question is clearly not an Iranian Soumar missile."

The evidence includes verified videos from the Guardian, photographs of the aftermath, and satellite imagery showing at least four buildings were hit during the strikes: the school and three IRGC compound buildings. Though the school building was once part of the wider IRGC complex, it had been walled off from the compound for at least eight years with clear educational markers visible in satellite images, including playing fields and colorful wall murals.

US President Donald Trump disputed responsibility for the school bombing. "In my opinion, based on what I've seen, that was done by Iran," Trump said on Sunday without providing evidence. He suggested Iranian munitions were inaccurate and claimed Iran was responsible.

US military spokespeople said they are "investigating" the strike. UNESCO described the school bombing as a "grave violation" of international law. Human Rights Watch called for an investigation into whether the strike constitutes a war crime.

The incident represents a significant escalation in the conflict between the United States and Iran. US military bases throughout the Gulf region have historically been potential targets in any confrontation with Iran. The strike on a civilian school has drawn international scrutiny and raised questions about adherence to laws protecting educational facilities during armed conflict.

The bombing adds to mounting tensions between Washington and Tehran as diplomatic efforts continue to address regional security concerns and the strategic Strait of Hormuz, through which significant portions of global oil supplies pass.

2:00 AM ET

A meteor exploded over northeastern Massachusetts and southeastern New Hampshire on Saturday afternoon, producing a powerful sonic boom that reverberated across the region, according to Nasa. The space agency confirmed the event occurred just after 2pm local time.

The fireball was traveling at approximately 75,000 miles per hour at an altitude of about 40 miles when it broke apart in the atmosphere. The explosion released energy equivalent to 300 tons of TNT, Nasa's deputy news chief Jennifer Dooren said in a statement to AFP.

The blast created loud booms that were heard and felt by hundreds of people across a wide geographic area. Reports of the sonic boom came from Massachusetts, New Hampshire, and surrounding regions as the shock waves traveled outward from the explosion site.

When meteoroids enter Earth's atmosphere at high speeds, they create visible streaks of light commonly called shooting stars. In this case, the incoming space rock was large enough and moving fast enough that it produced significant effects that were noticed by many residents across the northeastern United States.

According to Nasa's website, most space rocks smaller than a football field will break apart when entering Earth's atmosphere. Though rare, some can explode in the air as this meteor did. Each year, approximately 17,000 meteorites fall to Earth, though most burn up during atmospheric entry or land in unpopulated areas.

The Saturday explosion followed a similar event in Ohio earlier in the week. A meteor over Ohio created a large boom that was felt as far away as Pennsylvania on Tuesday morning. That meteor, which was 2 meters in diameter and weighed about 6 tons, entered the atmosphere at around 9am local time. Residents described the sound as similar to fireworks that "lingered and rumbled like thunder." The National Weather Service's Cleveland office confirmed satellite data suggesting the boom resulted from a meteor.

In that Ohio incident, the meteor was traveling at 45,000 miles per hour before breaking apart over Valley City. Fragments continued southward, producing meteorites in the vicinity of Medina County. Despite the weight of the space rock, Nasa classified it as a small asteroid. Brian Mitchell, an NWS meteorologist, told the Associated Press that while small fragments might reach the ground, much of the meteor would have burned up in the atmosphere.

No reports of damage or injuries have emerged from either meteor event. Such occurrences, while startling to residents who experience the sonic booms, typically happen at altitudes high enough to prevent ground impact damage. The explosions represent the kind of celestial events that periodically remind people of the active space environment surrounding Earth.

Yesterday, 9:04 PM ET

President Trump has asked for several amendments to a deal framework his negotiators reached with Iranian counterparts, according to administration officials. The president wants the agreement finalized soon but is seeking to strengthen key provisions, particularly around Iran's nuclear program.

Trump's request has launched another round of negotiations between the parties that could extend several days. The tentative agreement reached this week would extend the current ceasefire and reopen the Strait of Hormuz if approved.

Trump announced on Saturday that an agreement had been "largely negotiated" after calls with Pakistani mediators, Gulf allies, and Israel. He posted on his social media platform that "final aspects and details" of a "memorandum of understanding" were still being discussed and would be announced shortly.

According to reports, the draft agreement includes provisions that the strait would reopen without tolls during a 60-day ceasefire extension, while Iran would be able to sell oil freely. Negotiations on curbing Iran's nuclear program would occur over 30 to 60 days. In exchange, the U.S. would lift its blockade on Iranian ports.

However, Iran's Fars news agency, which is close to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, disputed Trump's characterization of progress. The agency reported that "the management of the Strait, determining the route, time, method of passage, and issuing permits will continue to be the monopoly and discretion of the Islamic Republic of Iran," and said Trump's assertion that an agreement was nearly final was "inconsistent with reality."

Pakistan's Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif congratulated Trump on his peace efforts and said Pakistan hoped to host another round of talks between the U.S. and Iran soon. Pakistan's army chief, Syed Asim Munir, has been a key figure in the negotiations, holding recent meetings in Tehran with Iranian leaders.

Trump has described his negotiation strategy as conditional, telling media outlets he would only sign a deal "where we get everything we want," adding that if an agreement was not reached, the U.S. would begin striking Iran again.

The potential deal has sparked criticism from Republican hawks, particularly former officials from Trump's first term. Mike Pompeo, who served as CIA director and secretary of state, denounced the rumored terms as too similar to the 2015 nuclear deal negotiated under President Barack Obama, which Trump withdrew from in 2018.

Former Obama negotiators defended the approach differently. Robert Malley responded to criticism by stating that while the current deal might not follow the exact path his team would have taken, "if this deal brings an end to an unlawful, unjustifiable war, to the senseless loss of life and destruction, and to the cascading global economic fallout, I am quite sure we'd willingly accept it over the alternative."

The timing for final approval remains uncertain as negotiations continue.
