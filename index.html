Beatty challenged the board's authority to rename the federally designated venue without congressional approval. The Kennedy Center was originally established by Congress as the national cultural center and named in honor of President John F. Kennedy.

The judge's decision addressed a core legal question: whether the board of trustees had the power to make such a significant change to a federal institution on its own authority. Cooper's ruling concluded that they did not.

Cooper ordered Kennedy Center officials to comply with the name removal within 14 days of the ruling. The directive requires the removal of all physical signage bearing Trump's name and the elimination of any references to a "Trump Kennedy Center" from official materials.

The ruling also halted a planned two-year closure of the Kennedy Center for renovation work that had been announced alongside the name change decision. This closure would have suspended the venue's regular programming schedule during the renovation period. The court's order prevents this extensive shutdown, at least for now, though the judge did not make a permanent determination about future renovation plans.

The decision represents a significant legal setback for the renaming effort. It prevents not only the name change but also the lengthy operational disruption that had been scheduled to begin in the coming months. The renovation plans tied to the closure will require revision following the court order.

The Kennedy Center has not yet issued a public statement regarding whether it will appeal the judge's decision. The venue continues its regular programming schedule while the legal situation remains in flux.

The case reflects broader legal tensions about presidential authority and the proper role of institutional boards when managing federally designated properties. By requiring congressional approval for any renaming, the judge reinforced existing legal protections for federally established monuments and cultural institutions. The decision ensures that significant changes to such institutions require action from the legislative branch rather than unilateral decisions by boards of trustees.
