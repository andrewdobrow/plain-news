Yesterday, 5:11 PM ET

Anthropic has surpassed OpenAI in valuation following a major funding announcement. The AI company raised $65 billion in Series H funding, reaching a post-money valuation of $965 billion and claiming the title of world's most valuable AI startup.

The funding round was led by Altimeter Capital, Dragoneer, Greenoaks, and Sequoia Capital. Anthropic's ascent marks a significant shift in the competitive dynamics of the artificial intelligence industry. The company, which develops Claude, a large language model that competes directly with OpenAI's GPT models, has transformed from being perceived as a smaller player in the global AI race into a dominant force.

Much of Anthropic's rapid growth stems from widespread enterprise adoption of its products. The company gained particular traction following the release of coding assistants and tools like Claude Code and Cowork. Krishna Rao, Anthropic's chief financial officer, stated that "Claude is increasingly indispensable to our growing global community of customers, and we work tirelessly to make tools like Claude Code and Cowork more helpful, more powerful, and more adaptable to their needs."

Anthropic's business strategy has focused on enterprise and coding services rather than consumer-facing products. The company has also distinguished itself through its emphasis on AI safety. One of Anthropic's cofounders participated in Pope Leo's recent release of an extensive document warning against the dangers of artificial intelligence and calling for greater restrictions on the technology.

The company has maintained its safety commitments despite government pressure. Anthropic engaged in a legal dispute with the Pentagon after refusing to remove safeguards from Claude that would enable use in mass surveillance or autonomous weapons systems capable of killing without human intervention. Reports indicate the U.S. government has nonetheless used Claude for military operations.

Anthropic recently withheld the release of its Mythos model due to cybersecurity concerns, prompting international alarm about potential vulnerabilities to financial systems and critical infrastructure. Vice President JD Vance contacted AI company leaders urging greater cooperation on security matters.

The company has also become an influential political force, investing millions in lobbying efforts and political action committees supporting candidates and legislation aligned with its regulatory vision for artificial intelligence. Unlike other tech industry leaders and OpenAI, Anthropic advocates for increased government oversight of AI development.

The record valuation reflects continued investor confidence in AI despite public skepticism about the technology. This comes after OpenAI raised $122 billion in March to achieve a valuation of $852 billion, with reports suggesting the company may pursue a $1 trillion initial public offering later this year.

Yesterday, 5:46 PM ET

More than 245 people are now suspected to have died from Ebola in the Democratic Republic of Congo, with health officials confirming 65 deaths so far. The outbreak, centered in Ituri province, has prompted urgent international coordination as the virus spreads across borders.

The World Health Organization estimates the death rate between 30 and 50 percent based on confirmed cases. "It means that up to five out of 10 people are likely to die," said Anaïs Legand from the WHO's high threat pathogens team.

There have been 246 suspected cases reported in Ituri province, a conflict-affected region that shares borders with Uganda and South Sudan. Uganda has already confirmed cases, including a 59-year-old man who died in a Kampala hospital after traveling from the DRC. Cases have been reported mainly in the Mongwalu and Rwampara health zones, with suspected cases also appearing in the neighboring city of Bunia.

The medical charity Médecins Sans Frontières warned that the spread of the virus is deeply alarming. The WHO director visited the region worst affected by the outbreak to support containment efforts.

Health workers in affected areas lack adequate equipment to respond to the crisis. Aid organizations are racing to provide resources and support. The situation is complicated by the location's challenging circumstances. Ituri province is a mining hub where thousands of people work in close proximity daily, and it remains a conflict zone with ongoing fighting between rebel groups. Displaced people are being forced into overcrowded camps to escape the violence, making disease control even harder.

The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention detected the Ebola virus in 13 of 20 samples tested. Notably, the virus identified is the Bundibugyo strain, for which there is no licensed vaccine. This differs from the Zaire strain typically seen in previous DRC outbreaks. Only two prior outbreaks of Bundibugyo virus have occurred, in 2007 and 2012.

The DRC has experienced 16 Ebola outbreaks since the virus was first identified there in 1976. Experts point to several factors behind these recurring epidemics. Close human contact with animal reservoirs, particularly bats and possibly primates, creates risk. The movement of people between rural and urban environments, combined with the tropical climate and extensive rainforest coverage, enables transmission.

International health organizations are coordinating with Congolese authorities, Uganda, and South Sudan to deploy medical teams and supplies. The Africa CDC is convening urgent meetings with pharmaceutical companies to address the outbreak. Efforts focus on isolating patients, tracing contacts, and providing treatment to those infected.

The regional spread underscores the need for rapid coordination. "Given the high population movement between affected areas and neighbouring countries, rapid regional coordination is essential," said Dr Jean Kaseya, director general of Africa CDC.

11 Dead After Tank Explosion at Washington Paper Mill

Eleven people died in an explosion at a paper mill in Longview, Washington. Recovery crews have retrieved all victims from the site, and authorities have confirmed their identities. The disaster occurred at Nippon Dynawave Packaging Co. on Tuesday morning when an industrial tank ruptured and released white liquor, a highly corrosive chemical mixture used in paper manufacturing...

Yesterday, 10:03 PM ET

Eleven people died in an explosion at a paper mill in Longview, Washington. Recovery crews have retrieved all victims from the site, and authorities have confirmed their identities.

The disaster occurred at Nippon Dynawave Packaging Co. on Tuesday morning when an industrial tank ruptured and released white liquor, a highly corrosive chemical mixture used in paper manufacturing. Officials initially reported one death and nine injuries. As recovery operations continued, one hospitalized victim died, bringing the total death toll to 11. Seven workers remained hospitalized following the incident.

Governor Bob Ferguson stated at a news conference that the state was "bracing ourselves for this being the deadliest industrial tragedy in modern Washington state history."

The tank held approximately 600,000 gallons of liquid composed mainly of sodium hydroxide and sodium sulfide. White liquor is used with heat to break down wood into kraft paper, a durable material used for packaging and shopping bags. About 25,000 gallons remained in the damaged tank and slowly leaked into a drainage ditch.

Recovery operations began Wednesday following delays caused by structural safety concerns with the damaged tank. Matt Amos of the Longview fire department said recovery efforts would be "slow, methodical and deliberate." Crews performed decontamination procedures on recovered victims before transport to the coroner, and responders worked in chemical protective equipment throughout operations.

The chemical spill contaminated a drainage ditch and entered the Columbia River on Tuesday. Dead carp were found in the water. Authorities advised the public to avoid ditches and dikes in the area while water testing continued. However, officials reported no air quality or drinking water concerns in Longview.

The US Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board announced it would launch a federal investigation into the incident. The Washington state department of ecology and the US Environmental Protection Agency oversaw operations to assess environmental impacts.

The sprawling Longview facility employs approximately 1,000 people and manufactures material for tissues, printing paper, cups, plates and cartons. It sits along the Columbia River near other timber, paper and chemical businesses.

Prior to the explosion, safety complaints were filed against Nippon Dynawave in March and May. The state's labor and industries department stated both complaints were unrelated to the current situation. One involved an anonymous complaint about a tank valve, though it was not the tank that imploded. Since 2021, the company had been fined $3,400 for three separate health and safety violations found by state inspectors.

Brian Wood with Nippon Dynawave said the company was focused on victims and would cooperate fully with the investigation. "These are our people," he stated at a news conference. The facility remains closed as investigators examine the scene to determine what caused the tank failure.

Yesterday, 11:23 PM ET

A meteor exploded over northeastern Massachusetts and southeastern New Hampshire on Saturday afternoon, triggering sonic booms that reverberated across a wide swath of the northeastern United States. NASA confirmed the event occurred just after 2pm local time.

The fireball was traveling at 75,000 miles per hour at an altitude of 40 miles when it broke apart in the atmosphere. The explosion produced a blast equivalent to 300 tons of TNT, according to NASA's deputy news chief Jennifer Dooren.

Reports of the event came from a vast geographic area. Witnesses across New England, from Delaware to Montreal, reported hearing the mysterious loud boom. Residents in the Boston area and throughout Massachusetts described the sound and vibrations that initially puzzled many who experienced it.

The meteor's fragmentation in the atmosphere explains why the explosion was so widely heard without corresponding reports of impact damage. When meteors are large enough and enter at the proper angle, the extreme heat and pressure of atmospheric entry can cause them to fragment violently, producing powerful sonic booms audible across multiple states and into Canada.

The event took place over a populated region, making it particularly notable due to the number of witnesses. Social media quickly filled with reports from residents trying to determine the source of the mysterious sound.

Meteors entering Earth's atmosphere occur regularly, but most burn up quietly at high altitudes or over unpopulated areas. When a meteor breaks apart at lower altitudes over populated regions, it becomes more noticeable to residents on the ground.

Weather monitoring systems and seismic equipment likely recorded the event. No injuries or property damage were reported from the meteor explosion.

The widespread nature of the sonic boom demonstrates the power of atmospheric friction on large space objects. As meteoroids descend through increasingly dense layers of air, they experience mounting pressure and heat. Eventually, for larger objects, this stress becomes too great and the meteor shatters explosively.

Any fragments that survived atmospheric entry would be scattered over a wide area, making them extremely difficult to locate. Most material burns up completely during the violent descent.

The event serves as a reminder that Earth is constantly bombarded by space debris. According to NASA, most space rocks smaller than a football field will break apart in the atmosphere. Though rare, some explode in the air rather than burning up silently.

Larger meteors capable of producing sonic booms audible over such extensive geographic areas occur regularly on a global scale. This event highlighted how visible and dramatic these cosmic encounters can be when they happen over populated regions during daylight hours when many people are outdoors or near windows.

Four-time Stanley Cup champion Claude Lemieux has died at 60, and his family has decided to donate his brain for chronic traumatic encephalopathy research.

Lemieux won Stanley Cup championships throughout his National Hockey League career. He played as a right winger known for his physical style of play.

The family announced their decision to donate Lemieux's brain to scientists studying CTE, a degenerative brain condition associated with repeated head trauma. The condition has been found in numerous former professional athletes, particularly in contact sports like hockey and football.

CTE can only be definitively diagnosed after death through examination of brain tissue. Researchers have been working to understand the condition's progression and its connection to symptoms including memory loss, confusion, depression, and changes in behavior.

Lemieux's brain donation will contribute to ongoing medical research into the long-term effects of head injuries sustained during professional sports careers. Scientists continue to study the brains of former athletes to better understand how repeated impacts affect brain health over time.

The hockey community has been mourning the loss of Lemieux, who built a reputation as a playoff performer during his years in the NHL. His four Stanley Cup victories came during a career that spanned multiple teams and decades.

Brain donation by former athletes and their families has become increasingly common as awareness of CTE has grown. These donations provide crucial data for researchers attempting to develop better diagnostic tools and potential treatments for the condition.

Lemieux's death and his family's decision to support CTE research highlight ongoing concerns about player safety in professional hockey and other contact sports. The NHL and other leagues have implemented various rule changes and protocols in recent years aimed at reducing head injuries, though the long-term effects of careers played before these changes remain a subject of medical study.
