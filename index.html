General Motors Pays $12.75 Million Over Illegal Sale of Driver Data

General Motors has agreed to pay $12.75 million to settle allegations that it sold hundreds of thousands of Californians' personal location and driving information to data brokers without their knowledge or consent. California Attorney General Rob Bonta announced the settlement on Friday, subject to court approval.

The agreement requires GM to stop selling consumer driving data to data brokers for the next five years and restricts how the company can use such information going forward. Bonta criticized the automaker's conduct, stating that "General Motors sold the data of California drivers without their knowledge or consent. This trove of information included precise and personal location data that could identify the everyday habits and movements of Californians."

According to investigators, between 2020 and 2024, GM sold names, contact information, geolocation data, and driving behavior information of hundreds of thousands of California residents to Verisk Analytics and LexisNexis Risk Solutions. The company collected this information through OnStar, its in-vehicle security subscription service. Authorities found that GM made approximately $20 million from these sales.

The data sharing raised serious privacy concerns. When precise vehicle locations are revealed, sensitive information can be inferred about where people live, work, attend school, or worship. Once data reaches brokers in the industry, consumers have limited ability to control how it spreads further.

San Francisco District Attorney Brooke Jenkins emphasized the responsibility automakers bear. "Modern cars are rolling data-collection machines," Jenkins said. "Californians must have confidence that they know what data is being collected, how it is being used and what their opt-out rights are. Those duties fall on the automobile companies."

The settlement also found that GM misled consumers by stating in its privacy policy that it would not sell driving or location data, then proceeded to do exactly that without obtaining consent.

California initiated its investigation into GM and other automakers in 2023 through its attorney general's office, working alongside district attorneys across the state and the California privacy protection agency. The inquiry was prompted in part by a New York Times investigation in 2024 that examined how carmakers share driving behavior data with insurance companies. That reporting found some insurers used the information to raise customer rates.

Bonta noted that California drivers will not face increased insurance premiums resulting from GM's data sales, as state law prohibits insurers from using driving data to set rates. However, the settlement holds GM accountable for the deceptive practices that allowed the sales to occur in the first place.

The case reflects growing scrutiny of automakers' data practices as vehicles become increasingly connected and data-rich. GM did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

Yesterday, 10:10 PM ET

A meteor broke apart in the atmosphere over northeastern Massachusetts and southeastern New Hampshire on Saturday afternoon, producing an explosion equivalent to 300 tons of TNT. NASA confirmed the event, which triggered loud booms heard across a wide region including Southern New England, with reports coming from as far away as Delaware and Montreal.

The fireball exploded just after 2pm local time at an altitude of 40 miles, according to NASA's deputy news chief Jennifer Dooren. The meteor was traveling at 75,000 miles per hour when it fragmented in the upper atmosphere.

The explosion sent shock waves across the region, causing homes to shake and prompting numerous eyewitness reports and video documentation of the event. Residents throughout the northeastern United States and into Canada reported hearing the loud booms and feeling the force of the blast.

NASA regularly tracks near-Earth objects and meteors as part of its planetary defense efforts. Most meteors disintegrate harmlessly high in the atmosphere without producing noticeable effects on the ground. However, the size and trajectory of this particular meteor allowed it to penetrate deeper into the atmosphere before breaking apart, creating shock waves strong enough to be felt by people across multiple states.

The explosion occurred at an altitude high enough to pose no direct threat to people or property below. Instead, the acoustic effects of the blast were the primary impact felt by residents across the region. According to NASA, most space rocks smaller than a football field will break apart in Earth's atmosphere, though on rare occasions they may explode in the air as happened in this case.

The fireball's path took it over densely populated areas of the northeastern United States, leading to widespread eyewitness accounts and video footage documenting the spectacular event. The visible light from the explosion and the accompanying booms drew attention from many observers across the affected region.

NASA confirmed that data analysis placed the initial visibility of the meteor above the area, with the explosion occurring as it moved through the lower portions of the upper atmosphere. The agency noted that while fragments may have been produced by the explosion, most of the meteor would have burned up during its passage through the atmosphere.

The event highlighted the ongoing activity of meteors entering Earth's atmosphere. According to research, thousands of meteorites fall to Earth annually, though most go unnoticed because they occur over unpopulated areas or break apart at high altitudes. The Massachusetts explosion was notable for its size, energy release, and occurrence over a region where many people could witness and report the event.

Coach Kenny Atkinson acknowledged New York's superior performance. "They're playing better basketball. You got to give them credit. They're on a heater. Yesterday, 8:31 PM ET

Jerome Powell has issued a stark warning about the Federal Reserve's credibility in his first public remarks since stepping down as chair. Speaking Sunday while accepting the John F. Kennedy Profiles in Courage award, Powell stated the central bank is undergoing a "stress test" with its credibility on the line.

Though Powell did not directly name the Trump administration, his comments clearly referenced recent efforts to pressure the Fed and undermine its independence from political control. These efforts include a criminal investigation into the Federal Reserve's over-budget building renovation project and an attempted removal of Fed governor Lisa Cook.

Powell framed threats to Fed independence as endangering an institution essential to American economic strength. The Federal Reserve's ability to function effectively depends on its statutory independence from direct political interference, allowing it to set monetary policy based on economic conditions rather than political pressure. This independence helps maintain public confidence in the institution's decisions regarding inflation and employment.

The tension between the Fed and the administration stems partly from disagreements over interest rate policy. The Trump administration has called for lower interest rates, while inflation currently stands at 3.8 percent, making rate cuts economically questionable. A White House spokesperson responded to recent Fed chair Kevin Warsh's Senate confirmation by saying his appointment represents "a welcome step towards finally restoring accountability, competence and confidence in Fed decision-making."

Warsh's confirmation on Wednesday marked a significant moment for the Fed's leadership. The Senate voted 54-45 along party lines to confirm him, with the exception of Democratic Senator John Fetterman of Pennsylvania. The vote was the most divisive Fed chair confirmation in history. Warsh, an Ivy League economist and former Wall Street banker, will officially assume the role on May 14, taking over from Powell.

During his confirmation hearing, Warsh committed to maintaining Fed independence and removing politics from monetary policy decisions. However, he declined to answer whether Trump won the 2020 election, raising concerns among Democrats who fear he may prioritize the administration's policy preferences over economic analysis.

Powell announced last month that he would remain on the Fed's board as a voting governor rather than fully retiring. His decision to stay depends on whether the White House ends its scrutiny of the Fed's headquarters renovation project. Powell characterized this scrutiny as "pretext" masking the administration's actual goal of pressuring the central bank toward lower interest rates.

Warsh must now navigate pressure from the Trump administration to lower rates while convincing other Fed board members to support such moves despite rising inflation. The central bank's independence from political control remains crucial for its effectiveness, and Powell's public warning suggests he views current threats to that independence as serious enough to warrant speaking out after leaving office.
