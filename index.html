Former first lady Jill Biden has defended the presidential pardon issued to her stepson, Hunter Biden, explaining that the decision became necessary after Donald Trump's election victory.

In a recent interview, Jill Biden stated that the circumstances surrounding the pardon changed once Trump won the presidency. "When [President] Trump was elected, things changed, and we knew that he would target Hunter, and we just could not let our son go to jail on a charge that no one would," she said.

The pardon, issued by former President Joe Biden before leaving office, covered federal charges that had been brought against Hunter Biden. According to Jill Biden's account, the family's decision to support the pardon was motivated by concerns about how Trump might use his position to pursue her stepson.

Jill Biden's comments suggest that the pardon was partly a protective measure against potential future prosecution. She emphasized that Hunter faced charges that were not typically prosecuted against others, implying the case against him was unusual or politically motivated.

The former first lady's remarks come as she prepares to release a new memoir titled "View from the East Wing," which documents her experiences during her time in the White House. The book is expected to include her perspective on her husband's 2024 presidential campaign and his decision to withdraw from the race.

Jill Biden has remained a steadfast supporter of her husband throughout his long political career. However, her recent comments about the pardon have drawn scrutiny from some observers. A former spokesperson for Jill Biden suggested that her current statements about events in the White House may have damaged her credibility, noting that she is now saying things "completely different" from what she said previously.

The pardon decision sparked considerable public debate when it was announced. While some viewed it as a father protecting his son from legal jeopardy, others criticized it as an abuse of presidential power. The former first lady's explanation that Trump would have targeted Hunter appears to be her fullest public justification for supporting the controversial pardon.

Jill Biden's upcoming memoir is likely to provide additional context about the pardon decision and other significant moments from the Biden presidency. Her willingness to discuss the pardon in interviews suggests she intends to address the controversy directly in the book, offering her perspective on one of the most debated decisions of the Biden administration's final days.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu ordered the Israeli military to attack the southern suburbs of Beirut, a stronghold of the Iran-backed militant group Hezbollah. The announcement prompted thousands of residents to flee the area after Israel issued evacuation warnings for Dahieh and other neighborhoods in the capital's outskirts.

The strikes represent a significant escalation in Israel's military campaign against Hezbollah in Lebanon. Netanyahu said the attacks were in response to Hezbollah assaults on Israeli civilians. The expansion of military operations into Beirut's suburbs marks a new phase in the conflict, bringing the fighting directly to Lebanon's capital city for the first time in this campaign.

Israel issued sweeping displacement orders affecting nearly a million people. The military instructed residents up to 25 miles from the border to head north of the Zahrani River, covering major cities including Nabatieh and dozens of villages. An evacuation order was also issued for a neighborhood in central Beirut near restaurants, where the Israeli military said it would strike a building.

The escalation followed Hezbollah's most intense attacks yet on Israel. The group, in coordination with Iran for the first time, launched successive volleys of rockets and drone swarms in what it called "Operation Chewed Wheat." Hezbollah fired more than 200 rockets toward Israeli territory, including at military bases in Haifa, Tel Aviv and Beersheba. Most projectiles were intercepted or fell into open areas, injuring two people.

Israeli warplanes responded immediately with intense bombardment of Beirut's southern suburbs and southern Lebanon. The Lebanese health ministry said 70 people were killed in Israeli strikes on Thursday. Among the dead were two academics, Hussein Bazzi and Murtadha Sarour, killed in an attack on the Lebanese University campus. At least 17 people were injured in strikes on Beirut's southern suburbs, with more casualties expected.

Israel struck two buildings in central Beirut, leveling them after warning residents to evacuate. One building in the Zouq Blat neighborhood sat next to a shelter hosting displaced people, causing large crowds to gather in central Beirut's main square seeking shelter. The other building was located near the prime minister's office, UN agencies and foreign embassies.

In Israeli border communities, residents described a harrowing night. "Last night was a bit crazy," said Daniel Dorfman, a 43-year-old from Metula near the Lebanese border. "I don't get anxious usually but all the walls were shaking."

The exchange marked the most severe escalation in the 10-day conflict between Israel and Hezbollah. Human rights groups condemned the displacement orders as forced displacement that could amount to war crimes. They said Israeli military should still attempt to prevent civilian harm, even if civilians did not evacuate.

The escalation complicates diplomatic efforts to contain the wider regional conflict involving Iran and the United States.

Iran has suspended negotiations with the United States and threatened to completely block the Strait of Hormuz, according to state media reports. The announcement marks a significant collapse in diplomatic efforts that had been conducted through intermediaries in recent weeks.

The breakdown in talks comes after a tumultuous period of mixed signals and escalating military confrontations. For weeks, the US and Iran had maintained a ceasefire that began in early April, though sporadic exchanges of fire continued throughout May. Negotiations aimed at a more durable agreement had stalled repeatedly as both sides demanded concessions.

The Trump administration had been pushing hard for a deal. Trump requested changes to a proposed agreement, citing issues related to the Strait of Hormuz and the removal of highly enriched uranium. On one occasion, Trump announced that "great progress" had been made toward a complete agreement and paused a military operation called Project Freedom to allow space for peace negotiations. However, hours later he threatened that if Iran did not agree to terms, "the bombing starts, and it will be, sadly, at a much higher level and intensity than it was before."

According to reporting, Washington and Tehran had been close to agreeing on a one-page memorandum of understanding to end the war, with the US expecting Iran to respond to key points within 48 hours. However, those efforts ultimately failed to produce results.

The military situation remained volatile throughout negotiations. The US military said it destroyed Iranian small boats and intercepted incoming cruise missiles and drones. Iran denied these claims but acknowledged firing at commercial vessels and seizing two container ships since the ceasefire began. The Pentagon stated that Iran's attacks had fallen below the threshold of restarting major combat operations.

The Strait of Hormuz serves as a critical waterway through which a substantial portion of global oil shipments pass. Iran's threat to close the strait represents a major escalation in tensions between the two nations. Oil prices have fluctuated dramatically during the conflict, rising to $114 per barrel at one point and falling to around $97 as negotiations appeared to progress.

Trump faced pressure from declining poll numbers and appeals from Gulf allies to resolve the conflict before it caused further damage to the global economy. However, commentators noted that the self-proclaimed master dealmaker appeared to be sabotaging his own negotiations. Iran, strengthened by its military performance during the war, had become better positioned to demand concessions than before the conflict began.

The suspension of talks leaves the path forward uncertain. Both countries continue military operations, and Iran's decision to walk away from negotiations suggests the conflict may intensify further before any diplomatic resolution can be reached.

10:16 AM ET

Oil prices rose sharply following escalating military strikes between Israel and Iran in the Middle East, with crude climbing more than 3 percent as energy markets reacted to heightened geopolitical tensions and threats to critical shipping routes.

The escalation centered on attacks against energy infrastructure. Israel targeted Iran's South Pars gasfield, prompting Iranian retaliation against facilities in Qatar. QatarEnergy reported that Iranian strikes caused "extensive damage" to Ras Laffan, the world's largest liquefied natural gas facility, damaging equipment responsible for 17 percent of the company's LNG export capacity. The company estimated repairs would take three to five years.

Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, initially spiked 10 percent to $119 a barrel before settling at $110, representing a 3.3 percent gain. Crude prices have surged 60 percent since the conflict began on February 28. European gas prices jumped dramatically, with Dutch wholesale gas rising 24 percent to 68 euros per megawatt hour, its highest level since December 2022. UK gas prices more than doubled since late February, raising concerns about household bills.

The Strait of Hormuz remains a critical chokepoint for global energy supplies. Any prolonged disruption could significantly affect worldwide oil supplies and prices. Richard Meade, editor-in-chief of Lloyd's List Intelligence, warned that the first confirmed strike on an operational gasfield marked a significant escalation, expanding risks to the entire Middle East Gulf energy and logistics system beyond just the strait.

Stock markets reflected the uncertainty worldwide. Japan's Nikkei tumbled 3.4 percent, South Korea's Kospi fell 2.7 percent, and Hong Kong's Hang Seng dropped 2 percent. European markets followed suit, with the UK's FTSE 100 declining 2.35 percent. Wall Street opened lower as investors weighed rising oil prices against recent technology stock gains.

Energy analysts warned of broader economic consequences. Susannah Streeter, chief investment strategist at Wealth Club, said fears of a sustained energy shock have resurfaced, noting that the prospect of prolonged conflict threatens economies across the region and worldwide through higher energy prices.

Thomas Pugh, chief economist at consulting firm RSM UK, cautioned that elevated energy prices could trigger inflationary pressures, potentially pushing inflation toward 5 percent if prices remained high through summer. Such increases could prompt interest rate hikes from the Bank of England, with money markets now fully pricing in a quarter-point rise by July.

Major European airlines, including Lufthansa, announced that ticket prices would rise if fuel cost increases persisted for months. The energy consultancy Wood Mackenzie said the attacks had fundamentally altered the global gas market outlook, as disruption at the Qatari facility could exceed initial two-month expectations, with each additional month removing about 1.5 percent from annual global LNG availability.

9:51 AM ET

Nvidia has launched a new "superchip" designed to bring artificial intelligence capabilities directly to Windows laptops and desktop computers, marking the company's entry into the consumer PC processor market. The move intensifies competition with Intel, Apple, Qualcomm, and AMD.

The RTX Spark chip combines a microprocessor with a graphics processor and was developed in collaboration with Taiwan's MediaTek. It is designed to run AI agents locally on computers rather than relying on cloud computing servers. According to Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang, the technology will allow AI agents to navigate PCs autonomously, potentially replacing traditional mouse and keyboard interactions.

Major computer manufacturers including Dell, Lenovo, Asus, and HP will integrate the RTX Spark chip into their devices, paired with Microsoft's Windows software. Microsoft has already unveiled the Surface Laptop Ultra as the first device featuring the technology. Huang announced the development at the Computex conference in Taiwan, following three years of collaboration between Nvidia and Microsoft.

Huang described the chip as "reimagining the PC for the first time in 40 years." Despite the chip's power, Nvidia stated that computers using it will remain thin and light. The unified memory design allows the processor and graphics chip to share data more efficiently than traditional laptop architectures.

The RTX Spark represents Nvidia's expansion beyond its dominant position in data center AI processors. The company is also developing the Vera central processing unit for AI agents, with early adopters including OpenAI, Anthropic, and SpaceX.

Industry analysts view the move as strategically significant but a longer-term growth opportunity. Neil Shah of Counterpoint Research compared the RTX Spark's potential impact to major technological shifts like the iPhone and ChatGPT, suggesting it could transform PCs into "agentic AI personal computers."

Nvidia's push into consumer PCs comes as the industry attempts to revive PC sales by promoting AI features as a reason for customers to upgrade older machines. However, analysts note this business line will take time to generate substantial revenue, with Nvidia's current earnings still overwhelmingly dependent on demand for AI infrastructure and data center computing.

The launch has prompted competitive responses. Intel announced it will ship an AI chip later this year using cheaper memory and cooling technology than Nvidia and AMD competitors. Intel's new graphics processing unit, codenamed Crescent Island, is "purpose-built for this upcoming AI generation of agents," according to Anil Nanduri, vice-president of AI products at Intel's Data Center Group.

The RTX Spark is scheduled for release this year, signaling the start of a new competitive battleground in the semiconductor industry as major tech companies race to dominate the emerging market for AI-capable personal computers.

Williams has been cleared to return after rejoining the professional tennis drug testing pool six months ago. She will compete for a wildcard spot in the doubles draw alongside Victoria Mboko, a 19-year-old Canadian ranked number 9 in singles. The Queen's Club tournament is a WTA 500 event beginning on June 8, just one day after the French Open concludes.

The 23-time Grand Slam singles champion has been preparing for this return while training in Florida. She has been working with Alycia Parks, currently ranked number 79, and posted a video of them practicing together in March. While Williams did not formally announce her retirement in 2022, she appeared on the retired players' list until her recent return to the drug testing pool.

The announcement has generated excitement throughout the tennis world. Fellow competitors have expressed enthusiasm about her return. Naomi Osaka, who recently encountered Williams at the Met Gala this month, said: "I think it's good for me. I'll be very entertained. But overall the scope of it, I think it will bring people to watch tennis, which she always does bring an audience with her. I'm going to be tuned into the first match, for sure. Everyone knows Serena and [her sister] Venus were my role models growing up, so it's going to be cool to see her on the grounds again."

Madison Keys, ranked number 19, added: "Serena Williams playing tennis is only good for tennis. Let's be real, we all want to watch Serena. I am excited. She's the GOAT so it would be amazing if she came back. You get to watch history every single time she takes the court, so why not watch more?"

The WTA has celebrated Williams' return to the professional stage, recognizing her significant impact on the sport over more than two decades at elite level. Queen's Club represents a strategic choice for her comeback, as the grass court tournament traditionally precedes Wimbledon and allows players to prepare for the summer grass court season.

Williams has not disclosed her long-term competitive plans beyond the Queen's Club event. Her decision to return through doubles rather than singles suggests a cautious approach to assessing her physical condition and competitive readiness. The Queen's appearance will provide the first indication of whether this represents a brief return to the sport or the beginning of a more sustained comeback.
