The US Supreme Court ruled Thursday in favor of Terry Pitchford, a Black man on death row in Mississippi who challenged his capital murder conviction based on racial discrimination during jury selection. The justices voted 5-4 in his favor.

Pitchford was 18 years old when he and another teenager robbed a grocery store in 2004. The other teen, who fired the fatal shots, was a minor and therefore ineligible for the death penalty. Pitchford, now 40, was convicted of capital murder and sentenced to death.

The central issue in the Supreme Court case involved jury selection. Prosecutors removed four of the five Black jurors from the pool, resulting in a jury of 11 white jurors and one Black juror that convicted Pitchford and imposed his death sentence. The prosecutor, Doug Evans, who had a documented history of dismissing Black jurors for discriminatory reasons, made the strikes.

Pitchford's attorney objected to the strikes during trial, but Judge Joseph Loper allowed them to stand. In his majority opinion, Justice Brett Kavanaugh wrote that "the trial court did not afford Pitchford's counsel a sufficient opportunity to rebut the prosecutor's proffered race-neutral reasons for striking the four Black jurors and never determined whether the prosecutor's stated reasons were pretextual."

The Supreme Court's decision reinstated a federal judge's earlier ruling that had invalidated Pitchford's conviction. That federal ruling found his attorney was prevented from questioning whether Evans's jury selection decisions were based on race.

The case centered on application of the Batson challenge, a legal framework established by the Supreme Court in 1986 through Batson v. Kentucky. The Batson challenge creates a three-step process for addressing potential racial discrimination in jury selection. First, the objecting party must demonstrate an inference of discrimination. Second, the striking party must provide race-neutral explanations for removing specific jurors. Third, the judge must determine whether purposeful discrimination occurred.

Much of the oral arguments focused on Judge Loper's handling of the third step of this process. The case parallels a similar situation from seven years ago involving the same judge and prosecutor. In that case, the Supreme Court overturned the death sentence and conviction of Curtis Flowers, another Black man who had been tried six times over more than 20 years. At that time, Justice Kavanaugh wrote that Evans showed a "relentless, determined effort to rid the jury of Black individuals."

The Supreme Court's decision provides Pitchford with a pathway to challenge his conviction. Prosecutors may seek to retry him, but the ruling reinforces constitutional protections designed to prevent racial bias in jury selection and ensure fair trials in capital cases.
