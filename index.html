The United States and Iran have escalated military hostilities during an ongoing ceasefire, with each side conducting strikes and accusing the other of violations. The exchange threatens to derail peace negotiations aimed at ending the three-month conflict.

U.S. Central Command forces shot down four Iranian attack drones near the Strait of Hormuz and struck a ground control station in Iran's Bandar Abbas that was preparing to launch a fifth drone. American officials described the actions as "measured, purely defensive, and intended to maintain the ceasefire."

Within two hours, Iran's Revolutionary Guards responded by targeting an American base in Kuwait, claiming it was the source of the U.S. strikes. Kuwait's military confirmed it intercepted hostile missile and drone threats early Thursday morning. Iran also reported that its forces fired at four vessels attempting to cross the Strait of Hormuz without coordination with security forces.

The tit-for-tat strikes underscored the fragility of the ceasefire announced in April and exposed the significant distance between the two nations in peace talks. President Trump said he faces no pressure to rush a deal with Iran, stating that Tehran wants to make an agreement but "hasn't gotten there." He claimed Iran was "negotiating on fumes" and refused to accelerate the diplomatic timeline in response to renewed fighting.

At a White House cabinet meeting, Trump also directed warnings at regional players, saying the Strait of Hormuz would remain "open to everybody" and threatening consequences for Oman if it did not comply with U.S. expectations regarding control of the waterway. The White House called Iranian claims about a draft agreement involving Iran and Oman managing strait traffic a "complete fabrication."

One major unresolved issue in negotiations is whether a U.S.-Iran ceasefire will extend to Israeli operations against Hezbollah in Lebanon. Iran has insisted that Lebanon must be included in any ceasefire agreement with the United States. Meanwhile, Israeli forces have intensified operations in Lebanon, striking over 550 targets since the beginning of the week and pushing farther north against Hezbollah fighters.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said his military is "deepening its operation" in Lebanon. The fighting has continued despite the U.S.-brokered ceasefire announced in April, which now appears close to collapse. More than 1.2 million Lebanese have been displaced by Israeli strikes and evacuation orders since March 2, when Hezbollah began firing at Israel in support of Iran. Israeli strikes have killed more than 3,200 people in Lebanon, according to the health ministry.

Oil prices jumped 2 percent following Iran's announcement that it had targeted the American base, reflecting market concerns about potential supply disruptions in a region crucial to global petroleum exports.

A few minutes ago

Core inflation climbed to an annual rate of 3.3% in April, matching what economists had predicted and marking the highest reading since May 2023. The Federal Reserve released the data using its preferred inflation gauge.

The spike reflects the impact of the ongoing conflict with Iran, which has disrupted energy markets and driven up commodity prices globally. Energy costs have been particularly affected, with oil supply concerns from the region contributing to the broader price increases.

This marks the first inflation report released under newly appointed Federal Reserve chair Kevin Warsh. The data underscores that prices remain elevated compared to the Fed's target, as war-related pressures continue to affect the economy.

The April result represents a setback after months of declining inflation. Prices had been falling from their 2023 peak before the conflict began disrupting global supply chains and energy markets. The war has essentially reversed recent progress toward price stability.

Federal Reserve officials have signaled they are prepared to use interest rate increases to combat the escalating price pressures. Raising interest rates typically slows economic activity by making borrowing more expensive for both consumers and businesses, which can help cool inflation.

The conflict has created what economists describe as a price shock. Energy costs have surged particularly sharply, reflecting concerns about oil supply disruptions from the region. This energy-driven inflation has pushed the overall reading to its highest level in nearly three years.

Economists had anticipated the 3.3% reading based on price increases observed throughout April related to the war. The confirmation of sustained inflation adds urgency to the Federal Reserve's decision-making at upcoming policy meetings.

The elevated inflation complicates the central bank's efforts to maintain economic stability while supporting growth. The Fed faces pressure to act decisively without triggering unintended consequences for employment and business investment.

Looking ahead, much depends on how the conflict develops. If the war continues, inflation pressures are likely to remain elevated. However, if supply chain disruptions ease in coming months and energy markets stabilize, inflation could moderate.

Analysts note that even a peace deal may not immediately bring relief to consumers. According to energy market experts, US fuel prices are unlikely to return to prewar levels soon, even if the conflict ends quickly. Gas prices that averaged about three dollars per gallon before the war are expected to remain elevated through 2026.

In the UK, the war's impact is also visible in household energy bills, with typical consumers facing annual increases of 221 pounds under the regulator's new price cap. Supply chain experts have warned that Britain's critical infrastructure remains unprepared for major shocks such as war, adding to broader economic concerns.

Valve has increased prices for its Steam Deck handheld gaming device by more than 40 percent, with the company attributing the hikes to the current state of component costs. The price adjustments represent increases of up to $300 depending on the model.

The gaming giant emphasized that the handheld device itself has not changed, only the pricing structure reflects higher manufacturing expenses. The company did not specify which particular components drove the cost increases or provide details about whether the pricing changes are temporary.

The Steam Deck had been out of stock for months before returning to availability at the new higher prices. The device, which launched in 2022, allows users to play PC games on a portable handheld system running a Linux-based operating system with access to games from the Steam digital distribution platform.

Valve offers multiple versions of the hardware at different price points based on storage capacity and screen type. The price increases apply to units currently available for purchase. However, customers who ordered devices before the price changes will receive their units at the previously advertised prices.

The move comes as handheld gaming devices face mounting pressure from supply chain economics. The Steam Deck competes in a growing market of portable gaming systems that includes offerings from manufacturers such as Asus and Lenovo.

Valve has not announced plans to release a new version of the hardware that might offset the higher costs through updated components or manufacturing processes. The company also provided no timeline for how long the increased prices would remain in effect or whether future changes in component costs might allow for price reductions.

The Steam Deck remains available through Valve's official channels at the new pricing. The company has not issued further statements explaining which specific cost pressures necessitated the substantial price increases or provided consumers with clarity about the potential duration of these changes.

The price hikes represent a significant shift for the handheld device, which had established itself in the portable gaming market since its launch three years ago. Industry observers continue to monitor whether other handheld gaming manufacturers will follow suit with their own price adjustments.

CNN filed a lawsuit against artificial intelligence company Perplexity, accusing the firm of widespread copyright and trademark violations. The suit was filed in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York.

The news network alleges Perplexity scraped more than 17,000 CNN stories, photographs, videos and other content without authorization. CNN claims the AI company copied and distributed its material without consent.

The lawsuit centers on allegations that Perplexity used CNN's copyrighted content to build and operate its services. CNN says the company violated intellectual property protections by reproducing and disseminating the network's journalism and branded materials.

The case adds to growing legal battles between traditional media companies and AI firms over the use of copyrighted content. News organizations have increasingly challenged tech companies that use their reporting to train AI systems or generate responses without licensing agreements.

Perplexity operates an AI-powered search and answer engine that provides information to users. The service competes with traditional search engines by generating direct answers to queries rather than simply listing links to source material.

CNN's complaint accuses Perplexity of trademark infringement in addition to copyright violations. The suit suggests the AI company used CNN's brand identity and reputation without permission while distributing the network's content.

The lawsuit seeks to stop Perplexity from continuing to use CNN's material and demands compensation for the alleged infringement. The case represents one of the most significant legal challenges yet from a major news organization against an AI company over content usage.

Neither CNN nor Perplexity immediately provided details about the specific content at issue or the damages being sought. The court filing marks an escalation in tensions between legacy media outlets and emerging AI platforms over how journalistic content can be used in the development and operation of artificial intelligence services.
