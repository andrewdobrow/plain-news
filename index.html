Lemieux's playing style was often controversial. He was involved in several notable playoff incidents that generated strong reactions from opponents. While his aggressive approach sometimes created animosity with opposing players, his teammates appreciated his commitment to winning.

After his playing career ended, Lemieux remained involved in professional hockey. He worked in player development and coaching roles at various levels of the sport. He was also the father of Brendan Lemieux, who played in the NHL for multiple teams including the Winnipeg Jets and New York Rangers.

Lemieux also worked as a player agent, continuing his involvement with hockey after leaving the game as a competitor.

The hockey community responded to his death with numerous tributes. Former teammates and opponents acknowledged both his exceptional skill and his competitive intensity. Many highlighted how Lemieux consistently elevated his performance when stakes were highest, establishing himself as one of the most productive playoff performers in hockey history.

The Trump administration confirmed this week that Americans exposed to Ebola will not be permitted to return to the United States. Instead, the government is constructing a 50-bed quarantine and treatment facility in Kenya that became operational Friday.

The White House said the facility was "designed to provide access to high-quality care for Americans who would need to quickly get out of DRC and quarantine without the risks of a lengthy transport back to the US," according to a statement from a White House official.

The Kenya center will handle both quarantine for exposed individuals and treatment for those who contract the virus. The official stated the facility would provide "critical care needs, though each case will be evaluated for forward transport for more advanced care as appropriate in order to maximize patient outcomes." The official did not specify whether additional treatment would occur in the United States or Europe, where some Americans have previously received care.

The administration also did not clarify whether Americans could choose to return to the U.S. instead of going to Kenya.

The policy has drawn criticism from public health experts. Jennifer Nuzzo, an epidemiologist and director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University School of Public Health, said the approach raises serious concerns.

"It is shocking to me that the administration is looking to prevent Americans from coming home to receive the proven world-class care that our taxpayer-funded biocontainment and treatment units are equipped to provide," Nuzzo said. "There are profound ethical concerns with this approach."

She worried that the policy could discourage people from reporting exposures. "Without adequate plans for the safe quarantine of exposed individuals and prompt isolation of those who become infected, I fear these facilities could amplify the spread of the virus. The prospect of not being able to come home for safe and effective care may create disincentives for people to disclose having been exposed, which could drive cases underground, and cause the virus to spread even more."

Jeremy Konyndyk, president of Refugees International and former leader of USAID's Ebola response, expressed similar concerns about recruitment. "That's basically telling any American health worker who might go and work on the effort to contain this outbreak that if they get sick, they can't come home," he said. "It disincentivizes people from going."

Konyndyk noted that during the 2014-2015 outbreak, officials avoided travel restrictions specifically because they understood such measures would undermine containment efforts.

The U.S. has also implemented broader travel restrictions related to the outbreak, banning green card holders who recently traveled to the Democratic Republic of Congo, Uganda, and South Sudan from returning home. Other recent travelers to these countries are also barred from entering the U.S.

Experts emphasize that transportation risks are minimal for people without symptoms, and the U.S. has extensive experience evacuating exposed individuals.

His reputation as a fierce competitor made him one of the most intimidating players during the postseason.

In 1995, Lemieux won the Conn Smythe Trophy as playoff MVP when the New Jersey Devils won their first Stanley Cup. That year, he scored 13 goals during the postseason, helping the Devils defeat the heavily favored Detroit Red Wings in the finals.

Lemieux's playing style was often controversial. He was involved in several notable playoff incidents that generated strong reactions from opponents. One such incident was a hit on Detroit's Kris Draper in 1996, which sparked a prolonged rivalry between the Colorado Avalanche and Detroit Red Wings. While his aggressive approach sometimes created animosity with opposing players, his teammates appreciated his commitment to winning.

After his playing career ended, Lemieux remained involved in professional hockey. He worked in player development and coaching roles at various levels of the sport. He was also the father of Brendan Lemieux, who played in the NHL for multiple teams including the Winnipeg Jets and New York Rangers.

Lemieux also worked as a player agent, continuing his involvement with hockey after leaving the game as a competitor.

The hockey community responded to his death with numerous tributes. Former teammates and opponents acknowledged both his exceptional skill and his competitive intensity. Many highlighted how Lemieux consistently elevated his performance when stakes were highest, establishing himself as one of the most productive playoff performers in hockey history.

His four Stanley Cup championships placed him among an elite group of players who won multiple titles with different organizations. His legacy in professional hockey remains defined by his fierce determination and postseason success across his long career in the sport.

The Trump administration confirmed this week that Americans exposed to Ebola will not be permitted to return to the United States. Instead, the government is constructing a 50-bed quarantine and treatment facility in Kenya that became operational Friday.

The White House said the facility was "designed to provide access to high-quality care for Americans who would need to quickly get out of DRC and quarantine without the risks of a lengthy transport back to the US," according to a statement from a White House official.

The Kenya center will handle both quarantine for exposed individuals and treatment for those who contract the virus. The official stated the facility would provide "critical care needs, though each case will be evaluated for forward transport for more advanced care as appropriate in order to maximize patient outcomes." The official did not specify whether additional treatment would occur in the United States or Europe, where some Americans have previously received care.

The administration also did not clarify whether Americans could choose to return to the U.S. instead of going to Kenya.

The policy has drawn criticism from public health experts. Jennifer Nuzzo, an epidemiologist and director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University School of Public Health, said the approach raises serious concerns.

"It is shocking to me that the administration is looking to prevent Americans from coming home to receive the proven world-class care that our taxpayer-funded biocontainment and treatment units are equipped to provide," Nuzzo said. "There are profound ethical concerns with this approach."

She worried that the policy could discourage people from reporting exposures. "Without adequate plans for the safe quarantine of exposed individuals and prompt isolation of those who become infected, I fear these facilities could amplify the spread of the virus. The prospect of not being able to come home for safe and effective care may create disincentives for people to disclose having been exposed, which could drive cases underground, and cause the virus to spread even more."

Jeremy Konyndyk, president of Refugees International and former leader of USAID's Ebola response, expressed similar concerns about recruitment. "That's basically telling any American health worker who might go and work on the effort to contain this outbreak that if they get sick, they can't come home," he said. "It disincentivizes people from going."

Konyndyk noted that during the 2014-2015 outbreak, officials avoided travel restrictions specifically because they understood such measures would undermine containment efforts.

The U.S. has also implemented broader travel restrictions related to the outbreak, banning green card holders who recently traveled to the Democratic Republic of Congo, Uganda, and South Sudan from returning home. Other recent travelers to these countries are also barred from entering the U.S.

Experts emphasize that transportation risks are minimal for people without symptoms, and the U.S. has extensive experience evacuating exposed individuals.
