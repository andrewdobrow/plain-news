12:10 PM ET

The Securities and Exchange Commission proposed eliminating a regulation that would have required publicly traded companies to disclose climate change risks. The rule targeted whether companies faced significant risks from climate change and its effects.

The proposal marks a reversal for the agency on corporate climate transparency. The original regulation aimed to standardize how public companies report climate-related financial risks to investors.

The disclosure requirement would have applied to all publicly traded companies in the United States. Under the rule, firms would have needed to report whether climate change posed material risks to their operations, supply chains, or long-term business prospects.

The SEC has not specified when a final decision on the proposal will be made. The agency typically allows a public comment period before finalizing regulatory changes.

Environmental advocates had supported the disclosure rule as a way to give investors better information about climate risks. Business groups raised concerns about the compliance costs and feasibility of accurately quantifying climate-related risks.

The proposal comes amid broader debates about corporate environmental reporting standards. Several states and foreign jurisdictions have implemented their own climate disclosure requirements for large companies operating within their borders.

Investors have increasingly pressed companies to provide more detailed information about how climate change might affect their business models. Major institutional investors have argued that climate risks represent material financial information that shareholders need to make informed decisions.

The outcome of the SEC proposal could influence how American companies approach climate risk reporting. Even without a federal mandate, some large corporations have voluntarily disclosed climate-related risks in their annual reports and sustainability statements.

A meteor exploded over the northeastern United States on Saturday afternoon, producing a powerful sonic boom that residents reported hearing across a wide geographic area. NASA confirmed the meteor caused the explosion and indicated that meteorite fragments may have fallen near Cape Cod Bay.

The fireball streaked across the sky at around 2:06 pm ET, visible to multiple observers who captured it on camera. The explosion was so significant that it shook houses in the region and appeared clearly in satellite imagery, lighting up the sky during daylight hours. The incident generated reports from locations stretching across multiple states and into Canada, including areas as far south as Delaware and as far north as Montreal.

Residents in the affected region initially puzzled over the loud booms, with many uncertain about the source of the disturbance. Some thought the shaking and noise might have come from other causes before NASA identified the meteor as responsible. Reports poured in from across the northeastern corridor, indicating the meteor's trajectory covered a substantial distance before fragmenting.

According to NASA's analysis, data placed the initial visibility of the fireball above Lake Erie. The meteor was a small asteroid measuring nearly 6 feet in diameter and weighing about 7 tons. It traveled at approximately 45,000 miles per hour before fragmenting over the region north of Cape Cod Bay.

When meteors enter Earth's atmosphere at high speeds, they typically burn up due to friction with air molecules. However, larger objects can survive long enough to create visible and audible events before breaking apart. The sonic boom occurs when a meteor travels faster than the speed of sound, generating pressure waves that produce the characteristic loud noise heard on the ground.

NASA's meteoroid environment office noted that most space rocks smaller than a football field will break apart in the atmosphere, though explosions in the air occur only on rare occasions. While meteor sightings are relatively common, events that produce sonic booms loud enough to be heard across such a wide area are less frequent.

The widespread nature of the reports suggested the meteor was traveling at significant speed when it entered the atmosphere. Residents across the affected region took to social media to share their experiences and discuss the unusual sounds. NASA's confirmation provided clarity for those who had heard the unexplained noise.

The potential presence of meteorite fragments in the vicinity of Cape Cod Bay could provide scientists with material for further study. According to NASA data, about 17,000 meteorites fall to Earth annually, though most remain undiscovered.
