1:21 PM ET

More than 245 suspected cases of Ebola have been reported in the Democratic Republic of Congo's Ituri province, with confirmed deaths reaching at least 65 people. The outbreak has prompted urgent international attention as health officials and aid organizations mobilize to contain the spread of this rare and dangerous virus.

The World Health Organization chief has arrived in Congo's capital to support containment efforts. The WHO estimates the death rate from this outbreak at between 30 to 50 percent among confirmed cases. "It's huge. It means that up to five out of 10 people are likely to die," said Anaïs Legand from the WHO's high threat pathogens team.

Médecins Sans Frontières has raised alarm about the outbreak's rapid progression. The charity warned that no previous Ebola outbreak has recorded so many cases so soon after being declared, highlighting the unusual speed at which this epidemic is spreading compared to past incidents.

The outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo strain of Ebola, a particularly concerning development. The Democratic Republic of Congo's national research laboratory detected the virus in 13 of 20 samples tested. Unlike the Zaire strain of Ebola, for which vaccines exist, there is currently no licensed vaccine for the Bundibugyo variant. Only two previous outbreaks of this strain have occurred, in 2007 and 2012.

Cases have been reported mainly in the Mongwalu and Rwampara health zones, with suspected cases also appearing in the neighboring city of Bunia. The Ituri province, which borders Uganda and South Sudan, is home to mining towns with significant population movement, creating challenges for disease control efforts. Uganda's health ministry has confirmed an outbreak in the country after a 59-year-old man died in a Kampala hospital following travel from the DRC.

The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention has expressed concern about further spread across the region. The organization has called an urgent meeting with authorities from the DRC, Uganda, and South Sudan, along with the WHO and pharmaceutical companies, to coordinate response efforts.

Health workers in the region face particular challenges as they treat patients with limited medical equipment and protective resources. The Democratic Republic of Congo has experienced 16 Ebola outbreaks since the virus was first identified there in 1976. Experts point to multiple factors contributing to recurring outbreaks, including close contact with animal reservoirs such as bats and primates, movement between rural and urban areas, and the region's tropical climate and rainforest environment.

Ebola spreads through direct contact with body fluids including blood and vomit from infected people, as well as during funeral preparation rituals. The disease causes severe illness with high fatality rates.

A New Glenn rocket owned by Blue Origin exploded during a test at Cape Canaveral in Florida on Thursday night, destroying the launchpad and creating a massive fireball visible across the region. The explosion occurred at 9pm ET during a scheduled "hotfire" test at Launch Complex 36, with an orange sky visible as far south as Fort Pierce, 115 miles away.

The incident poses a significant threat to NASA's lunar exploration timeline. Blue Origin had recently won a contract to launch the first of three planned missions this year to begin construction of NASA's $20 billion moon base. The explosion raises questions about whether the company can meet these commitments.

Blue Origin founder Jeff Bezos confirmed that all employees were safe and uninjured. He described the day as "very rough" but committed to rebuilding and resuming operations. The company called the incident "an anomaly" and said it was already investigating the root cause.

NASA administrator Jared Isaacman acknowledged the setback in a post on X, stating that spaceflight is "unforgiving" and that developing new heavy-lift launch capability is "extraordinarily difficult." He announced that NASA would conduct a full evaluation of mission timelines following the explosion and work with partners to investigate the incident and assess its impact on the Artemis and Moon Base programs.

The shockwaves from the explosion were felt across Florida's space coast, with residents in nearby Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach reporting that their homes shook. People in South Carolina, hundreds of miles north, reported seeing a glow in the sky. Flames continued burning at the launchpad more than two hours after the explosion, though emergency officials said there was no threat from fumes or other hazards.

This explosion represents the latest in a series of setbacks for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA contracts. Last month, a payload from New Glenn's third flight ended up in the wrong orbit, leading the Federal Aviation Administration to temporarily ground the rocket. The FAA cleared it to return to flight last week, making Thursday's test the first static fire test since that clearance.

Blue Origin is competing with SpaceX to provide lunar landers for NASA's Artemis program. Artemis III, scheduled for 2027, will test Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander alongside SpaceX's Starship Human Landing System to determine which will carry astronauts to the lunar surface in Artemis IV, planned for 2028. This would mark the first crewed lunar landing since 1972.

Elon Musk, owner of SpaceX and X, commented briefly on the explosion, saying "Most unfortunate" and noting that "Rockets are hard."

11:19 AM ET

Microsoft has warned a security researcher with potential legal action after publicly releasing multiple zero-day vulnerabilities without first coordinating with the company. The researcher, known online as Nightmare Eclipse, disclosed six zero-day exploits publicly, along with proof-of-concept code.

The confrontation has reignited debate within the cybersecurity community about responsible vulnerability disclosure. Microsoft contacted the researcher and raised the possibility of a criminal investigation in response to what the company characterized as uncoordinated disclosure of security vulnerabilities. Some posts from the researcher suggest they may be a disgruntled former employee, though this remains unclear.

Microsoft typically prefers that security researchers report vulnerabilities privately through coordinated disclosure processes. This approach gives the company time to develop and distribute patches before vulnerability information becomes public, protecting users from potential attacks. The researcher, however, has described feeling humiliated by Microsoft's response and has pledged what they called a "bone shattering drop," suggesting additional vulnerability releases may be forthcoming.

Zero-day vulnerabilities are security flaws that software makers do not yet know about and for which no patch exists. Public disclosure of such vulnerabilities before fixes are available can leave users exposed to exploitation. The timing and nature of disclosure has long been contentious among researchers, companies, and security experts.

Microsoft has established formal channels for security researchers to report vulnerabilities responsibly, including a bug bounty program offering financial rewards. The company argues that coordinated disclosure protects users by allowing adequate time to develop, test, and distribute security updates before attackers can exploit newly revealed flaws.

The dispute highlights ongoing tensions in cybersecurity over disclosure practices and ethics. Some researchers contend that public disclosure serves the public interest by forcing companies to address security issues more quickly. Companies generally maintain that uncoordinated disclosure puts users at risk and can violate computer fraud laws in certain jurisdictions.

Security researcher Kevin Beaumont drew attention to how Microsoft has handled this particular situation, sparking broader criticism of the company's response. The incident has generated coverage across major technology and cybersecurity publications, with commentators questioning whether Microsoft's legal threats represent an appropriate response to uncoordinated vulnerability disclosure.

The specific technical details of the six disclosed vulnerabilities have not been detailed in available reporting. Microsoft has not publicly confirmed whether patches for these issues have been developed or distributed. The company also has not released statements regarding the severity of the vulnerabilities or their potential impact on users.

This confrontation underscores the complex relationship between technology companies and independent security researchers, where questions about disclosure timing, legal responsibility, and public safety remain unresolved.

Gabriel Magalhães, Arsenal's defensive leader, was given the responsibility of taking the final penalty of the regulation five rounds. The defeat came just days after Arsenal had won the Premier League for the first time in 22 years, a drought that had defined the club's recent history.
