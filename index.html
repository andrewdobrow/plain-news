A federal judge in Washington, D.C., has declined to temporarily block President Trump's executive order that restricts mail-in voting. The decision allows the order to remain in effect while legal challenges continue through the courts.

Democrats filed a lawsuit seeking to halt the executive order, arguing that it improperly limits voting access for Americans. The judge's refusal to issue a preliminary injunction represents a setback for those opposing the measure, though the underlying legal questions about the order's validity will continue to be litigated.

The executive order places new restrictions on voting by mail, a practice that has grown significantly in recent elections. Supporters of the restrictions argue they are necessary to ensure election security, while critics contend that the limitations could make it harder for some Americans to vote. Voting rights advocates have expressed particular concern that the changes could disproportionately affect voters who depend on mail-in ballots due to work schedules, disabilities, or other circumstances.

By declining to block the order, the judge has allowed it to take effect as originally planned. However, the lawsuit challenging its legality will continue moving through the courts system. The case may ultimately determine whether the executive order exceeds presidential authority over election procedures.

The ruling highlights ongoing disputes about election administration and the balance of power between federal and state authorities in setting voting rules. Legal experts have noted that the case could eventually shape how mail-in voting is conducted in future elections, depending on how courts ultimately rule on the merits of the legal challenge.

Another judge may also rule on the order soon, according to reports. The decision came after arguments from both sides about the scope of presidential power over election procedures and whether the order complies with existing voting rights laws.

The order's opponents indicated they plan to continue pursuing their legal challenge despite this setback. The case represents a significant development in the broader debate over voting methods and election access that has intensified in recent years.

The ruling was reported by multiple major news outlets including Reuters, NPR, The Hill, Bloomberg, and The New York Times, underscoring the national significance of the decision.

Iran disputed the American account. Iran's military said the U.S. violated the ceasefire by attacking an Iranian oil tanker and another ship in the strait. He emphasized that no American assets sustained damage and that Iranian attackers and their equipment were "completely destroyed."

Despite the military exchange, both sides signaled reluctance to return to full-scale war. Consumer prices jumped at their fastest rate in nearly three years during April, driven largely by surging energy costs tied to the ongoing conflict with Iran. The personal consumption expenditures price index, the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation measure, climbed 3.8% over the 12-month period, exceeding the central bank's 2% target.

The April inflation report marks the first official reading under new Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh. The data reveals that Americans' real purchasing power continues to decline, with disposable income falling 0.5% after adjusting for inflation in April. This represents the third consecutive monthly drop in household income when accounting for price increases.

The war with Iran has significantly disrupted global energy markets and supply chains. The conflict has disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and strained access to goods including fertilizers, aluminum, and consumer products. Gasoline prices have surged more than 50% since the conflict began in late February, with the national average retail price shooting up 12.3% in April alone.

Beyond energy, inflation pressures are spreading across the economy. Core inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy components, rose to 3.3% year-over-year in April. Food prices rebounded 0.5%, while services prices increased 0.3% for the third straight month. Housing and utilities costs climbed 0.6%.

The inflation surge comes as consumer spending weakens. Real consumer spending edged up just 0.1% in April after increasing 0.3% in March. The government also revised down first-quarter GDP growth to 1.6% from the previously reported 2.0%, while cutting consumer spending growth from 1.6% to 1.4%.

Households are depleting savings to maintain spending levels despite rising prices. The savings rate dropped to 2.6% in April, the lowest level since June 2022, down from 3.2% in March. Tax refunds have provided some cushion for lower-income families, but economists expect consumers will eventually pull back and attempt to rebuild savings.

The inflation pressure threatens both the Fed's credibility and Trump's political standing. Trump won the 2024 election partly on promises to lower inflation, but rising prices have eroded his approval rating, particularly among Republicans. The issue poses risks to the Republican party's congressional majority ahead of midterm elections.

Financial markets are watching for the Fed's response. Current expectations suggest the central bank will maintain interest rates in the 3.50% to 3.75% range well into 2027. However, minutes from recent Fed meetings show a growing number of policymakers are open to rate hikes, setting up potential conflict with the White House, which has continued pressuring the Fed to cut rates.

U.S. military personnel have been targeted using location data collected and sold through commercial channels, according to government statements that have prompted lawmakers to raise alarm about the advertising technology industry's national security implications.

The disclosure highlights a growing vulnerability in how personal location information flows through the commercial data marketplace. Advertising technology companies routinely collect location data from smartphones and other connected devices, then package and sell this information to data brokers and other buyers. This data can reveal detailed patterns about where individuals live, work, and travel.

Military personnel who carry personal devices face particular risks. Applications installed on these devices can share location information with advertising networks without users fully understanding how that data will be used or who might eventually access it. Once location data enters the commercial data broker market, it becomes difficult for consumers to track or control its distribution.

The concern is especially acute because precise location information can expose sensitive details when connected to other databases. Knowing where someone travels regularly can reveal their home, workplace, school, or place of worship. For military personnel, this information could theoretically be purchased by foreign adversaries or other actors seeking to understand troop movements or deployment patterns near military installations.

Security researchers have repeatedly demonstrated that location data described as anonymized or aggregated can be re-identified to track specific individuals. Despite these findings, the commercial data collection industry continues to expand, with companies collecting information through vehicles, apps, and other connected devices.

The statement about troops being targeted comes as lawmakers intensify scrutiny of the broader data collection ecosystem. A leading privacy advocate in Congress called for treating the advertising technology industry as a national security threat, suggesting that legislative action may follow. Previous attempts to restrict commercial data collection through privacy legislation have stalled, but the national security angle could shift the political dynamics.

The case highlights how commercial data practices create risks that extend beyond consumer privacy concerns. Industries from automotive to advertising have faced increasing pressure over their data handling practices. General Motors recently agreed to pay 12.75 million dollars to settle claims that it sold location and driving data from California consumers to data brokers without proper consent.

The government statement did not specify how many troops were targeted, which military units were affected, or whether the targeting led to actual security breaches. It also did not identify which foreign actors or adversaries may have purchased or accessed the location data.

As commercial data collection becomes more sophisticated and widespread, the tension between industry practices and national security grows sharper. The disclosure about military personnel suggests policymakers are beginning to view data broker regulation not primarily as a consumer privacy issue, but as a military and intelligence concern.

Daniel Ingham, 33, died in a crash during qualifying for the Isle of Man TT on Wednesday. The incident occurred as competitors prepared for the annual motorcycle race on the island's public road course.

The Isle of Man TT is one of the most dangerous motorsport events in the world, held annually on a 37-mile course of closed public roads. Riders compete at speeds exceeding 200 miles per hour through towns, over mountains, and past stone walls with minimal safety barriers.

Ingham's death marks another fatality in the history of the race, which has claimed more than 260 lives since its inception in 1907. The event attracts top motorcycle racers from around the world despite its well-documented risks.

Qualifying sessions allow riders to familiarize themselves with the course and set lap times that determine their starting positions for the main races. These practice runs are often as dangerous as the races themselves, with riders pushing their machines to the limit on unfamiliar sections of the course.

The TT races are scheduled to continue despite the fatal crash. Organizers typically pause briefly to investigate incidents but resume racing after safety reviews. The event draws tens of thousands of spectators to the island each year and represents a significant part of the local economy.

Ingham was competing in his qualifying session when the crash occurred. No additional details about the circumstances of the accident have been released. Race officials conduct investigations after fatal incidents to determine contributing factors and assess whether course modifications are needed.

The Isle of Man, a self-governing British Crown dependency, closes its roads for two weeks each year to host the TT. The race has continued for more than a century despite mounting safety concerns and periodic calls for its cancellation. Supporters argue that competitors participate willingly and understand the risks involved in one of motorsport's most challenging events.

CBS News has undergone a significant leadership shake-up at its flagship newsmagazine 60 Minutes. Editor-in-chief Bari Weiss removed executive producers Tanya Simon and Draggan Mihailoivich, along with correspondents Sharyn Alfonsi and Cecilia Vega. Nick Bilton, a former technology columnist, was named to lead the program.

The departures mark a major restructuring at the television industry's most-watched newsmagazine. Simon and Mihailoivich held the two most senior executive producer positions at the show.

Alfonsi's departure came after her contract expired and was not renewed for the show's 59th season. She accused CBS News of penalizing her for refusing to compromise factually accurate reporting. "This was not a routine corporate transition; it was a deliberate choice to penalize a journalist for refusing to sanitize factually accurate reporting, and it sends a chilling message to the entire newsroom," Alfonsi wrote in a statement.

Alfonsi's exit followed an editorial dispute over a segment she reported about a notorious prison in El Salvador. Weiss shelved the story, which sparked national controversy. After the segment was killed, Alfonsi had not appeared on the final three episodes of the show's 58th season.

The correspondent expressed concern about the direction of CBS News operations. "The wall between editorial independence and corporate interest at CBS is being methodically torn down. Journalists willing to challenge authority are being pushed aside in favor of those who will not," Alfonsi said. She also raised questions about editorial decision-making, noting that some executives ask "Is it good for business?" rather than "Is the story true?"

In a public speech last month, Alfonsi decried "the spread of corporate meddling and editorial fear" at CBS News. She warned about the industry's fears of offending powerful interests and losing access to sources, saying journalists should instead fear silence.

The restructuring also affects veteran correspondent Anderson Cooper, who opted to leave 60 Minutes. Cooper emphasized that "the independence of 60 Minutes has been critical" and expressed hope that the program remains true to its core mission. "There are very few things that have been around for as long as 60 Minutes has and maintained the quality that it has," he said.

Bilton brings a background in technology and digital media journalism to his new executive producer role at CBS News. His appointment represents a notable shift in leadership for the program that has maintained its position as a prominent fixture in broadcast journalism for decades.

CBS News has not announced replacements for the correspondent positions vacated by Alfonsi and Vega. The program typically features multiple segments per episode, relying on in-depth interviews and investigative reporting across politics, culture, and other topics.

During this period, the Strait of...

A few minutes ago

The United States and Iran have reached a tentative agreement to extend their ceasefire for 60 days and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, according to U.S. sources. The deal requires approval from President Trump before it can take effect.

The memorandum of understanding would establish terms for maintaining the ceasefire over the next two months. During this period, the Strait of Hormuz would be reopened to restore a vital shipping route for global oil transport. The waterway has been largely closed since the conflict began, contributing to energy price increases that have affected household budgets worldwide.

Under the proposed agreement, Iran would be permitted to freely sell oil and would clear mines it deployed in the strait. Iran would also agree not to impose tolls on ships using the waterway. In return, the United States would lift its blockade on Iranian ports that has been in place since April 13. Additionally, some Iranian assets held in foreign banks would reportedly be unfrozen.

U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated that the deal could achieve "a completely open" Strait of Hormuz "without tolls" if successful. However, Iranian media reported that the strait would remain under Iranian control.

The agreement would also include talks on limiting Iran's nuclear program during the 60-day period. Hostilities would reportedly be halted on all fronts, including Israeli operations and the conflict involving Lebanon.

A senior Iranian source told Reuters that Tehran had not agreed to surrender its highly enriched uranium stockpile as part of the preliminary agreement. The source indicated that Iran's nuclear issue was not included in these initial talks, contradicting earlier reports that Tehran had expressed willingness to give up such material.

The agreement notably does not appear to address Iran's ballistic missile program or constraints on Iran's support for regional allies including Hezbollah in Lebanon and the Houthis in Yemen.

The tentative nature of the deal means its implementation remains uncertain. Trump's approval is necessary before the arrangement becomes operational. While negotiations continue, recent military activity has occurred, with the U.S. reportedly striking Iranian targets and prompting an apparent Iranian retaliatory attack on an American airbase in Kuwait.

The reopening of the Strait of Hormuz represents a significant development in restoring international commerce. The waterway handles a substantial portion of the world's oil supply, and its closure has been a major factor driving up energy prices. Oil markets responded positively to reports of the breakthrough, with global oil prices falling following news of the tentative agreement.

This data can reveal detailed patterns about where individuals live, work, and travel.

Military personnel who carry personal devices face particular risks. Applications installed on these devices can share location information with advertising networks without users fully understanding how that data will be used or who might eventually access it. Once location data enters the commercial data broker market, it becomes difficult for consumers to track or control its distribution.

The concern is especially acute because precise location information can expose sensitive details when connected to other databases. Knowing where someone travels regularly can reveal their home, workplace, school, or place of worship. For military personnel, this information could theoretically be purchased by foreign adversaries or other actors seeking to understand troop movements or deployment patterns near military installations.

Security researchers have repeatedly demonstrated that location data described as anonymized or aggregated can be re-identified to track specific individuals. Despite these findings, the commercial data collection industry continues to expand, with companies collecting information through vehicles, apps, and other connected devices.

The statement about troops being targeted comes as lawmakers intensify scrutiny of the broader data collection ecosystem.
