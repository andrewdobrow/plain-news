3:37 PM ET

Oil prices climbed sharply following a new round of military exchanges between the United States and Iran, with the main international oil benchmark rising 6 percent. Energy markets remain sensitive to any disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly one-fifth of the world's oil supply passes.

According to U.S. officials, American forces carried out strikes targeting an Iranian military facility and successfully shot down four Iranian one-way attack drones near the Strait of Hormuz. A ground control station in Iran's Bandar Abbas that was preparing to launch a fifth drone was also hit. U.S. Central Command described the actions as "measured, purely defensive, and intended to maintain the ceasefire."

Iran responded approximately two hours later. Iranian state media reported that the Revolutionary Guards had targeted an American military base in Kuwait in retaliation. Kuwait's army confirmed that its air defense systems were intercepting hostile missile and drone threats early Thursday morning.

The escalation coincided with reports that Iranian forces fired on four commercial vessels attempting to cross the Strait of Hormuz. Iranian state media said the incident occurred without prior coordination with security forces but provided no details on the ships or any damage.

The fresh military strikes underscored widening divisions between Washington and Tehran in negotiations aimed at resolving their broader conflict. President Donald Trump warned that if Iran did not reach an acceptable deal, "we'll have to just finish the job." He claimed Iran was "negotiating on fumes" and stated that the administration would not be pressured by upcoming elections to rush into an agreement.

One unresolved disagreement centers on whether a U.S.-Iran ceasefire would extend to Israel's operations against Hezbollah in Lebanon. Iran has insisted that any ceasefire agreement must cover Lebanese operations, while Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said his military is "deepening its operation" in Lebanon. Israeli forces have struck over 550 targets this week and have clashed with Hezbollah fighters in southern Lebanon's strategic areas.

The humanitarian toll continues mounting. More than 1.2 million Lebanese have been displaced since March, and Israeli strikes have killed over 3,200 people according to Lebanon's health ministry. Fighting has persisted despite a U.S.-brokered ceasefire announced in April, which now appears close to collapse.

Analysts warned that current oil price increases could represent just the beginning if shipping disruptions persist. The Strait of Hormuz closure would force tankers to take longer routes around Africa, adding significant costs and delays to global oil deliveries. With limited spare production capacity and inventories, traders are closely monitoring both military developments and diplomatic channels for signs of whether the conflict will expand or stabilize.

Authorities confirmed that no children at the center's school were harmed, and a reunification center was established for affected families. The FBI indicated it was investigating the shooting and established a tip line seeking public information.

Police have not released the names of the other two victims. No specific threat had been made against the Islamic Center, but investigators recovered evidence that the attackers engaged in what Wahl described as "generalized hate rhetoric." The FBI confirmed there were no officers involved in firing their weapons.

Florida has filed a lawsuit against OpenAI and its CEO Sam Altman in what appears to be the first state-level legal action of its kind against the artificial intelligence company. The lawsuit centers on violent incidents allegedly connected to ChatGPT, including a shooting at Florida State University last year.

According to Florida's Attorney General, the lawsuit accuses OpenAI of failing to warn users that ChatGPT could be dangerous. Instead, the company allegedly marketed the chatbot as safe and reliable. The complaint characterizes Altman as having an "utter disregard" for human lives.

The legal filing cites multiple violent incidents in connection with the chatbot, though the Florida State University shooting appears to be a central focus. The specific nature of ChatGPT's alleged role in these incidents has not been fully detailed in available reporting.

This lawsuit represents a significant moment in the growing legal challenges facing AI companies as their technologies become increasingly integrated into daily life. OpenAI has previously emphasized its commitment to building safety measures into ChatGPT and preventing misuse of the platform. The Florida action, however, suggests that state authorities believe these safeguards have proven inadequate in preventing real-world harm.

The legal action arrives as policymakers and regulators at both state and federal levels continue to grapple with how to address potential risks from increasingly capable AI systems. Florida's decision to pursue legal action rather than wait for new regulations represents a potentially significant shift in how states may approach AI governance.

The outcome of this case could establish important precedents for holding AI companies accountable for incidents involving their products. If Florida successfully establishes legal liability for OpenAI, it could prompt similar lawsuits in other states and potentially influence how AI companies design and deploy their systems going forward.

The decision to name Altman personally in the lawsuit is relatively uncommon and signals the severity with which Florida is treating the allegations. This approach places the CEO himself in legal jeopardy alongside the company.

As AI technology continues to advance rapidly and become embedded in consumer products and services, questions about corporate responsibility and government oversight have intensified. The Florida lawsuit illustrates how states are willing to take direct legal action to address what they view as inadequate safety protocols and transparency from major AI developers.

The case will likely attract significant attention from technology companies, regulators, and legal observers interested in understanding how courts will approach liability for AI-related harms. The outcome could reshape how AI developers market their products and what safety responsibilities they face under state law.

The Los Angeles Rams have acquired star edge rusher Myles Garrett from the Cleveland Browns in a major trade deal, multiple outlets reported Monday. The Rams sent linebacker Jared Verse, a 2027 first-round draft pick, and additional draft compensation to Cleveland in exchange for the two-time Defensive Player of the Year.

Garrett, 30, is one of the league's premier pass rushers and a seven-time Pro Bowl selection. Since the Browns drafted him first overall in 2017, he has accumulated 125.5 sacks, 413 pressures, and 149 tackles for loss. In 2025, he set the single-season sacks record with 23 and earned All-Pro recognition despite the team finishing 5-12 and missing the playoffs for the second consecutive year.

Garrett had requested a trade from Cleveland in 2024 after the team started that season 3-14, stating he wanted to play for a championship contender. A month later, he signed a four-year, $160 million extension that made him the highest-paid non-quarterback in league history at the time. The deal includes $122.8 million in guaranteed money and an average annual salary of $40 million.

The trade comes as the Browns undergo significant changes. Kevin Stefanski was fired as head coach in January and replaced by Todd Monken, the former Baltimore Ravens offensive coordinator. Monken indicated last month he had not yet met face-to-face with Garrett since taking the job.

Verse, the 25-year-old heading to Cleveland, was a first-round pick by the Rams in 2024 and earned Defensive Rookie of the Year honors. He was selected to the Pro Bowl this past season and has 12 career sacks along with 22 tackles for loss.

The Rams, who lost to the eventual Super Bowl champion Seattle Seahawks in last season's NFC championship game, have a history of aggressive trades. In 2021, they acquired Matthew Stafford from the Detroit Lions and won the Super Bowl in his first season with the team. Super Bowl LXI will be played at the Rams' home SoFi Stadium in Inglewood, California, next February.

The acquisition significantly strengthens Los Angeles' defensive line and signals the team's commitment to competing for a championship. Both organizations confirmed the trade, with Cleveland officials acknowledging the difficulty of the decision to part with their defensive cornerstone. For the Browns, the deal provides youth and draft flexibility as they begin a rebuild under new leadership.

CBS News has appointed Nick Bilton, a former New York Times technology journalist and documentary filmmaker, as executive producer of 60 Minutes for its upcoming 59th season. The announcement comes after a significant staff shake-up at the newsmagazine under the direction of Bari Weiss, the CBS News editor in chief.

The personnel changes announced Thursday were substantial. Tanya Simon, who served as the show's top producer, was ousted from her role. Simon had taken the position after longtime executive producer Bill Owens resigned in spring 2025. The network also parted ways with correspondent Cecilia Vega, who joined the program in 2023, and Draggan Mihailovich, the executive editor. Additionally, veteran correspondent Sharyn Alfonsi was terminated after her contract was not renewed.

Both Vega and Alfonsi issued statements suggesting editorial concerns influenced their departures. Vega stated that she and her producing teams had experienced attempts to insert political bias into stories. She described the situation as "censorship, both imposed and self-driven," and said reporting teams had withheld story pitches out of fear of internal consequences. Alfonsi claimed she was penalized for pushing back on Weiss's criticisms of her December 2025 segment about a prison in El Salvador.

The changes have generated internal friction. During a staff meeting with Bilton and CBS News managing editor Charles Forelle, veteran correspondent Scott Pelley directly criticized CBS News management, attacking the decision to fire multiple staff members. Pelley reportedly used harsh language in condemning the leadership decisions.

Bilton brings extensive experience in technology journalism and documentary filmmaking, though he has not previously produced broadcast television programming. In a memo to staff, he acknowledged the show's historical significance while suggesting the need for evolution. "The world we are reporting on, and the world we are reporting to, where people consume their news, has moved," he wrote. "And if we don't move with it, in the ways that matter, we won't be here for the next sixty years."

He outlined his philosophy as balancing preservation with change. "I'm here to lead this show, not preserve it under glass," Bilton wrote. "That means honoring what works and being honest about what doesn't."

Weiss expressed confidence in the appointment, calling Bilton "one of the most entrepreneurial and ambitious journalists working today." However, Bilton's lack of broadcast television experience, combined with Weiss's background in opinion commentary rather than television news, has raised questions among 60 Minutes staff who had viewed Simon's promotion as an indication that CBS leadership wanted to preserve the show's established approach.

The newsmagazine has maintained its position as the most-watched news program on television for 52 consecutive seasons, averaging 9.1 million viewers in its most recent season.

Mills attempted to highlight controversies from Platner's past during the campaign.
