The United States conducted new strikes on military sites in southern Iran on Wednesday, marking the second such attack in three days. According to U.S. officials, the strikes targeted attack drones and a drone ground control station that Iran had launched.

The military operations were described as defensive in nature. Officials stated the attacks focused on four one-way attack drones, hitting military installations in Iran's southern region.

The strikes come while diplomatic negotiations between Washington and Tehran continue, though a cease-fire between the countries remains fragile. The two nations have been engaged in talks aimed at resolving a three-month conflict that has involved multiple regional actors. The latest military operations threaten to complicate these ongoing diplomatic efforts, even as negotiations have stretched over an extended period without producing a final agreement.

Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, said his government expects to have a draft counterproposal ready within days following nuclear talks with the U.S. this week. During indirect discussions in Geneva with Trump's special envoy Steve Witkoff and son-in-law Jared Kushner, Araghchi stated the sides had reached an understanding on main "guiding principles." He indicated more talks could occur in about a week.

President Trump has signaled he is considering limited military strikes to pressure Iran into making concessions on its nuclear program. He ordered a massive buildup of naval forces in the Middle East, including repositioning aircraft carriers and other warships. Trump gave Iran a deadline of 10 to 15 days to make a deal or face "really bad things."

Araghchi countered that there is no "military solution" to Iran's nuclear program. "That has been tested last year," he said, referring to strikes on Iranian facilities. "They couldn't kill our nuclear programme."

During Geneva discussions, the U.S. did not seek zero uranium enrichment and Iran did not offer to suspend enrichment. Araghchi explained the focus shifted to ensuring Iran's nuclear program remains peaceful. A White House official responded that "the president has been clear that Iran cannot have nuclear weapons or the capacity to build them, and that they cannot enrich uranium."

Oil markets responded to the strikes with price increases, as traders expressed renewed concerns about potential disruptions to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. The waterway is critical for global energy supplies.

The conflict has affected neighboring countries. Kuwait reported being targeted by missiles and drones, though no attribution was provided. Israel has intensified military operations in southern Lebanon during the same period, with Lebanese residents expressing doubt that any U.S.-Iran agreement would bring peace to their country.

The Australian government filed a lawsuit against US manufacturing giant 3M seeking $2 billion in damages over contamination caused by PFAS chemicals in firefighting foam used at dozens of defense sites across the country.

The case centers on per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, commonly known as PFAS or "forever chemicals," which were present in foam products supplied by 3M. These chemicals earned their nickname because they do not break down naturally in the environment and can persist for decades.

The contamination affected multiple Australian defense facilities where the foam had been deployed. PFAS chemicals have raised health and environmental concerns globally due to their resistance to degradation and their accumulation in soil, water, and living organisms.

The $2 billion lawsuit represents one of the largest legal actions taken by a national government against 3M over PFAS contamination. The company has faced similar litigation in multiple countries as awareness of the chemicals' environmental persistence has grown.

3M, headquartered in Minnesota, manufactures a wide range of industrial and consumer products. The company previously produced firefighting foams containing PFAS for use by military installations, airports, and fire departments around the world.

Australian defense sites used these foam products for firefighting and training exercises over many years. The legal action alleges that this use resulted in widespread contamination of soil and groundwater at the affected locations.

The lawsuit comes as governments worldwide have increased scrutiny of PFAS chemicals and their manufacturers. Several countries have moved to phase out or restrict the use of these substances in firefighting foam and other products.

3M announced in recent years that it would discontinue manufacturing PFAS-based products, though the timeline for complete phase-out varies by product line and region. The company has also faced cleanup costs and legal settlements related to PFAS contamination at sites in the United States and other countries.

The Australian government's legal action seeks to recover costs associated with investigating and remediating the contamination at defense facilities. The case will proceed through Australian courts, though no trial date has been announced.

The Department of Justice has launched a criminal investigation into E. Jean Carroll, who accused President Trump of sexual assault, according to multiple reports.

CNN first reported Wednesday night that the investigation focuses on whether Carroll committed perjury while speaking at a deposition during her two civil lawsuits against Trump. The probe represents a significant escalation in the legal battle between Carroll and the president.

Carroll won two civil cases against Trump. In the first case, a jury found Trump liable for sexual abuse and defamation. In the second case, a jury awarded Carroll additional damages for defamation after Trump continued to deny her allegations.

The criminal investigation comes as Trump has repeatedly denied Carroll's accusations and criticized the civil verdicts against him. The Justice Department has not publicly commented on the scope or timeline of the investigation.

Perjury charges require prosecutors to prove that a witness knowingly made false statements under oath during official proceedings. The penalty for perjury in federal court can include fines and imprisonment.

Carroll originally came forward in 2019 with allegations that Trump sexually assaulted her in a department store dressing room in the mid-1990s. Trump denied the allegations and said he had never met Carroll, despite a photograph showing them together.

The civil trials included testimony from both Carroll and Trump, as well as other witnesses. Carroll testified under oath during depositions and at trial about her allegations and the impact of Trump's public denials.

The investigation adds another layer to the ongoing legal confrontations between Trump and his accusers. It also raises questions about the Justice Department's independence, as the agency now investigates someone who successfully sued the current president in civil court.

Neither the Justice Department nor Carroll's legal team has made public statements about the investigation. The timing and potential outcomes of the probe remain uncertain.
