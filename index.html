6:00 AM ET

The Trump administration has abandoned its effort to establish a $1.8 billion fund intended to compensate the president's allies, removing a significant barrier to passing legislation on Capitol Hill. The decision came after mounting legal challenges and opposition from Republicans worried about the fund's lack of oversight and potential payments to January 6 Capitol riot participants.

Senate Majority Leader John Thune said the withdrawal clears the path for advancing the stalled budget reconciliation package, which focuses on funding immigration enforcement operations. Republican leaders had struggled to unite their conference around the legislation before the Memorial Day recess.

The fund, called the "anti-weaponization" fund, was announced last month as part of a settlement related to Trump's lawsuit against the Internal Revenue Service over the leak of his tax returns. The administration described it as a way to compensate victims of what it characterized as politicized prosecutions. However, the fund's terms did not require disclosure of how much money went to whom, and administration officials indicated that pardoned January 6 rioters could receive payments.

The proposal faced swift legal and political backlash. A federal judge in Virginia temporarily blocked the administration from transferring money from the fund after Democracy Forward filed a lawsuit challenging it. The Justice Department said it disagreed with the court's decision but agreed to pause the program to comply with the ruling.

Republican concerns centered on the fund's structure and potential beneficiaries. Mike Pence, Trump's first term vice president, called the possibility of compensating rioters "deeply offensive," saying this view was widely shared among Republicans and Americans.

Several states pursued their own responses to the fund. California's governor proposed a 100 percent income tax on any distribution to a California taxpayer, with similar proposals emerging in Illinois, New York, and Connecticut.

Senate Democrats vowed aggressive opposition. Minority Leader Chuck Schumer called the fund "corruption in broad daylight" and described it as Trump's "most brazen act of self-dealing yet." Democrats promised to force Republicans to vote on measures blocking fund payments, preserve records, demand hearings, and pursue amendments during reconciliation discussions.

Schumer wrote that Democrats would prevent Republicans from hiding behind "fake guardrails or backroom promises." He said if Republicans pursued reconciliation, Democrats would be prepared with amendments to shut down the fund. If Republicans attempted to bury the issue, Democrats would force a Senate floor vote.

The administration's decision to back away from the fund removes what had become a major obstacle to Republican unity on the reconciliation package. Party leaders now hope to advance key priorities ahead of the November midterm elections without the controversy surrounding the fund derailing their efforts.

Russian missile and drone strikes on Ukraine killed at least 18 people, including an eight-year-old boy and a woman in an apartment block attack, Ukrainian officials said. Rescue workers pulled bodies from rubble across multiple locations as the death toll mounted from the overnight assault.

Russia launched 73 missiles and 656 drones at Ukraine, according to the air force, with eight hypersonic Tsirkon missiles among the weapons used. The main targets were Kyiv, the central cities of Dnipro and Zaporizhzhia, and the eastern cities of Poltava and Kharkiv. In Kyiv, loud explosions rocked the capital as residents sheltered in basements, corridors and metro stations. Black smoke billowed above the city. The authorities reported six people dead and 66 injured in the capital, including three children.

At least 12 people were killed and 36 injured in Dnipro, including a rescuer who died during a "double tap" attack as emergency responders arrived at the scene of the first strike. Six people were missing after their four-storey apartment block collapsed. In Kharkiv, at least 14 people were injured and residential homes, garages and cars were damaged.

Kyiv's mayor reported that a suspected missile strike on a 24-storey apartment block triggered a collapse with people thought to be trapped under the rubble. Other buildings including a nine-storey block caught fire. Rescue operations continued in the early hours as air raid alerts remained in effect.

Electricity was cut for 140,000 residents of the capital, according to power company DTEK. Workers later restored power to 110,000 residents, though two engineers were injured.

The strikes followed a week of Russian warnings threatening a major attack on Kyiv. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy had reiterated warnings on Monday, urging residents to pay special attention to air raid alerts. "Intelligence warnings regarding Russian strikes remain in effect. A massive strike is possible, they have prepared one," Zelenskyy said.

The assault represents a shift in Russian strategy as the military campaign faces setbacks. Zelenskyy has requested that Donald Trump send Patriot missiles to Ukraine, describing them as a "vital tool" in saving human lives. He has repeatedly warned that Kyiv is running out of Patriot interceptors, the only air defence system capable of blocking fast-moving enemy ballistic missiles.

"If Ukraine is not protected from ballistic and other missile strikes, these strikes will continue," Zelenskyy wrote on social media. "Europe needs its own anti-ballistic missiles so that this war can finally end. And we definitely need the help of the United States in supplying missiles such as Patriots."

Ukraine's foreign minister said Russia's latest strikes showed Moscow was running out of options. "Putin is a war criminal and loser who has no cards except terror," he said. "Moscow is losing on the battlefield."

Republican Senator Ron Johnson echoed these hawkish sentiments, urging Trump to take decisive action. "We have to finish the job," Johnson said. "We will not have won until we have completely defanged the Iranian regime."

Democratic leaders have responded with strong opposition to renewed hostilities. Senator Mark Warner, the top Democrat on the Senate intelligence committee, criticized the proposal to seize Iran's enriched uranium as extremely dangerous. The political divide reflects fundamental disagreements about how to proceed as the two-week ceasefire continues. The situation remains fluid as officials navigate negotiations and regional military developments.

Berkshire Hathaway agreed to purchase $10 billion of the offering, providing a high-profile anchor investor for the capital raise.

The company stated it is experiencing strong demand for its AI solutions and services from both enterprises and consumers that exceeds its current supply capacity. Half of the $80 billion will be used to scale AI infrastructure and global computing resources, while $40 billion addresses an administrative change related to how the company meets tax obligations associated with employee equity vesting. The offering consists of a $30 billion initial raise alongside the Berkshire investment, plus a $40 billion flexible mechanism that can be deployed gradually over time.

The capital raise reflects the unprecedented financial requirements of maintaining competitiveness in AI development. The $10 billion commitment accounts for more than 12 percent of the total offering.

Market analysts noted that Alphabet's fundraising signals the AI sector is entering a more capital-intensive phase. One equity analyst at Hargreaves Lansdown observed that while the $80 billion is substantial, it represents less than 2 percent of Alphabet's $4.6 trillion market cap. The analyst added that Alphabet is spending from a position of strength, with demand for AI compute running ahead of supply, Google Cloud growth accelerating sharply, and search proving more resilient than anticipated.

The announcement triggered broader market ripples, with infrastructure companies advancing on expectations of increased demand for computing equipment. Other major technology firms have similarly committed tens of billions to AI development, suggesting the capital requirements for staying competitive continue to escalate.

Alphabet's financing move comes as other AI companies prepare for public markets. Anthropic, which develops the Claude chatbot, confidentially filed for an initial public offering. OpenAI and Elon Musk's xAI are also slated to go public this year. Analysts noted that investors will demand continued evidence that Alphabet's infrastructure buildout translates into durable revenue growth rather than simply expanding data center capacity.

Alphabet announced plans to raise up to $80 billion through an equity offering to finance its artificial intelligence infrastructure investments. The fundraising, one of the largest ever undertaken by a corporation, reflects the massive capital requirements needed to compete in the AI sector and signals investor confidence in the company's AI growth prospects.

The funding structure includes a $30 billion initial raise, a $10 billion investment from Berkshire Hathaway, and a $40 billion flexible mechanism that can be deployed gradually over time. The Berkshire Hathaway investment is particularly notable, marking a significant vote of confidence from one of the world's most prominent investment firms.

According to Alphabet's statement, the company is experiencing demand for its AI solutions and services from both enterprises and consumers that exceeds its current supply capacity. The company plans to use roughly half of the $80 billion to scale its AI infrastructure and global computing capacity. The remainder covers an administrative change related to how the company manages tax obligations associated with employee equity awards.

This massive capital commitment comes as Alphabet previously announced capital expenditures would reach between $180 billion and $190 billion this year, with further significant increases expected in 2027. The company is investing heavily in data centers, specialized processors, and the energy infrastructure needed to power advanced AI systems.

Analysts note that Alphabet's fundraising underscores how the AI sector is entering a more capital-intensive phase. Matt Britzman, a senior equity analyst at Hargreaves Lansdown, observed that while $80 billion represents less than 2 percent of Alphabet's market capitalization, it signals a fundamental shift for technology giants. These companies are no longer the capital-light, high-margin businesses they once were.

However, Britzman emphasized that Alphabet's spending comes from a position of strength. Google Cloud growth has accelerated sharply, backlog has surged, and search remains resilient. These factors give the investment case more credibility than some other AI spending stories where paths to profitability remain unclear.

The fundraising also raises broader questions about the economics of the AI boom. Despite enormous capital investments across the industry, meaningful returns to investors have been limited so far. Market strategists suggest that funding the AI capital expenditure boom is becoming an increasingly critical topic for financial markets.

Alphabet's move comes as rival AI companies prepare for public markets. Anthropic, maker of the Claude chatbot, confidentially filed for an initial public offering. OpenAI and other major AI firms are also expected to pursue public listings this year, creating a competitive race to secure capital for AI infrastructure development.
