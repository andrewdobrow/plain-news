7:23 AM ET

A federal judge has ordered the removal of Donald Trump's name from the Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, ruling that the prestigious Washington DC venue cannot be renamed without congressional action.

U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper directed the Trump administration to take down all physical signage bearing Trump's name and eliminate references to a "Trump Kennedy Center" from official materials within 14 days. In his 94-page opinion, Cooper stated that the Kennedy Center's founding law makes clear the institution must be named for President Kennedy and cannot bear any other formal name based on unilateral board decisions.

"Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name, and only Congress can change it," Cooper wrote. The judge concluded that the board of trustees overstepped its legal authority by attempting to rename the center after Trump.

Trump responded swiftly on his Truth Social platform, announcing he had instructed the Department of Commerce to arrange a complete transfer of the Kennedy Center to Congress. He suggested that unless he remained free to renovate and restore the institution, he had no interest in continuing with it.

The ruling also temporarily blocked a proposed two-month summer closure for renovations that Trump had announced. Judge Cooper found the board's decision to halt operations was inadequately researched and neglected to consider the institution's statutory obligations. He characterized the closure decision as "ill-informed and seemingly preordained."

The lawsuit was brought by Joyce Beatty, an Ohio Democratic representative and member of the Kennedy Center's board. She called the ruling a vindication of efforts to prevent what she described as Trump's attempt to use the institution for personal vanity. "The Kennedy Center is an institution that belongs to the American people, not to Donald Trump," Beatty said.

The dispute began after Trump took office and appointed himself chair of the Kennedy Center's board of trustees in February, calling it a "take-over." He removed existing board members and replaced them with his appointees. In December, the reconstituted board voted to add Trump's name to the institution.

The attempt to rename the center sparked significant backlash. Artists and performing groups canceled bookings in protest, including the Tony award-winning musical Hamilton. The National Symphony Orchestra's executive director left her position, and Richard Grenell, a Trump appointee who championed the overhaul, announced his resignation as president in March.

The Kennedy Center, which receives federal funding, has long enjoyed bipartisan support as one of the nation's leading arts venues. Under Trump's leadership, it has become an extension of the White House's cultural agenda, hosting events from the World Cup draw to House Republican addresses and the premiere of a documentary about First Lady Melania Trump.

A combination drug developed by Chinese pharmaceutical company Akeso in partnership with Summit Therapeutics reduced the risk of death by 34% in a late-stage clinical trial, according to results presented at the American Society of Clinical Oncology conference.

The drug, ivonescimab, was tested in patients with squamous cell lung cancer, a subtype of non-small cell lung cancer. Researchers compared ivonescimab combined with chemotherapy against a standard PD-1 inhibitor also given with chemotherapy. Patients receiving ivonescimab experienced longer survival times than those on the comparison treatment.

Ivonescimab works through a dual mechanism, combining two distinct treatment approaches. The drug includes a PD-1 inhibitor, which activates the immune system to fight cancer cells, and a VEGF inhibitor, which blocks the blood vessel growth that tumors require to survive. This two-pronged strategy represents an emerging trend in cancer medicine where drugs target multiple biological pathways simultaneously.

The trial results have generated considerable discussion within the medical community. The drug has been described as hotly debated, though specific details about the controversy remain unclear from the trial data itself.

Squamous cell carcinoma represents a significant portion of lung cancer diagnoses, particularly among smokers. Lung cancer continues to rank among the most common and deadliest cancers worldwide. Current treatment approaches typically involve combinations of chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted medications.

The results unveiled at ASCO, an annual conference where major pharmaceutical companies present clinical trial findings, could have implications extending beyond Akeso and Summit. Other companies developing similar dual-pathway cancer treatments will likely monitor how ivonescimab performs through regulatory review processes and potential approval. The trial outcomes may influence development strategies across the oncology industry.

The dual action of blocking both PD-1 and VEGF pathways represents a strategic approach to combating cancer from multiple angles. By simultaneously enhancing immune activation and restricting tumor blood supply, the medication aims to attack the disease more effectively than single-pathway treatments.

As ivonescimab moves forward in the regulatory process, the trial's results could shape how other pharmaceutical companies approach cancer drug development. The 34% reduction in death risk demonstrates the potential value of combination approaches targeting multiple mechanisms within tumors. Companies developing competing treatments will be watching closely to understand how this strategy performs in real-world use and whether similar combinations might offer comparable benefits for patients with other cancer types.

The trial represents an important step toward expanding treatment options for squamous cell lung cancer patients, a population with limited effective therapies.
