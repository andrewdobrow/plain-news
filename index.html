Secretary of State Marco Rubio appeared before the Senate Foreign Relations Committee on Tuesday, offering his first public testimony since the Trump administration launched military operations against Iran in February. His testimony came as Tehran announced it was halting peace talks with the United States, citing Israeli military operations in Lebanon as a violation of ceasefire agreements.

Despite Iran's announcement to stop exchanging messages with Washington through intermediaries, Rubio expressed optimism about the diplomatic path forward. He told senators that Iran has agreed to negotiate aspects of its nuclear program that it had refused to discuss just a month earlier.

"There is the prospect before us, which could happen today, it could happen tomorrow, it could happen next week that for the first time, certainly in my memory, they have agreed to negotiate aspects of their nuclear program that just a month ago they said they would not," Rubio stated.

The secretary outlined a two-phase framework for potential negotiations. He said reopening the Strait of Hormuz, with Iran committing to remove mines, avoiding tolls, and pledging not to fire on ships, must come first as a precondition for talks rather than a bargaining chip. "That's the predicate that opens the door to phase two," Rubio explained.

Phase two would require Iran to dispose of its highly enriched uranium stockpile and negotiate strict, long-term limitations or cancellation of its enrichment activities. When asked by Senator Chris Murphy whether sanctions relief might be offered simply to reopen the waterway, Rubio rejected that idea, saying any sanctions relief would be tied to Iran's nuclear program.

Rubio also addressed the current blockade affecting Iranian oil revenue. He stated that Washington's counter-blockade, implemented after Iran allegedly failed to honor its commitment to reopen the strait under an April ceasefire agreement, costs Tehran "hundreds of millions of dollars a day" in lost revenue. "If they're going to shut down the straits for everybody, we're going to shut down the straits for them," Rubio said.

On Iran's military capabilities, Rubio claimed the country's missile program had been "substantially degraded" and its launcher capacity reduced. He stated that Iran's drone-building capability has been "eroded," though he acknowledged the regime still possesses numerous drones because they are relatively easy to manufacture.

The New York Times has reported, however, that Iran retained roughly 70 percent of its prewar missile stockpile, though analysts noted that over 85 percent of Iran's ballistic missile, drone, and naval defense industrial base has been damaged or destroyed.

Rubio's testimony reflects growing congressional pressure on the administration to clarify its Iran strategy as regional tensions persist and diplomatic efforts remain at an impasse.

Military analysts suggest Russia is attempting to gain leverage through intensified civilian strikes rather than achieving military objectives.

President Trump signed an executive order creating a voluntary framework for federal government review of powerful artificial intelligence models before public release. The order represents a shift from Trump's initial deregulatory approach to AI policy, which included revoking a Biden-era executive order on AI safety standards.

Under the new framework, technology companies are asked to share advanced AI models with the government for voluntary review up to 30 days before releasing them publicly. The Trump administration says this process will strengthen national security and cybersecurity protections. The order stops short of imposing mandatory review requirements, reflecting compromises made after industry objections and internal administration disagreements over how strictly to regulate the sector.

The executive order emerged after weeks of debate within the Trump administration. Some supporters pushed for stricter mandatory review processes, while technology industry advocates argued for lighter regulation to maintain competitive advantage. According to reports, Trump initially planned to back a stricter safety review requirement but reversed course, citing concerns about slowing American AI development and competition with China.

The National Security Agency and Department of Defense will help determine which AI models require government scrutiny. The Treasury Department will assist in identifying vulnerabilities within AI systems. The administration also directed the government to hire additional cybersecurity and AI professionals and strengthen cybersecurity systems at critical infrastructure like hospitals and banks.

The move builds on existing arrangements already in place. The Center for AI Standards and Innovation, part of the Department of Commerce, has similar agreements with OpenAI and Anthropic. The federal government recently made a deal with Microsoft, Google DeepMind, and xAI for early model reviews, though details of that agreement were subsequently removed from the government website.

The framework comes as AI safety experts and governments express growing concern about the capabilities of advanced AI models. Recent developments, including systems with sophisticated cybersecurity abilities, have raised alarms about potential misuse if these tools reach the wrong actors.

The Trump administration frames this approach as necessary for national security while maintaining innovation. However, free speech advocates have raised concerns that excessive government control over AI development could lead to censorship. Trump has also signed another AI-focused executive order aimed at preventing individual states from regulating artificial intelligence, establishing a federal taskforce to challenge state AI laws.

The voluntary nature of the review process means companies maintain the ability to decline participation, distinguishing this approach from stricter regulatory models that other countries have pursued.

UK Housing Market Gains Momentum Despite Middle East Uncertainty

UK house prices have surged unexpectedly in April, marking the fastest annual growth in 11 months even as the economy grapples with disruptions from the Iran conflict. According to Nationwide, the country's largest building society, property prices rose 3% year-over-year in April, up from 2.2% the previous month. The typical UK home is now valued at £278,880.

The monthly figures tell a similar story of resilience. Prices climbed 0.4% in April, defying economists' forecasts of a 0.3% decline. This marks the fourth consecutive month of price increases. When measured over three months to smooth out volatility, the growth rate reached 1.2%, the highest since February 2025.

Robert Gardner, Nationwide's chief economist, acknowledged the surprise: "Despite the uncertainty caused by developments in the Middle East and the subsequent rise in energy prices, the UK housing market has continued to regain momentum following the slowdown recorded around the turn of the year."

The strength of the housing market stands in stark contrast to weakening consumer sentiment. Consumer confidence has deteriorated noticeably, with the GfK confidence barometer falling to its lowest level since October 2023 in April. A separate survey of estate agents revealed that new buyer inquiries and property sales fell sharply in March, with expectations for future sales becoming significantly more pessimistic.

Lenders have also tightened conditions since the Middle East conflict began, withdrawing some mortgage products and increasing rates. The Bank of England held interest rates steady this week but signaled that increases may be necessary if energy costs remain elevated.

Despite these headwinds, Gardner suggested that household finances remain relatively healthy. "The market is likely being supported by the relative strength of household finances," he said. "In aggregate, household debt is at its lowest level relative to income for around two decades, and sizeable savings buffers have been built up in recent years."

However, economists urge caution about the sustainability of these price gains. Rob Wood, chief economist at Pantheon Macroeconomics, expressed skepticism about whether the momentum can continue. He suggested that some sales reflected in Nationwide's index may have been agreed during the early stages of the Iran conflict, before the full economic impact became apparent. "Unfortunately, we doubt prices can keep up their recent pace," Wood said.

The housing market data comes as significant new legislation takes effect. The Renters' Rights Act became law, introducing new protections for tenants including bans on no-fault evictions, limits on annual rent increases tied to market rates, and requirements that landlords reasonably consider pet requests.

Dashlane is among the largest password m...

11:40 AM ET

Password management company Dashlane has disclosed a security incident in which attackers gained unauthorized access to customer accounts and downloaded encrypted password vaults. According to the company, hackers exploited a vulnerability in its two-factor authentication system by using brute-force methods to bypass the security layer.

Dashlane is among the largest password management services available, with millions of users relying on the platform to securely store login credentials, payment card information, and other sensitive data. The company encrypts customer password vaults, which means the downloaded data remains scrambled without access to a user's master password. Security researchers have cautioned, however, that weaker master passwords could potentially be cracked through extended computational attacks.

The breach marks a notable security incident within the password management sector, an industry that has promoted itself as a more secure alternative to the widespread practice of reusing passwords across multiple online accounts. As cybersecurity threats have multiplied, both businesses and individual users have increasingly adopted password managers to generate and maintain complex, unique passwords for each account they maintain.

Dashlane has not disclosed the number of affected customers or the duration of the unauthorized access before the breach was detected. The company has also not indicated whether it will require users to change their master passwords or what preventive measures it intends to implement to avoid similar incidents in the future.

The incident reflects a broader pattern of significant breaches targeting password management services in recent years. The successful brute-force attack against two-factor authentication raises broader concerns about the security protocols employed by companies that handle sensitive credential information. Experts have long recommended that password manager users employ exceptionally strong master passwords and activate every available security feature to protect their stored vaults.

Two-factor authentication is generally considered one of the most effective security defenses available, but brute-force attacks can potentially overwhelm systems that lack sufficient protections against repeated login attempts. The vulnerability in Dashlane's implementation suggests that the company's rate-limiting or account lockout mechanisms may have been insufficient to prevent attackers from conducting sustained password-guessing campaigns.

The breach has implications for the millions of people who depend on Dashlane to manage their digital identities. Many users store years worth of account credentials in password managers, making the security of these platforms essential to protecting their broader online presence. A compromised master password could potentially give attackers access to numerous accounts across different services.

As investigations continue, cybersecurity professionals are likely to scrutinize how password managers implement two-factor authentication and what additional safeguards might be necessary to protect these sensitive repositories of credential data.

The White House Correspondents Association announced plans to reschedule its annual awards dinner for July 24 following a shooting incident at the Washington Hilton that forced an evacuation of President Donald Trump and hundreds of guests.

Weijia Jiang, president of the organization, informed members of the new date in a letter issued Tuesday. The original event was interrupted when a suspect charged a Secret Service checkpoint armed with a shotgun, a handgun, and multiple knives.

According to officials, the suspect was tackled to the ground by Secret Service members and apprehended. A Secret Service agent was injured during the incident and transported to a hospital. The suspect, identified as Cole Tomas Allen, 31, of Torrance, California, was also taken to a hospital for evaluation and faced felony firearm and assault charges.

Interim Metropolitan Police Chief Jeffery Carroll explained that the suspect appeared to have been a lone gunman who was staying at the hotel, which allowed him to pass through the outermost layer of security. The hotel had been closed to the public from 2 p.m. in anticipation of the 8 p.m. dinner, with access restricted to hotel guests, ticket holders, and people with event invitations or White House Correspondents Association credentials.

The roughly 2,300 guests in the ballroom had to pass through multiple security checkpoints, including ticket verification and magnetometer screening. The Secret Service maintained an additional perimeter around the president at the head table, with agents stationed in front of and alongside the stage.

After the shooting, guests hid under tables as Secret Service agents with drawn weapons rushed reporters from the room. Trump later praised law enforcement's response, noting that one officer was saved by wearing a bulletproof vest. Trump said he would have preferred to return and deliver his speech but agreed the gala should be rescheduled within 30 days.

The White House Correspondents Dinner has been a prominent Washington fixture for decades, bringing together journalists, government officials, and celebrities for speeches and journalism awards. The event has occasionally been controversial, with some presidents declining to attend, but security incidents of this magnitude have been rare.

The association's decision to proceed with a rescheduled dinner demonstrates its commitment to maintaining the annual tradition while addressing the serious security concerns raised by the shooting. The July 24 date allows security personnel and event organizers additional time to coordinate enhanced protective measures for what remains one of the most significant annual gatherings of political journalists and government officials in the nation's capital. The announcement reflects efforts to move forward from the alarming incident while prioritizing attendee safety.

UK Housing Market Gains Momentum Despite Middle East Uncertainty

UK house prices have surged unexpectedly in April, marking the fastest annual growth in 11 months even as the economy grapples with disruptions from the Iran conflict. According to Nationwide, the country's largest building society, property prices rose 3% year-over-year in April, up from 2.2% the previous month. The typical UK home is now valued at £278,880.

The monthly figures tell a similar story of resilience. Prices climbed 0.4% in April, defying economists' forecasts of a 0.3% decline. This marks the fourth consecutive month of price increases. When measured over three months to smooth out volatility, the growth rate reached 1.2%, the highest since February 2025.

Robert Gardner, Nationwide's chief economist, acknowledged the surprise: "Despite the uncertainty caused by developments in the Middle East and the subsequent rise in energy prices, the UK housing market has continued to regain momentum following the slowdown recorded around the turn of the year."

The strength of the housing market stands in stark contrast to weakening consumer sentiment. Consumer confidence has deteriorated noticeably, with the GfK confidence barometer falling to its lowest level since October 2023 in April. A separate survey of estate agents revealed that new buyer inquiries and property sales fell sharply in March, with expectations for future sales becoming significantly more pessimistic.

Lenders have also tightened conditions since the Middle East conflict began, withdrawing some mortgage products and increasing rates. The Bank of England held interest rates steady this week but signaled that increases may be necessary if energy costs remain elevated.

Despite these headwinds, Gardner suggested that household finances remain relatively healthy. "The market is likely being supported by the relative strength of household finances," he said. "In aggregate, household debt is at its lowest level relative to income for around two decades, and sizeable savings buffers have been built up in recent years."

However, economists urge caution about the sustainability of these price gains. Rob Wood, chief economist at Pantheon Macroeconomics, expressed skepticism about whether the momentum can continue. He suggested that some sales reflected in Nationwide's index may have been agreed during the early stages of the Iran conflict, before the full economic impact became apparent. "Unfortunately, we doubt prices can keep up their recent pace," Wood said.

The housing market data comes as significant new legislation takes effect. The Renters' Rights Act became law, introducing new protections for tenants including bans on no-fault evictions, limits on annual rent increases tied to market rates, and requirements that landlords reasonably consider pet requests.
