Yesterday, 5:11 PM ET

Anthropic has completed a Series H funding round raising $65 billion at a post-money valuation of $965 billion, making it the world's most valuable AI startup and surpassing its rival OpenAI. The funding round was led by Altimeter Capital, Dragoneer, Greenoaks, and Sequoia Capital.

The valuation represents a dramatic shift in the competitive landscape of the AI industry. Anthropic, which once occupied a smaller position in the global AI race, has emerged as a dominant player through widespread enterprise adoption of its products. The company's Claude chatbot and powerful coding assistants released last year have driven much of this growth and commercial prominence.

Krishna Rao, Anthropic's chief financial officer, highlighted the company's momentum in a statement: "Claude is increasingly indispensable to our growing global community of customers, and we work tirelessly to make tools like Claude Code and Cowork more helpful, more powerful, and more adaptable to their needs."

The company has distinguished itself through an enterprise-focused business strategy and a public commitment to AI safety. Anthropic co-founder Chris Olah appeared at the Vatican for Pope Leo XIV's release of a major encyclical warning about artificial intelligence's dangers and calling for stronger oversight of the technology. The company has also refused to remove safeguards from Claude that would allow its use in mass domestic surveillance or lethal autonomous weapons systems, leading to a legal dispute with the Pentagon.

Beyond product development, Anthropic has positioned itself as a proponent of government regulation in the AI space, diverging from other tech industry leaders. The company has invested millions in lobbying efforts and Super PACs supporting candidates and legislation aligned with its regulatory vision.

This valuation milestone underscores the substantial capital continuing to flow into the AI sector despite public concerns about the technology. The funding provides Anthropic with resources to further develop its AI systems and compete against OpenAI and other rivals in the rapidly expanding market.

The timing has broader implications for the technology industry. OpenAI raised $122 billion in March to reach a valuation of $852 billion and is expected to pursue a potential trillion-dollar initial public offering later this year. Anthropic's latest success adds to what is shaping up to be a blockbuster year for IPOs among AI companies and their competitors.

The deal reflects how intensely investors continue to back AI development despite questions about long-term profitability and business model sustainability. As both Anthropic and OpenAI race to develop and commercialize advanced language models and related technologies, valuations in the sector have climbed sharply.

Yesterday, 5:10 PM ET

An Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has claimed at least 65 confirmed deaths, with 246 suspected cases reported so far, according to health officials. The virus has primarily affected Ituri province, a conflict-hit region that shares borders with Uganda and South Sudan. Uganda's health ministry confirmed its own outbreak, reporting that a 59-year-old man died in a Kampala hospital after traveling from the Democratic Republic of Congo.

The World Health Organization has estimated the death rate between 30 and 50 percent based on confirmed cases. "It's huge. It means that up to five out of 10 people are likely to die," said Anaïs Legand from the WHO's high threat pathogens team. The WHO's director visited the worst-hit areas to support containment efforts.

Medical charity Médecins Sans Frontières described the situation as deeply alarming, warning that the virus is spreading rapidly through communities with limited medical infrastructure. Health workers in affected regions lack adequate equipment and trained personnel to contain the outbreak and implement standard containment measures such as contact tracing and isolation.

The strain identified in testing samples is Bundibugyo Ebola, for which no licensed vaccine exists. The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention confirmed the virus was detected in 13 of 20 samples tested. This strain has appeared only twice before, in 2007 and 2012.

Ituri province presents particular challenges for disease control. As a major mining hub, the region experiences constant movement of workers and high population density in close quarters. The area is also affected by ongoing conflict between armed groups, forcing waves of displaced people into overcrowded camps. These conditions create ideal circumstances for rapid virus transmission while making coordination of public health responses extremely difficult.

Officials from the Africa CDC, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Uganda, South Sudan, and the WHO convened an urgent meeting to coordinate regional response efforts. "Given the high population movement between affected areas and neighbouring countries, rapid regional coordination is essential," said Dr Jean Kaseya, director general of Africa CDC.

Aid organizations are working to deliver supplies and personnel to affected regions to support local health workers. However, the underdeveloped health infrastructure across much of the country continues to complicate these efforts. Cases have been reported mainly in the Mongwalu and Rwampara health zones, with suspected cases also appearing in neighboring Bunia.

The Democratic Republic of Congo has experienced 16 Ebola outbreaks since the virus was first identified there in 1976. This current outbreak represents another serious health crisis for a nation repeatedly vulnerable to infectious disease spread due to geographic, environmental, and socioeconomic factors.

Yesterday, 11:23 PM ET

A meteor exploded over northeastern Massachusetts and southeastern New Hampshire on Saturday afternoon, creating a powerful blast that was heard and felt across a wide region. The explosion occurred just after 2pm local time, according to Nasa officials.

The fireball broke apart at an altitude of 40 miles while traveling at 75,000 miles per hour. The blast was equivalent to 300 tons of TNT, producing sonic booms that resonated across New England and beyond. Hundreds of residents reported hearing and feeling the explosion, which left many puzzled about its source before authorities confirmed the meteor explanation.

Reports of the event came from a wide geographic area. Sightings extended from Delaware to Montreal, demonstrating how far the sound waves traveled from the explosion site. The widespread nature of the reports suggests the meteor was at a significant altitude when it broke apart, allowing the shock waves to reach populated areas across multiple states and into Canada.

Residents who experienced the boom described it as mysterious and startling. Many turned to social media and contacted local authorities to report the strange noise before the cause became clear. People in the Boston area and surrounding regions were particularly affected by the explosion.

When a meteor enters Earth's atmosphere at high speed, it generates intense heat. This causes rapid expansion of gases and creates a shock wave that produces the characteristic loud boom. A sonic boom occurs when an object travels through the atmosphere faster than the speed of sound, compressing the air in front of it and creating a pressure wave.

While meteors regularly enter Earth's atmosphere, most burn up completely without creating noticeable sounds or visual displays. The ones that explode with enough force to generate sonic booms are considerably less common and typically attract significant public attention when they occur over populated areas.

This event illustrates how even relatively small space rocks can produce dramatic effects when they enter the atmosphere at extreme velocities. The explosion over Massachusetts was detected by multiple monitoring systems and confirmed by Nasa officials.

The agency's deputy news chief Jennifer Dooren provided confirmation of the event to news organizations. Nasa continues to track and study such incidents as part of its efforts to monitor near-Earth objects and understand meteorite impacts on the planet.

The loud booms and the resulting public reports highlight how visible and audible such celestial events can be, even when occurring high in the atmosphere. For residents across New England, the mysterious sound served as a reminder of the dynamic processes occurring in the skies above Earth.

Yesterday, 9:37 PM ET

Claude Lemieux, a four-time Stanley Cup champion, has died at age 60, with his family announcing that his brain will be donated to Boston University's CTE Center for research.

Police confirmed that Lemieux died by suicide. The former NHL star enjoyed a successful career spanning nearly two decades, winning Stanley Cup championships with three different teams.

Lemieux's family made the decision to donate his brain to the research center at Boston University, which studies chronic traumatic encephalopathy, a degenerative brain condition linked to repeated head trauma. The center has become a leading facility for examining the long-term effects of concussions and head injuries in athletes.

The NHL player's death adds to growing concerns about player safety and mental health in professional hockey. Lemieux played 1,215 regular season games during his NHL career, known for his physical style of play and postseason performances.

Lemieux won his first Stanley Cup with the Montreal Canadiens in 1986, followed by back-to-back championships with the New Jersey Devils in 1995 and 1996. He captured his fourth title with the Devils again in 2000. He was particularly known for his playoff success, earning the Conn Smythe Trophy as playoff MVP in 1995.

The donation of his brain to the CTE Center will allow researchers to examine whether Lemieux showed signs of the condition. CTE can only be definitively diagnosed after death through brain tissue examination. The condition has been found in numerous former professional athletes and has been associated with symptoms including depression, memory loss, and behavioral changes.

Boston University's CTE Center, founded by Dr. Ann McKee, has examined hundreds of brains from former athletes and military veterans. The research has helped establish links between repetitive head impacts in contact sports and long-term neurological damage.

Lemieux's death represents a significant loss to the hockey community. His career was marked by memorable playoff performances and a reputation as one of the game's most effective postseason players.

Trump wants to finalize the deal soon but is seeking to strengthen several points that matter to him, particularly regarding Iran's nuclear material, two U.S. officials said. The president expects to complete the agreement despite the requested changes.

Trump's request has launched another round of negotiations between the parties that could last several more days. The back-and-forth comes as the administration works to extend the current ceasefire and reopen the Strait of Hormuz.

The two sides reached a tentative agreement earlier in the week. If both Trump and Tehran ultimately approve the final terms, the emerging deal would mark a significant shift in U.S.-Iran relations. Details of a potential agreement include that the strait would reopen with no tolls during a 60-day ceasefire extension, while Iran would be able to freely sell oil. According to reports, negotiations would be held on curbing Iran's nuclear program. In exchange, the U.S. would lift its blockade on Iranian ports.

Trump announced on Saturday that a peace deal "has been largely negotiated," after calls with a Pakistani mediator and Gulf allies. He wrote on his social media platform that "final aspects and details" of a "memorandum of understanding" were still being discussed and "will be announced shortly."

However, Iran's Fars news agency, which is close to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, reported that the strait of Hormuz would remain under Iranian control. The news agency stated that "the management of the Strait, determining the route, time, method of passage, and issuing permits will continue to be the monopoly and discretion of the Islamic Republic of Iran."

Pakistan's prime minister, Shehbaz Sharif, congratulated Trump on his peace efforts and said Pakistan hoped to host another round of talks between the U.S. and Iran "very soon." Pakistan's army chief, Syed Asim Munir, is a key figure in the negotiations.

The amendments focus on specific provisions related to Iran's nuclear program. Trump's decision to request modifications indicates his personal involvement in the final stages of the diplomatic process.

The agreement remains unsigned as the administration works to address Trump's concerns about nuclear material provisions and other key elements. Trump has said he would only sign a deal "where we get everything we want," adding that if an agreement was not reached, the U.S. would begin striking Iran again.

The negotiations have sparked criticism from Republican hawks who have spent years calling for military action against Iran and opposing the 2015 nuclear deal negotiated during the Obama administration.

A meteor exploded over northeastern Massachusetts and southeastern New Hampshire on Saturday afternoon, creating a powerful blast that was heard and felt across a wide region. The explosion occurred just after 2pm local time, according to Nasa officials.

The fireball broke apart at an altitude of 40 miles while traveling at 75,000 miles per hour. The blast was equivalent to 300 tons of TNT, producing sonic booms that resonated across New England and beyond. Hundreds of residents reported hearing and feeling the explosion, which left many puzzled about its source before authorities confirmed the meteor explanation.

Reports of the event came from a wide geographic area. Sightings extended from Delaware to Montreal, demonstrating how far the sound waves traveled from the explosion site. The widespread nature of the reports suggests the meteor was at a significant altitude when it broke apart, allowing the shock waves to reach populated areas across multiple states and into Canada.

Residents who experienced the boom described it as mysterious and startling. Many turned to social media and contacted local authorities to report the strange noise before the cause became clear. People in the Boston area and surrounding regions were particularly affected by the explosion.

When a meteor enters Earth's atmosphere at high speed, it generates intense heat. This causes rapid expansion of gases and creates a shock wave that produces the characteristic loud boom. A sonic boom occurs when an object travels through the atmosphere faster than the speed of sound, compressing the air in front of it and creating a pressure wave.

While meteors regularly enter Earth's atmosphere, most burn up completely without creating noticeable sounds or visual displays. The ones that explode with enough force to generate sonic booms are considerably less common and typically attract significant public attention when they occur over populated areas.

This event illustrates how even relatively small space rocks can produce dramatic effects when they enter the atmosphere at extreme velocities. The explosion over Massachusetts was detected by multiple monitoring systems and confirmed by Nasa officials.

The agency's deputy news chief Jennifer Dooren provided confirmation of the event to news organizations. Nasa continues to track and study such incidents as part of its efforts to monitor near-Earth objects and understand meteorite impacts on the planet.

The loud booms and the resulting public reports highlight how visible and audible such celestial events can be, even when occurring high in the atmosphere. For residents across New England, the mysterious sound served as a reminder of the dynamic processes occurring in the skies above Earth.
