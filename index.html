A few minutes ago

Anthropic, the AI company behind the Claude chatbot, has filed confidentially for an initial public offering on the US stock market, the company announced Monday. The confidential filing marks a significant step in the competitive race among AI firms to go public.

The company did not disclose the valuation it will seek on the stock market or provide other terms of the offering. However, Anthropic announced on Thursday that it had raised $65 billion in funding, valuing the company at $965 billion post-money. This represents a substantial increase from its February valuation of $380 billion, making Anthropic the world's most valuable AI startup.

Claude, Anthropic's conversational AI tool, has gained popularity among software engineers and other business clients. The chatbot competes directly with OpenAI's ChatGPT and other AI assistants in both consumer and enterprise markets.

Anthropic was founded by former OpenAI executives and has positioned itself as a leader in AI safety research. The company has raised substantial funding from investors including Google and various venture capital firms.

A confidential filing allows companies to submit their IPO paperwork to securities regulators without immediately making financial information public. This approach gives firms flexibility to assess market conditions and investor interest before committing to a public debut. Anthropic will need to publicly disclose its financial details before shares can begin trading.

The IPO filing comes as multiple major companies prepare to go public. Anthropic's move makes it the first to file ahead of its competitor OpenAI, which is expected to file for a public offering in the coming weeks. Additionally, Elon Musk's SpaceX has filed for a stock market listing at a valuation of approximately $1.75 trillion while seeking $75 billion in investment.

These filings reflect the escalating financial stakes in the AI industry. After years of heavy spending on computing infrastructure and research, AI companies face growing pressure to demonstrate sustainable business models to investors. The path Anthropic takes as a public company could influence how other AI startups decide between remaining private with continued venture backing or pursuing public listings.

The IPO filing represents a significant milestone for the AI sector, which has experienced explosive growth but seen limited exits to public markets. Anthropic did not indicate whether the offering would consist of new shares, sales of existing shareholder stakes, or both. Additional details about the offering are expected to emerge as the process advances.

A few minutes ago

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu ordered military strikes on the southern suburbs of Beirut following intensified attacks from the Iran-backed militant group Hezbollah. The operation marks a significant expansion of the conflict, bringing direct military action to Lebanon's capital for the first time in this round of fighting.

The escalation came after Hezbollah launched what it called "Operation Chewed Wheat," coordinated with Iranian forces. The group fired more than 200 rockets toward Israeli territory, including at military bases in Haifa, Tel Aviv, and Beersheba. Most projectiles were intercepted or fell in open areas, though two people were injured. Israel responded with intense bombardment of Beirut's southern suburbs and southern Lebanon throughout Thursday.

Israel issued sweeping evacuation orders covering a zone extending 25 miles from the Lebanese border, instructing residents north of the Zahrani River to leave immediately. The orders affect major cities including Nabatieh and dozens of villages. An additional evacuation order was issued for a neighborhood in central Beirut near restaurants, where Israel said it would strike a building.

Nearly a million people have been internally displaced over 10 days of fighting. Residents expressed desperation over the orders. Hamza Zbeeb, a municipal official from Nimiriya, asked: "Where can I go? Many people have gone to Beirut and returned because there's nowhere to stay. I don't want to be on the streets."

Israeli warplanes struck two buildings in central Beirut's Zouq Blat neighborhood, leveling them after warning residents to evacuate. One building was next to a shelter housing displaced families, creating large crowds in the main square seeking refuge. The other struck near the prime minister's office, UN agencies, and foreign embassies.

The Lebanese health ministry reported 70 people killed in Thursday's strikes. Among the dead were two academics, Hussein Bazzi and Murtadha Sarour, killed in an attack on Lebanese University's campus. Lebanon's president, Joseph Aoun, condemned the strike as a "flagrant violation of international laws and norms."

Israel's military stated it was targeting Hezbollah's missile launchers and warned of an imminent campaign of "overwhelming force." A spokesperson said residents should distance themselves from affected areas immediately.

Human rights organizations described the evacuation orders as forced displacement that could amount to war crimes. They argued that Israeli forces should still work to prevent civilian harm even if residents do not evacuate.

The exchange represented the most severe escalation yet in the 10-day conflict, with Hezbollah and Iran coordinating attacks for the first time. Israeli strikes have killed at least 634 people and injured 1,586 across Lebanon.

Iran and US Navigate Tense Negotiations as Ceasefire Holds

The United States and Iran are navigating a fragile ceasefire that has been tested repeatedly over the past week, with negotiations toward a permanent deal advancing and stalling in unpredictable ways.

The ceasefire, which began on April 7, remains technically in place, though both sides have reported military incidents. On May 4, the US military said it destroyed six Iranian small boats and intercepted Iranian cruise missiles and drones, claims Iran denied. The US also reported that Iran has fired at commercial vessels and seized two container ships since the ceasefire began, though these attacks have so far remained below the threshold that would restart major combat operations.

A major point of contention involves the Strait of Hormuz, the critical waterway through which roughly one-fifth of global oil supplies pass. Iran has effectively choked off the strait, while the US Navy has been blockading Iranian ports. Trump administration officials announced "Project Freedom" on May 3, a plan to help guide stranded ships through the waterway. However, Iranian officials warned that any American intervention in managing the strait would violate the ceasefire. After beginning the operation on May 4, Trump paused Project Freedom just one day later, announcing the pause would give space for peace negotiations.

According to reporting from May 6, Washington and Tehran were close to agreeing on a one-page memorandum of understanding. Trump said he expected Iran to respond to several key points within 48 hours. However, recent reporting indicates Trump is seeking edits to the proposed agreement, with requested changes related to the Strait of Hormuz and the removal of highly enriched uranium.

Trump has sent mixed signals about his commitment to a deal. On May 5, Secretary of State Marco Rubio declared "the operation is over" and said the US was focused on negotiations. But Trump has also threatened escalation, writing on social media that if Iran does not agree to the terms, "the bombing starts, and it will be, sadly, at a much higher level and intensity than it was before."

The negotiations have been complicated by broader regional tensions and Trump's own statements. In public remarks, Trump described a recent US operation to seize an Iranian ship as acting "like pirates," noting the US took the ship's cargo and oil. He also made threats that Iranian forces would be "blown off the face of the earth" if they attacked US vessels.

Oil prices have fluctuated with each development, trading between roughly $109 and $114 per barrel as markets react to the unpredictable course of negotiations. Officials in Pakistan, who have been helping facilitate talks, described negotiations as remaining "difficult," even as both sides have indicated they are seeking a resolution to avoid further escalation.

Kevin Keegan, the former England manager and Newcastle United icon, has publicly revealed he has stage four cancer. The 75-year-old initially had his diagnosis announced by his family in January, but provided an update on his condition during a recent appearance at the Tyne Theatre and Opera House.

"They said we have a top doctor with this new way of fighting what you have got, which is stage four cancer," Keegan said in remarks reported by the Daily Mail. "He was a Liverpool supporter so I went to meet him. I knew I wouldn't be walking alone, if you know what I mean."

Keegan had been admitted to hospital for evaluation of ongoing abdominal symptoms before tests confirmed his diagnosis. His family released a statement expressing gratitude to the medical team involved in his care and requesting privacy during this difficult period.

Keegan's playing career stands as one of English football's most distinguished. He earned 63 England caps, scoring 21 goals for his country and captaining the side 31 times. He competed in the 1982 World Cup and won two Ballon d'Or awards as European Footballer of the Year in 1978 and 1979.

As a club player, Keegan achieved remarkable success. He helped Liverpool win three English league titles while also securing the European Cup, two UEFA Cups, and the FA Cup. He later won the Bundesliga with Hamburg, cementing his status as one of Europe's finest footballers during the late 1970s and early 1980s.

His managerial career proved equally memorable. Keegan took charge of Newcastle United in 1992, overseeing the club's promotion to the top flight before building what became known as "The Entertainers". His Newcastle side topped the Premier League table for much of the 1995-96 season but ultimately finished as runners-up to Manchester United in a dramatic title race.

After leaving Newcastle in 1997, Keegan managed Fulham before becoming England manager from 1999 until his resignation in 2000. He then spent four years at Manchester City before returning to Newcastle for a brief eight-month spell in 2008 that ended amid boardroom tensions with owner Mike Ashley and director of football Dennis Wise. Following his departure from Newcastle, Keegan successfully pursued a constructive dismissal case and was awarded 2 million pounds in damages by an independent Premier League tribunal in 2009.

Newcastle United has sent their heartfelt support and warmest wishes to Keegan during his treatment. The club and football community have rallied behind the former manager as he faces this serious health challenge.

Federal Reserve officials warn AI costs may outpace benefits

Several Federal Reserve officials have cautioned that artificial intelligence's economic costs may materialize faster than its promised gains, challenging the assumption that AI will help solve inflation problems. The officials point to stronger evidence of AI-driven investment increasing demand for workers and equipment than evidence of productivity improvements materializing....

11:55 AM ET

Several Federal Reserve officials have cautioned that artificial intelligence's economic costs may materialize faster than its promised gains, challenging the assumption that AI will help solve inflation problems. The officials point to stronger evidence of AI-driven investment increasing demand for workers and equipment than evidence of productivity improvements materializing.

These warnings take on particular significance as the Fed operates under new leadership from Kevin Warsh, who has argued that AI's supply-side advantages justify maintaining lower interest rates. The question of how AI will influence inflation and productivity has become central to shaping monetary policy decisions. Some Fed officials now contend that the technology's impact on demand could complicate the effort to control prices before any productivity benefits actually emerge.

The debate reflects a broader tension within the Federal Reserve about how to interpret AI's economic impact. While technology enthusiasts and some policymakers expect AI to boost supply-side capacity and help reduce inflation over time, several Fed officials worry that demand effects are arriving first. Businesses appear to be spending heavily on AI infrastructure and hiring workers to implement new systems, which adds to inflationary pressures in the near term.

This concern carries weight given the Fed's historical focus on managing inflation expectations. If demand surges outpace productivity gains, the central bank may face pressure to maintain higher interest rates for longer than some officials prefer. This creates a dilemma for Warsh and other officials who believe lower rates are justified by AI's long-term potential.

The productivity question remains unresolved. While companies have invested billions in AI capabilities, widespread evidence of actual productivity improvements across the economy has not yet materialized at scale. This timing gap between investment and results means the Fed must make policy decisions based partly on expectations about future productivity rather than current data.

Fed officials highlighting the demand-side risks appear concerned that policymakers and markets may be getting ahead of themselves in assuming AI will quickly solve persistent inflation challenges. Inflation has remained stubbornly above the Federal Reserve's 2 percent target despite months of interest rate increases, suggesting that supply-side solutions may not arrive as quickly as some have hoped.

The debate matters because it affects decisions on interest rates, which ripple through the broader economy and influence borrowing costs for businesses and consumers. If demand effects dominate in the near term, the Fed may need to keep rates higher to prevent inflation from accelerating further. If productivity improvements arrive sooner than skeptics expect, lower rates could eventually prove justified.

As the Fed charts its course, officials will need to balance optimism about AI's long-term potential against wariness about its immediate economic effects. The outcome of this debate will significantly influence monetary policy direction and broader economic outcomes in coming months.
