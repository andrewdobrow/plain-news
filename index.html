Yesterday, 10:46 PM ET

A case of New World screwworm has been confirmed in South Texas, marking the first time the parasitic fly has crossed the US-Mexico border since being eradicated from the United States decades ago. The detection has prompted the US Department of Agriculture to expand monitoring and prevention efforts along the border.

The screwworm is a parasitic fly whose larvae burrow into living tissue of warm-blooded animals and humans, consuming flesh and creating larger wounds. Female flies lay eggs in open wounds, and once hatched, hundreds of larvae use sharp mouths to feed on living tissue, potentially killing their host if untreated.

The New World screwworm was once endemic throughout much of the southern United States, causing devastating losses to the livestock industry. In the 1950s, scientists developed a breakthrough technique using radiation to sterilize fly eggs. Since female flies typically mate only once, encounters with sterile males prevented fertilization and populations declined dramatically. The program eventually pushed the screwworm south to Panama.

However, around 2022, the screwworm began spreading northward again, likely aided by cross-border livestock trade. According to the FDA, approximately 89,000 cases have been detected in animals over the past three years in Central America. The parasite is now only a few hundred miles south of the US border.

The risk to humans from screwworm is low. A patient in Maryland was diagnosed in August after traveling to El Salvador, but the infection was detected before the insect matured, preventing transmission. According to Heather Walden, an associate professor of parasitology at the University of Florida College of Veterinary Medicine, screwworm does not spread rapidly. "This is not an instantaneous infestation. This takes time," she explained.

The primary concern centers on livestock. Officials worry the parasite could infect animals crossing the border and cause serious economic damage, particularly in Texas where large cattle populations exist. Wildlife could also carry the parasite across the border, as occurred during a 2016 infestation among deer in the Florida Keys.

In response to rising cases, the USDA announced plans to reopen a Texas breeding facility for sterile flies and launched an 850 million dollar initiative in August to combat the screwworm. The agency is expanding monitoring programs to trap flies and continuing inspections of livestock heading for slaughter.

The case highlights the importance of international cooperation on research and prevention efforts. However, some global programs to fight the screwworm were cut by the Trump administration, and scientific research and international coordination have faced disruption in recent months.

The Department of Health and Human Services declared the screwworm a significant potential public health emergency, paving the way to investigate whether existing anti-parasitic medications like ivermectin could help control infestations in animals.
