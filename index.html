A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded during a static fire test at Launch Complex 36 in Cape Canaveral, Florida on Thursday night at 9 p.m. ET. The massive fireball destroyed the launchpad seconds after the scheduled test began, with the explosion visible as an orange glow across Florida's space coast and even reported by residents in South Carolina over 100 miles away.

Blue Origin founder Jeff Bezos confirmed that all employees were safe and accounted for following the incident. The company described the explosion as "an anomaly" and said it was already investigating the cause. Bezos tweeted that it was a "very rough day" but expressed confidence the company would rebuild and return to flying.

The explosion represents a significant setback for Blue Origin's ambitions in the commercial space industry. Just two days before the test, NASA announced that Blue Origin had won a contract to launch the first of three planned uncrewed lunar missions this year to begin construction of a $20 billion moon base. The New Glenn rocket is the vehicle Blue Origin intends to use for these critical NASA missions, which are part of the Artemis program to return humans to the moon.

The incident threatens NASA's timeline for lunar base development. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said the agency would conduct a full evaluation of the program's schedule following the explosion. "Spaceflight is unforgiving, and developing new heavy-lift launch capability is extraordinarily difficult," Isaacman posted on social media. "We will work with our partners to support a thorough investigation of this anomaly, assess near-term mission impacts, and get back to launching rockets."

This explosion comes amid a string of recent setbacks for Blue Origin. Last month, a payload from the New Glenn's third flight ended up in the wrong orbit, and the Federal Aviation Administration temporarily grounded the rocket. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared the vehicle to return to flight.

Blue Origin is competing with SpaceX across multiple fronts. Both companies have built large facilities at Cape Canaveral to support crewed and cargo missions with NASA. For the Artemis program specifically, Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander will compete against SpaceX's Starship Human Landing System to determine which system will carry astronauts to the lunar surface during the Artemis IV mission planned for 2028.

The explosion was widely felt across Florida's space coast, with shockwaves shaking homes in nearby Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach. Flames continued burning at the launchpad more than two hours after the blast. Emergency officials reported no threat from fumes or other hazards, though the extent of damage to the launch facilities remains under assessment.

Uganda has shut its border with the Democratic Republic of Congo following a growing outbreak of the Bundibugyo strain of Ebola in the neighboring country. The closure represents an effort to prevent further spread of the virus, which has already reached Uganda.

Health officials in the Democratic Republic of Congo have reported 65 deaths from the outbreak, with 246 suspected cases identified primarily in Ituri province. The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention confirmed that laboratory testing detected the Ebola virus in 13 of 20 samples analyzed. Uganda's health ministry has confirmed cases within its own borders, including a 59-year-old man who died in a Kampala hospital after traveling from the DRC. Ugandan officials have reported seven confirmed cases of the virus.

The Bundibugyo strain is distinct from the more common Zaire strain responsible for previous major outbreaks in the region. This particular virus has no licensed vaccine currently available, unlike the Zaire strain. The Bundibugyo virus was first identified in Uganda in 2007, with previous outbreaks occurring in 2007 and 2012.

Officials express particular concern about the outbreak's location in Ituri province, which borders Uganda and South Sudan. The region contains mining towns characterized by significant population movement, making disease control efforts challenging. The World Health Organization's director general, Dr. Jean Kaseya, emphasized that rapid regional coordination is essential given the high movement of people between affected areas and neighboring countries.

The World Health Organization has estimated the outbreak's death rate at between 30 and 50 percent among confirmed cases. The organization's head arrived in the Democratic Republic of Congo to support containment efforts and called for an immediate ceasefire in the eastern DRC, warning of a "catastrophic collision of disease and conflict" that is outpacing response efforts.

Ebola spreads through direct contact with body fluids of infected individuals, including blood and vomit, as well as contact with dead bodies during funeral preparations. The disease is characterized by severe illness and high fatality rates.

Several factors contribute to regular Ebola outbreaks in the Democratic Republic of Congo, according to health experts. Close human contact with animal reservoirs, likely bats or primates, plays a significant role. Additional concerns include movement between rural and urban environments, tropical climate conditions, and extensive rainforest coverage.

The Democratic Republic of Congo has experienced 16 Ebola outbreaks since the virus was first identified there in 1976. The border closure is expected to remain in place while health authorities assess the outbreak's scope and implement containment measures. The Africa CDC has convened an urgent meeting with authorities from affected countries and pharmaceutical companies to coordinate response efforts.

A central issue fueling the debate is the lack of transparent evidence. US Southern Command has not provided definitive proof that the vessels being struck are involved in drug trafficking operations. The military has also not disclosed the nationalities of those killed or the specific locations where the boats were operating when struck.

The operations target vessels in the eastern Pacific and Caribbean, regions long used as corridors for drug smuggling operations attempting to move narcotics northward. The Trump administration frames the military action as part of a broader counter-narcotics strategy using military force in international waters.

The legality of these strikes remains contested among experts and human rights organizations both in the United States and internationally. Critics argue that targeting individuals based solely on intelligence assessments, without providing evidence or due process, violates international law and fundamental human rights principles.

US Southern Command has not responded to requests for additional details about targeting procedures or the evidence used to identify vessels as drug smuggling operations. The military continues to describe those targeted as operators of designated terrorist organizations while declining to specify which groups are involved.

The mounting death toll underscores the expanding scope of US military involvement in drug interdiction operations at sea, even as questions persist about oversight, transparency, and the legal authority for such strikes.

Proposed rules would let government cancel research grants at any time

The federal government has proposed new rules that would allow officials to cancel research grants at any point without cause. The proposal marks a significant shift in how the government awards and manages funding for scientific research. Under curre...

Yesterday, 6:58 PM ET

The federal government has proposed new rules that would allow officials to cancel research grants at any point without cause. The changes would also make peer review optional and introduce political screening of grant applications for prohibited topics.

The proposal marks a significant shift in how the government awards and manages funding for scientific research. Under current practice, peer review serves as the standard method for evaluating research proposals, with panels of independent scientists assessing applications based on scientific merit.

The new framework would give political staff authority to screen grant applications before approval. Reviewers would look for topics the administration has designated as forbidden, though the proposal does not specify which subjects would be restricted.

The cancellation provision represents the most sweeping change in the proposed rules. Grant recipients currently receive funding commitments for the duration of approved research projects, typically spanning multiple years. The new policy would eliminate that protection, allowing the government to terminate funding at any stage of a research project.

Researchers depend on multi-year funding commitments to plan long-term studies, hire staff, and purchase equipment. Sudden cancellations could leave projects incomplete and researchers without the resources to finish work already underway.

The optional peer review provision would permit the government to award grants based on criteria other than scientific evaluation. Peer review was developed to ensure that taxpayer funds support the most promising research, with decisions made by experts in each field rather than by administrators.

The proposal applies to federally funded research across multiple agencies. Federal grants support work at universities, research institutes, and private laboratories throughout the country. In 2025, the government distributed billions of dollars in research funding across fields including medicine, physics, computer science, and climate research.

The rules have not yet taken effect. The government typically allows a comment period before finalizing policy changes of this scope, giving researchers and institutions time to respond to the proposal.
