The United States military struck Iranian radar and drone control sites inside Iran after Tehran shot down an American MQ-1 Predator drone over the weekend. The attacks represent the third known escalation between the two countries in the past week, centered around the Strait of Hormuz.

Kuwait reported that its air defenses opened fire after detecting missile and drone attacks on Monday. Both Iran and the US confirmed they carried out fresh strikes in the region. The US characterized its actions as defensive measures following the downing of its unmanned aircraft.

Satellite imagery analyzed by BBC Verify shows that Iranian attacks have damaged 20 US military sites since the start of the conflict. Analysts said Tehran's strikes were more extensive than US officials have publicly acknowledged. The images provide visual confirmation of the scale of damage to American military infrastructure in the region.

The Strait of Hormuz is a critical waterway for global oil shipments, with roughly one-fifth of the world's petroleum passing through the narrow channel. The escalating military actions between the US and Iran raise concerns about the security of international shipping lanes and the potential for broader regional conflict. Iran has previously threatened to close the strait in response to American military pressure.

The exchange of strikes follows a pattern of tit-for-tat military actions between Washington and Tehran. The US has maintained a military presence in the Persian Gulf for decades, citing the need to protect commercial shipping and counter Iranian influence in the region. Iran has developed asymmetric warfare capabilities including drones and missiles to challenge American military superiority in the area.

The weekend confrontation began when Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps launched air base attacks following the US strikes. Iran claimed it had targeted military sites in response to what it described as American aggression. The US military maintained that its initial bombing campaign was limited to radar and drone control facilities, designed to degrade Iranian capabilities without targeting broader military infrastructure.

Regional observers warn that the cycle of escalation risks spiraling into a wider conflict that could affect global energy markets and international shipping. The repeated exchanges demonstrate how quickly tensions in the Persian Gulf can intensify, particularly given the strategic importance of the waterway to world commerce.

Both nations have shown willingness to conduct military operations in the region despite international calls for de-escalation. The situation remains fluid, with military officials from both sides signaling readiness to respond to further provocations. The targeting of civilian shipping lanes and military assets continues to pose risks to regional stability and global economic interests tied to Middle Eastern oil production and transportation.

Gilgeous-Alexander's individual performance was exceptional. He averaged a league-leading 32.7 points per game on 51.9% shooting while adding 6.4 assists, 5.0 rebounds, 1.7 steals and 1.0 block. His offensive and defensive excellence helped the Thunder achieve a league-best 68 wins and set an NBA record with a plus-12.9 average point differential.

The 26-year-old guard's statistical achievements place him among basketball's all-time greats. He joined Michael Jordan as the only players to average at least 30 points on 50% shooting while recording five rebounds, five assists, 1.5 steals and one block in a season. This marks his third consecutive year topping 30 points per game while shooting better than 50%, a feat previously accomplished only by legends including Kareem Abdul-Jabbar, Wilt Chamberlain, Giannis Antetokounmpo and Jordan.

Beyond his scoring prowess, Gilgeous-Alexander was critical to Oklahoma City's top-ranked defense. He compiled 208 combined steals and blocks, ranking third in the league. His two-way excellence earned him the MVP award over Denver's Nikola Jokic, who he finished behind last season.

The MVP honor carries significant historical weight for both Gilgeous-Alexander and the franchise. He became the first Thunder player to win the award since Russell Westbrook in 2017 and only the third in team history alongside Westbrook and Kevin Durant. Gilgeous-Alexander is also the first Canadian to claim the award since Steve Nash in 2006.

Despite the individual accolades, Gilgeous-Alexander's primary focus remains on team success. The Thunder entered the postseason as championship contenders, having won the previous season's title. Their early playoff exit left the star disappointed after failing to advance deep enough to pursue back-to-back championships.

The reigning champions lost to the San Antonio Spurs in the Western Conference Finals in a dramatic Game 7 series that saw the Thunder struggle when Victor Wembanyama sat on the bench with foul trouble.

Looking ahead, Gilgeous-Alexander is positioned to become the NBA's highest-paid player. He qualifies this summer for a four-year supermax extension worth $294 million, the richest annual deal in league history. The Thunder will have the opportunity to build around their MVP-caliber star as they pursue championship aspirations in coming seasons.

Powell warns Federal Reserve credibility at risk in first remarks since leaving office

Powell did not explicitly name the Trump administ...

Yesterday, 8:31 PM ET

Former Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell has warned that the central bank's credibility faces a significant test following his departure from the role. Powell made the comments while accepting the John F. Kennedy Profiles in Courage award, delivering his first public statements since his term as Fed chair concluded on May 15.

Powell did not explicitly name the Trump administration in his remarks, but his message clearly referenced ongoing efforts to challenge the Federal Reserve's independence from political pressure. These efforts include a criminal investigation into the Fed's building renovation project, which exceeded its budget, and attempts to remove Fed Governor Lisa Cook from her position.

In framing his concerns, Powell emphasized that weakening the central bank's independence from politics threatens an institution vital to American economic strength. The Fed has long operated under the principle that monetary policy decisions should be based on economic data rather than political influence.

Powell's warning reflects mounting tensions between the White House and the Federal Reserve over the bank's autonomy. The investigation into building renovation costs and other recent challenges represent what Powell described as a stress test for the institution's credibility and authority in economic policymaking.

The situation has taken on added urgency with the recent confirmation of Kevin Warsh as Powell's successor. The Senate confirmed Warsh in a 54-45 vote that split largely along party lines, making it the most divisive Fed chair confirmation in history. Democratic Senator John Fetterman of Pennsylvania was the sole Democrat to support Warsh's nomination.

Warsh, a former Fed governor from 2006 to 2011 and an Ivy League economist with Wall Street experience, has echoed calls from Trump to lower interest rates. However, with inflation currently at 3.8 percent, convincing other voting board members to reduce rates could prove challenging. The Fed's rate-setting decisions influence borrowing costs throughout the economy, making the chair's role particularly consequential.

During his Senate confirmation hearing, Warsh pledged to maintain Fed independence and keep politics out of monetary policy decisions. However, his refusal to answer whether Trump lost the 2020 election raised concerns among Democrats, who worry he may prioritize political pressure over economic data when making rate decisions.

Powell has announced he will remain on the Fed's board as a voting governor, stating he will stay until the White House ends its scrutiny of the building renovation project. Powell characterized this scrutiny as a pretext masking attempts to pressure the central bank into lowering rates against sound economic judgment.

As Warsh takes over leadership of the Fed on May 14, the central bank faces sustained pressure from the administration while navigating rising inflation and ongoing geopolitical tensions in the Middle East.
