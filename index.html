The United States and Iran have agreed on a framework for extending their ceasefire, but significant obstacles remain in finalizing a comprehensive peace deal, according to recent reports.

Vice President JD Vance stated that the two sides are "very close" to a deal, though important details still need to be resolved. U.S. officials confirmed that negotiators have reached agreement on the basic structure of a ceasefire extension and new nuclear talks, but the deal requires final approval from President Trump and Iran's leadership before taking effect.

However, recent military escalations have cast doubt on whether peace negotiations will resume. Iran's parliament speaker, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, the lead Iranian negotiator, declared that reopening the Strait of Hormuz would be "impossible" while the U.S. and Israel commit what he called "flagrant" ceasefire breaches. He specifically cited the U.S. naval blockade and accused both countries of using economic pressure and military aggression to achieve their goals.

The tensions escalated when Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps seized two ships in the Strait of Hormuz, claiming they were attempting to exit covertly. The vessels included the Panama-flagged MSC Francesca and the Liberia-flagged Epaminondas. These seizures mark the first time Iran has taken control of ships since the conflict began on February 28. The move came after the U.S. seized an Iranian cargo vessel and boarded an Iranian oil tanker in the Indian Ocean.

The Strait of Hormuz, through which approximately 20 percent of the world's oil and liquefied gas passes in normal times, has become a focal point of conflict. Both sides have imposed competing blockades on the waterway, creating cascading global economic consequences.

The situation has severely damaged international economies. The closure has stranded approximately 20,000 seafarers and 2,000 ships in the Gulf, according to the International Maritime Organization. Germany cut its 2026 growth forecast to 0.5 percent, while Greece announced 500 million euros in emergency aid for households and farmers. Energy bills are rising across Europe, with typical households facing significant increases.

Planned peace negotiations in Pakistan fell through when the Iranian delegation never confirmed attendance and the U.S. delegation led by Vice President Vance never left Washington. A Pakistani official said they had prepared everything for the talks but the cancellation was an unexpected setback.

The conflict has also expanded beyond Iran. Israel and the Iranian proxy group Hezbollah are fighting a second front in Lebanon, with a tenuous ten-day ceasefire set to expire. Israeli strikes killed five people in Lebanon on Wednesday, including journalist Amal Khalil.

While neither side appears interested in returning to full-scale warfare, the recent seizures and strikes demonstrate that meaningful progress toward peace remains elusive despite the tentative framework agreement.

A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded during a ground test at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida on Thursday night, creating a massive fireball that destroyed the launchpad and was visible for miles across the state's space coast.

The explosion occurred at 9pm ET during a scheduled "hotfire" test, in which the rocket remains stationary on the launchpad while its engines are ignited. The blast was so powerful that shockwaves were felt in nearby communities, including Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach, with residents reporting that homes shook from the impact. An orange glow was visible as far south as Fort Pierce, 115 miles away, and residents hundreds of miles north in South Carolina reported seeing the sky light up.

Blue Origin confirmed that all employees at the facility were safe and unaccounted for, with no injuries reported. The company described the incident as an "anomaly" during testing procedures. In a statement on social media, founder Jeff Bezos acknowledged the setback, saying "it's a very rough day" for the company but pledging to rebuild and return to flying operations.

The explosion carries significant consequences for NASA's lunar programs. Just two days before the incident, NASA announced that Blue Origin had won a contract to launch the first of three uncrewed lunar missions planned for this year to begin construction of a 20 billion dollar moon base. The New Glenn rocket was specifically selected to launch lunar landers for NASA, including those designed to carry astronauts to the moon's surface as part of the Artemis program.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman acknowledged the serious implications, posting that the agency would conduct a full evaluation of timeline impacts following the explosion. He noted that "spaceflight is unforgiving, and developing new heavy-lift launch capability is extraordinarily difficult," while committing to work with Blue Origin on a thorough investigation.

The explosion marks another setback for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA contracts. The company had already faced challenges this year, including a payload from New Glenn's third flight ending up in the wrong orbit last month, which triggered a temporary grounding by the Federal Aviation Administration. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared the rocket to resume operations.

The New Glenn rocket is central to Blue Origin's efforts to compete in the commercial space sector. In addition to NASA contracts, the company is in competition with SpaceX to provide a lunar lander for the Artemis IV mission planned for 2028, which would return astronauts to the moon for the first time since 1972.

Flames continued burning at the launchpad more than two hours after the explosion. Emergency officials indicated there was no threat from fumes or other hazards from the incident.

Blue Origin rocket explodes during ground test at Florida launchpad

A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded during a ground test at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida on Thursday night, creating a massive fireball that destroyed the launchpad and was visible for miles across t...

4:00 AM ET

A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded during a ground test at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida on Thursday night, creating a massive fireball that destroyed the launchpad and was visible for miles across the state's space coast.

The explosion occurred at 9pm ET during a scheduled "hotfire" test, in which the rocket remains stationary on the launchpad while its engines are ignited. The blast was so powerful that shockwaves were felt in nearby communities, including Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach, with residents reporting that homes shook from the impact. An orange glow was visible as far south as Fort Pierce, 115 miles away, and residents hundreds of miles north in South Carolina reported seeing the sky light up.

Blue Origin confirmed that all employees at the facility were safe and unaccounted for, with no injuries reported. The company described the incident as an "anomaly" during testing procedures. In a statement on social media, founder Jeff Bezos acknowledged the setback, saying "it's a very rough day" for the company but pledging to rebuild and return to flying operations.

The explosion carries significant consequences for NASA's lunar programs. Just two days before the incident, NASA announced that Blue Origin had won a contract to launch the first of three uncrewed lunar missions planned for this year to begin construction of a 20 billion dollar moon base. The New Glenn rocket was specifically selected to launch lunar landers for NASA, including those designed to carry astronauts to the moon's surface as part of the Artemis program.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman acknowledged the serious implications, posting that the agency would conduct a full evaluation of timeline impacts following the explosion. He noted that "spaceflight is unforgiving, and developing new heavy-lift launch capability is extraordinarily difficult," while committing to work with Blue Origin on a thorough investigation.

The explosion marks another setback for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA contracts. The company had already faced challenges this year, including a payload from New Glenn's third flight ending up in the wrong orbit last month, which triggered a temporary grounding by the Federal Aviation Administration. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared the rocket to resume operations.

The New Glenn rocket is central to Blue Origin's efforts to compete in the commercial space sector. In addition to NASA contracts, the company is in competition with SpaceX to provide a lunar lander for the Artemis IV mission planned for 2028, which would return astronauts to the moon for the first time since 1972.

Flames continued burning at the launchpad more than two hours after the explosion. Emergency officials indicated there was no threat from fumes or other hazards from the incident.
