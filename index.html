The U.S. military carried out another strike on a vessel in the eastern Pacific Ocean, killing three people. This marks the second deadly attack in as many days, with the overall death toll from recent operations now exceeding 200 since last September.

Officials with U.S. Southern Command announced the strike on social media, stating that intelligence had confirmed the vessel was traveling along routes associated with drug trafficking and engaged in narco-trafficking operations. The military posted a video of the attack showing the boat being destroyed in an explosion.

The strikes are part of an ongoing campaign by the U.S. military's Joint Task Force Southern Spear, the counter-narcotics unit operating in the region. Since September of last year, this campaign targeting boats in the Caribbean and eastern Pacific has resulted in at least 178 deaths. The Trump administration has defended the operations as necessary and legal.

"What we're doing is actually an act of kindness," Trump said regarding the strikes, arguing that the military campaign is necessary to prevent overdose deaths in the United States and stop the flow of illicit drugs into the country.

The administration has claimed the strikes are legal under the laws of conflict, asserting that the U.S. is engaged in warfare against drug cartels. However, legal experts have raised serious concerns about the operations. Civil rights groups argue the U.S. military is violating both domestic and international law through these attacks.

The families of two men from Trinidad who were killed in a previous strike have filed a lawsuit against the government. Additionally, the American Civil Liberties Union has called for investigations into what it characterizes as human rights violations.

"We are doing everything in our power to hold the Trump administration responsible for its egregious violations of both U.S. and international law, and that includes asking the widely respected Inter-American Commission on Human Rights to investigate these heinous killings," said Jamil Dakwar, director of the ACLU's Human Rights Program.

U.N. officials have described the U.S. campaign as a flagrant violation of human rights. Civil rights organizations have vowed to challenge the legality of the deadly attacks through all available legal pathways.

The military has released little detailed evidence supporting its claims that the targeted vessels are involved in drug trafficking. U.S. Southern Command has posted a steady stream of announcements about the strikes on social media in recent months, typically accompanied by low-resolution videos showing vessels being destroyed.

Despite the lack of publicly disclosed evidence and mounting legal challenges, the Trump administration has continued to frame the operations as a justified response to the narcotics crisis affecting the United States.

The disease has a high fatality rate and previous outbreaks have caused thousands of deaths in West and central Africa. The current outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has so far remained contained within the country.

Brazilian authorities are following protocols for suspected Ebola cases, which typically include isolation of patients and monitoring of anyone who may have had contact with them. Laboratory testing is required to confirm whether the patients have contracted the virus.

The monitoring comes as international health organizations track the spread of infectious diseases across borders. Ebola can take up to 21 days after exposure for symptoms to appear, making containment efforts challenging when infected individuals travel before showing signs of illness.

Brazil has experience managing infectious disease outbreaks, having dealt with Zika virus, dengue fever, and yellow fever in recent years. The country's health system has established protocols for handling highly contagious pathogens, though Ebola would present unique challenges given its severity and transmission characteristics.

Confirmation of Ebola cases in South America would require coordination between Brazilian health authorities, the World Health Organization, and international disease control agencies to prevent further spread and provide appropriate medical care to infected patients.

12:10 PM ET

The Securities and Exchange Commission has proposed eliminating a regulation that would have required all publicly traded companies to disclose climate change risks. The rule targeted whether companies faced significant risks from climate change and its effects.

The regulation represented a major effort to standardize how businesses report climate-related financial risks to investors. By proposing to kill the rule, the SEC reverses course on requiring climate risk transparency in corporate financial disclosures.

The proposal affects thousands of publicly traded companies that would have been subject to the disclosure requirements. These companies would have needed to evaluate and report whether climate change posed material risks to their operations, supply chains, or long-term business strategies.

Climate-related financial disclosures have become a significant topic for investors seeking to understand how environmental changes might affect corporate performance. Some investors have pushed for standardized reporting to compare climate risks across different companies and industries.

The SEC did not provide details in the available information about the reasoning behind the proposal to eliminate the rule or the timeline for finalizing the change. The agency also did not specify what process or public comment period might precede a final decision.

Companies that prepare financial statements for investors typically must disclose material risks that could affect their business performance. The proposed climate rule would have created specific requirements around environmental and climate-related risks, making them a mandatory part of corporate reporting.

The regulation's elimination would remove a structured framework for climate risk disclosure, though companies could still choose to report such information voluntarily. Many large corporations already include climate-related information in sustainability reports or other public communications, but these disclosures vary widely in scope and detail.

A meteor exploded over the northeastern United States on Saturday afternoon, creating a bright fireball and sonic boom that startled residents across a wide region. NASA confirmed the event occurred around 2:06 pm ET, with the explosion taking place north of Cape Cod Bay in Massachusetts.

The meteor streaked across the sky and was captured on camera by multiple witnesses. The explosion shook houses in the area and produced such bright light that it was clearly visible in satellite imagery. The event generated significant public attention as residents reported the unusual disturbance on social media and to authorities.

The fireball resulted from a small asteroid approximately six feet in diameter, weighing about seven tons, entering Earth's atmosphere at extremely high speed. According to NASA, the object was moving at 45,000 mph when it first became visible above Lake Erie. The intense heat and pressure from atmospheric entry caused the asteroid to fragment and explode.

The meteor's trajectory carried it southeastward before it broke apart over the area north of Cape Cod Bay. Fragments continued on a southern path, with meteorites potentially landing in the vicinity of the affected region.

The sonic boom from the explosion was felt across a substantial geographic area, with reports coming from multiple states. The loud noise startled many residents who were initially uncertain about its source. Some people described the sound as similar to fireworks, with lingering rumbling effects comparable to thunder. Others thought the disturbance might have been caused by something striking their homes.

NASA noted that despite its size and weight, the asteroid was still considered relatively small. According to the space agency, most space rocks smaller than a football field break apart in Earth's atmosphere. Though explosions in the air are rare, this event demonstrates that such occurrences do happen.

Witnesses caught the fireball on video as it crossed the sky, and these recordings helped confirm the meteor as the source of the sonic boom. The widespread nature of the reports and observations allowed scientists to quickly identify and analyze the event.

As of the latest reports, no debris had been found on the ground. Meteorologists indicated that while some small fragments may have survived atmospheric entry, the majority of the asteroid burned up during its passage through the atmosphere.

This event highlights how meteors regularly enter Earth's atmosphere, though events producing sonic booms audible across such a large area remain relatively uncommon. NASA continues to monitor near-Earth objects and provides public information following significant atmospheric events to explain unusual phenomena and address community concerns.
