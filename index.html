The U.S. military conducted defensive strikes against Iran after the country attacked American forces in the Persian Gulf region, marking a serious breakdown in a month-long ceasefire between the two nations.

According to U.S. Central Command, Iranian forces launched multiple missiles, drones and small boats at three American destroyers, the USS Truxtun, USS Rafael Peralta and USS Mason, which were transiting the Strait of Hormuz. Centcom stated that no U.S. assets were damaged in the attack, which it characterized as unprovoked hostilities by Tehran.

In response, the U.S. military shot down four Iranian one-way attack drones and struck a ground control station in Bandar Abbas that was preparing to launch a fifth drone, according to U.S. officials. The strikes targeted Iranian military facilities responsible for the attacks, including missile and drone launch sites, command and control locations, and surveillance nodes.

Iran disputed the American account, claiming the U.S. broke the ceasefire first. Military officials in Tehran accused the U.S. of targeting an Iranian oil tanker and another ship in the strait, and said American warplanes struck civilian areas along the Iranian coast near Bandar Khamir, Sirik and the island of Qeshm, which is home to roughly 150,000 people. Iran's military said its forces responded by attacking the American vessels.

The exchange of fire represents a major test of the fragile month-long ceasefire between Washington and Tehran. The violence raised questions about whether diplomatic efforts to end the three-month war could succeed.

President Trump characterized the military clash as minor, calling it a "love tap" in an interview with ABC News. "They trifled with us today. We blew them away," Trump told reporters during an evening visit to Washington.

Despite the strikes, Trump indicated he remained open to negotiations. When asked whether a deal remained possible, he said it "might not happen, but it could happen any day." He also claimed that Iran wanted an agreement more than he did.

The escalation followed Trump's warning to Oman to "behave" or face consequences. Iran responded by pledging support for the Gulf nation against U.S. threats. Additionally, Trump had stated that Iran was mistaken if it believed he would feel pressured to reach a settlement before the midterm elections later this year.

Before the military exchanges, reports suggested the U.S. and Iran were close to a temporary agreement to halt the war. Some Iranian officials have favored extending negotiations closer to the November elections, when they believed the Trump administration would face greater pressure to end the conflict.

The strikes sent oil prices higher, with Brent crude rising to roughly $101 per barrel.

A federal judge in Washington, D.C., has declined to temporarily block President Trump's executive order calling for restrictions on mail-in voting. The decision allows the order to remain in effect while legal challenges continue in court.

Plaintiffs seeking to halt the executive order had requested a temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction. These emergency legal tools would have paused the order while the underlying lawsuit proceeded. The judge's refusal to grant this relief means the voting restrictions can stay in place during ongoing litigation.

The ruling addresses only the request for immediate emergency intervention. It does not evaluate whether the executive order violates federal voting rights laws or the Constitution. Those substantive legal questions will be decided in later court proceedings as the case moves forward.

Legal experts note that the judge's decision to deny emergency relief does not reflect a judgment on the merits of the case. Instead, it means the court determined the plaintiffs had not met the legal threshold for stopping the order before the full litigation concluded.

Another judge in a separate jurisdiction may soon rule on similar requests to block the executive order. Multiple legal challenges to the order are currently working through the federal court system in different locations across the country.

Mail-in voting has become increasingly contentious in recent election cycles. Supporters argue that expanding access to mail-in ballots increases voter participation, particularly among those who face obstacles to in-person voting. Opponents have raised concerns about election security and the potential for fraud, though election officials have generally reported that mail-in voting remains secure.

The rules governing mail-in voting vary significantly by state. Some states conduct virtually all elections by mail, while others require voters to provide specific reasons or qualifications to receive absentee ballots. How the Trump executive order will interact with existing state voting laws remains unclear. Some legal experts expect conflicts between federal directives and state election systems.

The timing of this ruling comes well before the next federal election cycle, giving the courts several months to decide the case on its merits. States are watching the litigation closely, as the outcome could significantly affect how they administer elections.

The executive order's specific provisions have not been fully detailed in publicly available court documents. However, the order broadly seeks to limit the use of mail-in ballots, which have expanded substantially over the past several years, particularly following changes made during the 2020 election.

As litigation continues, uncertainty remains about how states will ultimately respond to the executive order. The legal battles over voting access and election procedures are likely to continue through multiple court levels before reaching final resolution.

Inflation Rises to 3.8 Percent as Iran Conflict Pushes Up Prices

The Federal Reserve's preferred measure of inflation climbed to 3.8 percent in April compared to the same month last year, marking the highest level since May 2023. The Personal Consumption Expenditures price index rose as economic effects from the Iran conflict disrupted global energy markets and supply chains. The PCE index tracks price changes for goods and services across...

A few minutes ago

The Federal Reserve's preferred measure of inflation climbed to 3.8 percent in April compared to the same month last year, marking the highest level since May 2023. The Personal Consumption Expenditures price index rose as economic effects from the Iran conflict disrupted global energy markets and supply chains.

The PCE index tracks price changes for goods and services across the economy. The Federal Reserve relies on this measure rather than the Consumer Price Index to guide monetary policy decisions and maintain its target inflation rate of 2 percent.

The Iran conflict has significantly impacted energy prices, the primary driver behind April's inflation increase. Oil prices surged more than 25 percent in a single week following military strikes, with Brent crude reaching $91.89 per barrel, its highest point since April 2024. Before the conflict began, oil traded around $72.50 per barrel.

The disruption extends beyond crude prices. Kuwait began cutting oil production after running out of storage capacity, and consultants at Kpler warned that storage facilities in Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates could reach their limits within 20 days. Qatar's energy minister predicted that if the war continued unabated, all Gulf energy exporters could shut down production within weeks, potentially pushing oil to $150 per barrel.

The conflict also damaged critical energy infrastructure. An Iranian drone strike hit an important terminal in Qatar, which accounts for about 20 percent of global liquified natural gas exports. Even if the war ended immediately, Qatar's energy minister indicated it would take weeks to months to resume LNG shipments.

These energy disruptions ripple through the broader economy. Higher oil prices increase transportation and shipping costs, which businesses pass along to consumers. In the UK, energy bills are set to rise by £221 annually for households using typical amounts of energy. For American drivers, gasoline prices have climbed significantly, with analysts warning that pump prices are unlikely to return to prewar levels even if the conflict ends soon.

Rising inflation reduces purchasing power. When prices climb faster than wages, consumers can afford less with the same amount of money. The 3.8 percent annual increase means items that cost $100 in April 2025 now cost $103.80 on average.

The inflation surge has broader economic consequences. UK government bond yields posted their biggest one-week jump since September 2022, and money markets shifted expectations away from interest rate cuts. Stock markets in Asia-Pacific countries that rely on Gulf energy imports experienced their worst week since the start of the Covid-19 pandemic.

The Federal Reserve faces difficult policy choices. Higher interest rates could slow inflation by reducing consumer spending and business investment, but they risk slowing economic growth and increasing unemployment. The central bank's next policy meeting is scheduled for mid-June.

Yesterday, 8:45 PM ET

A Google engineer faces charges of insider trading after allegedly profiting $1.2 million from wagers placed on the prediction market platform Polymarket. The charges relate to bets connected to Google's 2025 Year in Search campaign.

According to the complaint, the engineer risked more than $2.7 million on wagers tied to the campaign. The case highlights potential conflicts of interest when employees with access to confidential company information participate in prediction markets that allow betting on corporate outcomes.

Polymarket operates as a blockchain-based platform where users can place bets on the outcomes of real-world events, ranging from political elections to corporate announcements. The platform has grown in popularity as an alternative method for forecasting future events based on collective market predictions.

Insider trading laws prohibit individuals from using material, non-confidential information to profit from securities trades or other financial transactions. While prediction markets operate differently from traditional stock markets, prosecutors appear to be applying similar legal principles to this case.

The charges against the Google engineer represent one of the first major insider trading cases involving prediction markets. Legal experts note that as these platforms gain prominence, regulators are increasingly scrutinizing how employees with access to confidential information use them.

Google has not publicly commented on the charges or whether the engineer remains employed at the company. The case raises questions about whether technology companies need to establish clearer policies regarding employee participation in prediction markets, particularly those that allow wagers on company-related events.

The complaint does not specify which aspects of the Year in Search campaign were subject to the wagers or how prosecutors determined the engineer possessed insider knowledge. Year in Search is Google's annual compilation of top search trends, typically released at the end of each calendar year.

If convicted, the engineer could face substantial fines and potential imprisonment. The case may prompt other technology companies to review their policies on employee trading and participation in alternative financial platforms.

7:36 AM ET

Cynthia Erivo has spoken out about how reactions to a Singapore premiere incident revealed systemic bias against Black women and discouraged her from pursuing an Oscar campaign for Wicked: For Good.

The incident occurred when Johnson Wen jumped a barrier at Universal Studios Singapore and rushed toward Erivo and co-star Ariana Grande. Erivo intervened to protect Grande, pushing Wen away after he refused to release Grande. Both actresses were frightened during the encounter. Wen, who has a history of disrupting public events, was later sentenced to nine days in jail.

What followed on social media troubled Erivo deeply. Many online commenters suggested she had been Grande's "bodyguard," an assumption Erivo strongly rejects. She believes this characterization reflects deeper prejudices about how Black women are perceived.

"I think that we haven't really come to terms with the insidious nature of how we view Black women," Erivo told Variety. She explained that the mockery focused on her appearance, including her physique, shape, and shaved head. These observations led people to assume she was larger than Grande and therefore must have been controlling or protecting her rather than simply responding to danger.

"I would hazard a guess that it would not have been the same had it been the other way around," Erivo said, suggesting the backlash would have been different if Grande had intervened to help her.

The experience left Erivo feeling dehumanized. "I just felt like my humanity had been bastardised," she stated. The incident's aftermath made her reluctant to participate in traditional Oscar campaign activities for the sequel.

"It felt like something I did instinctively had been made to be something that it simply was not because of the way people see women who look like me, and because of the assumptions that are made," Erivo explained. She decided against pursuing an awards campaign because she did not want to subject herself to that treatment and felt she did not deserve to endure it.

Erivo emphasized that personal space deserves respect regardless of public status. "A stranger is a stranger. Personal space is still personal space. It doesn't belong to anyone, even if you feel you know the person," she noted.

The first Wicked film earned $765 million globally and won two Academy Awards. The sequel has underperformed commercially with $541 million worldwide and received no Oscar nominations. Despite the box office difference, Erivo's comments highlight the toll that public scrutiny and racial bias can take on performers of color during promotional cycles. Her willingness to discuss how these dynamics affected her mental health and career decisions contributes to ongoing conversations about equity in Hollywood's awards season.
