8:25 AM ET

The Justice Department will not proceed with an $1.8 billion fund proposed by the Trump administration to compensate people it believed were unjustly and politically targeted, according to Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche. The decision came after pressure from House lawmakers and marks a significant reversal of one of the administration's key initiatives.

The fund would have directed payments to individuals the Trump administration determined had been subjected to political prosecution or investigation. President Trump had framed the initiative as addressing what he called the weaponization of law enforcement and intelligence agencies against political opponents.

The reversal reflects growing discomfort among Republicans in Congress with the proposal. Lawmakers questioned both its substantial cost and the precedent it would establish. The fund's discretionary nature in determining who qualified for compensation raised concerns about oversight and accountability. Several Republican lawmakers argued the program lacked clear criteria and could be subject to political manipulation.

Trump has suggested he has not fully abandoned the concept, leaving open the possibility of pursuing similar compensation through alternative means. However, the Justice Department's decision effectively ends the current proposal in its original form.

The debate over the fund reflects broader tensions within the Republican Party over government spending and the appropriate response to perceived political targeting. Trump administration officials had argued the compensation was necessary to address what they described as years of politically motivated investigations. Critics contended the fund represented an inappropriate use of taxpayer money and lacked sufficient legal justification.

The decision to abandon the initiative indicates growing willingness among some Republicans to challenge Trump administration priorities on fiscal and procedural grounds. This pushback from within Republican ranks suggests that concerns about the fund's legitimacy ultimately prevailed within the administration's own Justice Department.

The $1.8 billion fund represented one of Trump's signature responses to his claims of being targeted by the federal government. The administration had promoted it as a necessary corrective measure to address what it viewed as weaponized investigations and prosecutions. However, the lack of unified support within the Republican Party, combined with concerns about how recipients would be selected and paid, proved insurmountable obstacles.

The reversal demonstrates that despite Trump's influence over the Republican Party, significant disagreements remain on specific policy proposals. Some Republican lawmakers prioritized fiscal responsibility and procedural concerns over alignment with Trump's broader narrative about political persecution. The decision to scrap the fund shows these lawmakers were willing to publicly oppose a Trump administration initiative when they felt it lacked adequate safeguards or justification.

While Trump has indicated the concept may not be entirely dead, the formal end to the $1.8 billion fund proposal represents a concrete defeat for the administration on this particular policy matter.

Oil prices rallied sharply as investors reacted to escalating tensions between the United States and Iran, with markets pricing in a 70% probability of a potential American strike on Tehran. Both Brent crude and West Texas Intermediate crude rose 4% overnight, with Brent climbing 1.95% to $71.72 per barrel and WTI gaining 2.12% to $66.67 per barrel as of Thursday afternoon.

The price movements reflect concern over Iran's strategic position. As a major oil producer, Iran controls access to the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20% of global oil supply transits. Even limited disruption to shipping lanes could trigger an immediate supply shock that ripples across global markets.

Daniela Hathorn, a senior market analyst at capital.com, warns that the risks are substantial. "Oil markets are starting to price in higher risk as Iran remains a major producer, and more importantly, sits at the heart of the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20% of global oil supply transits," she noted. "Even limited disruption or credible threats to shipping lanes could cause an immediate supply shock."

Potential supply disruptions could have broader economic consequences. Shipping delays and global supply chain disruptions would likely impact inflation expectations and influence central bank interest rate decisions. Gold has also benefited from heightened geopolitical uncertainty, with spot gold prices up 0.23% at $4,991.58.

Despite energy market volatility, US stock indices have remained largely resilient, though showing signs of weakness as trading opened Thursday. The Dow fell 0.25% to 49,537 points, the S&P 500 dropped 0.35% to 6,857 points, and the Nasdaq declined 0.49% to 22,641 points. European markets extended losses, with the FTSE 100 down 0.6%, Germany's DAX down 0.8%, France's CAC 40 down 0.79%, and Spain's IBEX down 0.85%.

Hathorn suggests investor sentiment reflects either skepticism about imminent escalation or confidence that any conflict would remain short-lived. However, she warns this complacency could prove costly. "This muted response suggests that investors are either sceptical of imminent escalation or confident that any conflict would be short-lived. This complacency, however, raises the risk of a sharp repricing event," she explained.

If tensions escalate from rhetoric into military action, the consequences could be severe. Oil could spike rapidly, bond yields could rise due to inflation concerns, and equities could experience significant volatility. Market focus remains fixed on the White House for any signals of escalation as investors monitor developments in the Persian Gulf region.

The divergence between rising oil prices and relatively steady equity performance reflects the complex calculations investors are making as geopolitical risks intensify in a region crucial to global energy supplies.

The gubernatorial contest takes on added importance given that Iowa has been a Republican stronghold, with the party controlling the governor's mansion since 2011. Iowa's primary results on Tuesday set the stage for months of campaigning ahead of the November general election, which could significantly influence the balance of power in Congress and control of statehouses. While Iowa has leaned Republican for the past decade, Democrats see opportunities to be competitive in multiple races across the state, including three of its four House seats and the Senate election to replace retiring Republican senator Joni Ernst.

He defeated Zach Wahls, a state senator who positioned himself as an outsider challenging party establishment figures. The contest became contentious, with Wahls arguing that Turek represented Democratic elites while claiming to offer voters a different direction for the party.

On the Republican side, Ashley Hinson, a U.S. representative backed by Trump, won her primary to face the Democratic Senate nominee. Hinson will attempt to replace Ernst in November.
