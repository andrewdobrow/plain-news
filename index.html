According to reports, the proposed agreement would include a 60-day ceasefire extension during which Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz, a critical shipping route it has effectively blocked since the war began. Under the terms, Iran would clear mines from the strait and allow ships to pass freely. In exchange, the United States would lift its naval blockade on Iranian ports, allowing Iran to freely sell oil. Nuclear negotiations would be deferred to a later phase of talks.

The reported concessions from Washington have alarmed several Republican foreign policy hawks who had backed the initial military action against Iran. Senator Lindsey Graham, a Trump ally, expressed concern that accepting Iran's control over the Strait of Hormuz would represent a "major shift of the balance of power in the region." He questioned why the war began in the first place if the United States was willing to accept these terms. "It makes one wonder why the war started to begin with if these perceptions are accurate," Graham wrote.

Other Republican senators including Tom Cotton and Ted Cruz also voiced opposition to the emerging agreement. Cruz warned that allowing Iran to continue uranium enrichment while receiving billions in sanctions relief would be "a disastrous mistake."

However, Graham later reversed course, hailing Trump's proposal as "brilliant" and suggesting it could expand diplomatic agreements in the Middle East beyond Israel.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio called the progress "significant" on Sunday. Former Secretary of State Mike Pompeo, who served under Trump's first administration, criticized the proposed deal as "not remotely America First," comparing it unfavorably to the 2015 nuclear agreement that Trump previously abandoned.

The negotiations represent Trump's effort to end the conflict while managing domestic political considerations and international pressure. The exact timeline for reaching a final agreement remains unclear.

8:00 AM ET

A pharmaceutical treatment called ivonescimab demonstrated a significant survival benefit in patients with squamous cell lung cancer during a late-stage clinical trial conducted in China. The drug, developed by Chinese company Akeso in partnership with Summit Therapeutics, reduced the risk of death by 34% compared to standard treatment approaches.

The trial compared ivonescimab combined with chemotherapy against a control group receiving a PD-1 inhibitor paired with chemotherapy. Patients who received ivonescimab experienced longer survival times than those in the comparison group. The findings are specifically relevant to squamous cell lung cancer, which is a subtype of non-small cell lung cancer.

What distinguishes ivonescimab from existing treatments is its dual mechanism of action. The drug targets two separate pathways in the immune system simultaneously, whereas current PD-1 inhibitors focus on only one pathway. This approach has sparked considerable discussion within the pharmaceutical industry about whether it offers meaningful advantages over treatments already available to patients.

Akeso developed the drug in China, where the company is headquartered. Summit Therapeutics holds the rights to develop and commercialize ivonescimab in markets outside greater China, including the United States and Europe. Both companies are pursuing expanded use of the medication based on these trial results and ongoing research.

The 34% reduction in death risk represents a statistically significant improvement over the control treatment. The trial specifically examined patients receiving chemotherapy combinations, which represents a standard approach for treating many lung cancer patients.

Squamous cell carcinoma represents a substantial portion of non-small cell lung cancer cases worldwide. The introduction of new treatment options could influence clinical decision-making for physicians treating this patient population. Lung cancer continues to rank among the leading causes of cancer deaths globally, making advances in treatment particularly important.

The research team presented these results at a major medical conference where they attracted attention from oncologists and industry professionals. The presentation highlighted the potential for ivonescimab to become an important addition to the available immunotherapy options for lung cancer patients.

Moving forward, regulatory agencies will review the trial data to determine whether ivonescimab qualifies for approval in additional markets. Both Akeso and Summit Therapeutics are preparing submissions to health authorities based on these findings and supporting evidence from other ongoing studies. The approval process will ultimately determine whether the drug becomes available to a broader population of lung cancer patients seeking new treatment alternatives.
