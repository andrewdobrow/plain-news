5:17 PM ET

A Kenyan court has blocked the Trump administration from sending Americans exposed to Ebola to Kenya for isolation. The ruling prevents the US from using Kenya as a quarantine destination for its citizens who have had contact with the virus.

The United States has previously built specialized facilities within its own borders specifically for isolating people exposed to infectious diseases like Ebola. The country maintains the infrastructure and expertise to handle such cases domestically.

The court decision comes as the administration sought to use Kenya as an alternative location for quarantining exposed individuals. The proposed plan would have marked a departure from past practice, when the US constructed dedicated facilities on American soil to manage potential Ebola cases.

Ebola is a severe viral disease that spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected people. Outbreaks have primarily occurred in parts of Africa, though cases have occasionally appeared elsewhere through international travel. The virus causes fever, severe bleeding, and organ failure, with fatality rates that can exceed 50 percent in some outbreaks.

The US has dealt with Ebola cases before by building containment units at medical facilities. During the 2014-2016 West Africa outbreak, American healthcare workers who contracted Ebola while working abroad were transported back to the US for treatment in specially designed isolation units.

The Kenyan court ruling effectively requires the US to handle quarantine and isolation of its own citizens exposed to the disease. Kenya has not publicly stated its reasons for opposing the arrangement through legal channels, but the court sided with objections to hosting potentially infected Americans.

This legal block creates logistical questions for how the US will manage future cases of Americans exposed to Ebola in regions where outbreaks occur. The administration has not announced alternative plans following the court decision.

6:30 PM ET

A federal judge has ordered the removal of Donald Trump's name from the Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, ruling that the Washington DC venue cannot be renamed without an act of Congress.

US District Judge Christopher Cooper issued a 94-page ruling on Friday stating that it was "crystal clear" the performing arts complex was named for the late President John F. Kennedy. The judge directed the Trump administration to remove all physical signage bearing Trump's name and eliminate any references to a "Trump Kennedy Center" from official materials within 14 days.

The decision also blocked the Trump administration's plan to close the Kennedy Center for two years of extensive renovations. The judge found that the Kennedy Center board violated legal requirements when it voted to approve both the renaming and the operational shutdown.

The Kennedy Center was established by Congress and operates under specific statutory requirements that govern how it functions as the nation's cultural center in Washington. The facility hosts theater, dance, orchestral performances and other arts programming. The court determined that changes to the building's name and operational status violated these legal requirements.

Following the court defeat, Trump responded by saying he has "no interest" in the Kennedy Center. The president's initial plans had included substantial changes to the venue, with the two-year renovation period set to shut down performances and events during that time.

The ruling addresses two separate issues that the Kennedy Center board had approved: the renaming of the building and the planned closure for renovations. The judge found legal problems with both decisions.

Trump announced his intention to withdraw from involvement with the Kennedy Center after the judicial decision. The administration has not indicated whether it will appeal the ruling or pursue alternative plans for the facility.

The Kennedy Center serves as the national performing arts venue in Washington, hosting major cultural events and programming throughout the year. Congress created the institution and established the legal framework under which it operates, which the judge cited in blocking the administration's proposed changes.

The decision means the Kennedy Center must continue operating under its established name and maintain its current programming schedule. The two-year closure plan cannot proceed under the court's current ruling.

This case represents a significant legal setback for the Trump administration's effort to reshape the iconic arts institution. The judge's interpretation of the legal requirements governing the Kennedy Center establishes that Congressional action would be necessary for any future name changes to the building.

Oil prices dropped below $100 a barrel as investors responded to reports that the United States and Iran are moving closer to a potential peace agreement. Brent crude futures, which serve as the global oil benchmark, fell 6 percent to $97.43 per barrel, marking the lowest level in two weeks. The decline reflected market optimism that a deal to end the conflict could be reached.

Stock markets rose on the news. Japan's Nikkei index climbed nearly 3 percent, while the pan-European Stoxx 600 index gained 1 percent. Several major markets, including the United States and United Kingdom, were closed for a public holiday on Monday.

Despite progress in negotiations, significant obstacles remain. While a framework has been negotiated between Washington and Tehran, the two sides continue to disagree on critical issues. Iran's blockade of the Strait of Hormuz remains a major sticking point. An Iranian government spokesperson cautioned that an agreement was "not imminent," signaling that a deal is far from certain.

Warren Patterson, head of commodities strategy at ING, warned against overreacting to the diplomatic signals. "We've been at this stage before, only for talks to break down. Therefore, the market will likely be more cautious about overreacting," he told Reuters.

Even if negotiations succeed and the strait reopens, analysts expect the recovery to take considerable time. Damaged energy infrastructure in Qatar and other regions will require months to repair. Physical oil flows through the strategic waterway remain restricted, according to UBS analyst Giovanni Staunovo, who emphasized that actual changes to oil supply are what matter most for markets.

Shipping data showed limited signs of resumed activity. Two tankers carrying liquefied natural gas departed the strait on Monday heading to Pakistan and China, while an Iraqi crude supertanker that had been stranded for nearly three months left the Gulf for China on Saturday.

Current oil prices remain substantially higher than before the conflict began in late February, when Brent crude traded around $70 per barrel. The elevated prices have contributed to broader inflation concerns worldwide. Rising costs for oil, gas, fertilizer, and other materials are expected to drive food prices higher in coming months.

These inflation worries have shifted market expectations regarding interest rates. Before the Iran conflict, traders anticipated central bank rate cuts. Now they predict rate increases instead. Markets currently expect the Bank of England to raise rates twice this year.

Other markets responded positively to the ceasefire prospects. Gold climbed 1.46 percent to $4,574 per ounce, while the pound gained almost 0.6 percent against the dollar, reaching $1.3506. Stephen Innes, an independent analyst, noted that the market reaction reflected investors pricing in the possibility that a critical global energy chokepoint could soon return to normal operations.

5:36 PM ET

A federal judge in Washington D.C. ordered President Trump to remove his name from the Kennedy Center within two weeks, ruling that the performing arts venue cannot be renamed without an act of Congress.

U.S. District Judge Christopher R. Cooper signed the order Friday, concluding that the Kennedy Center board exceeded its legal authority by unilaterally renaming the facility after Trump. In his 94-page ruling, Cooper wrote that it was "crystal clear" that Congress deliberately designated the arts complex as the "John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts" in 1964.

"The Court has concluded that the Board overstepped its statutory bounds by unilaterally renaming the Kennedy Center after President Trump," Cooper wrote in the decision.

Under the court order, Trump has 14 days to remove his name from all physical signage at the building and to eliminate any references to a "Trump Kennedy Center" from official materials.

The ruling also blocks the performing arts venue from implementing a planned two-year closure for extensive renovations. The closure had been scheduled to begin later this year. By halting this closure, at least temporarily, the Kennedy Center will continue its current programming while the legal dispute continues.

The decision represents a significant setback to the administration's effort to renovate the performing arts venue. The Kennedy Center board had previously approved adding Trump's name to the facility as part of these renovation plans.

Members of the Kennedy family celebrated the ruling, which came on Friday, the birthday of President John F. Kennedy. "An appropriate birthday present on my uncle's birthday today," wrote Maria Shriver, JFK's niece. "A federal judge ruled that President Trump and the Kennedy Center Board acted unlawfully in renaming the Kennedy Center. The judge held that only Congress can change the Center's name and blocked the planned two-year closure."

Kerry Kennedy, Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s daughter, wrote on social media: "Perhaps I won't need that pickaxe after all. What a great way to celebrate you on your birthday, Uncle Jack!"

Robert F. Kennedy Jr., Trump's health secretary, made no immediate public comment on the ruling.

Joe Kennedy III, a former congressman and grandson of Robert F. Kennedy, had previously called the name change illegal. "The Kennedy Center is a living memorial to a fallen president and named for President Kennedy by federal law," he wrote in December when the renaming was first announced. "It can no sooner be renamed than can someone rename the Lincoln Memorial, no matter what anyone says."

The judge's decision examined the statutory framework governing the Kennedy Center and determined that the board lacked the authority to change the facility's name without additional congressional authorization.
