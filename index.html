The explosion occurred at Launch Complex 36 on Thursday night at 9pm ET, creating a massive fireball visible from over 115 miles away in Fort Pierce.

The incident happened seconds after the start of the scheduled "hotfire" test. The explosion was powerful enough to shake homes in nearby Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach, with shockwaves felt across Florida's space coast. Residents in South Carolina, hundreds of miles north, reported seeing a glow in the sky from the blast. Flames continued burning at the launchpad more than two hours after the explosion.

Blue Origin described the incident as an "anomaly" and confirmed that all employees were safe with no injuries reported. Bezos acknowledged the setback in a tweet, stating it was "a very rough day" but expressing determination to rebuild and continue operations.

The explosion carries serious implications for NASA's lunar program. On Tuesday, just before the blast, NASA had announced that Blue Origin won a contract to launch the first of three planned missions this year to begin construction of a $20 billion moon base. The New Glenn rocket is the vehicle Blue Origin intends to use for launching lunar landers to the moon for NASA, including landers that will carry astronauts to the lunar surface.

The incident also threatens NASA's timeline for returning humans to the moon within the next two years. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman stated that a full evaluation of the impact on the Artemis program would be conducted following a thorough investigation. Blue Origin is currently competing with SpaceX to provide a lunar lander for the Artemis IV mission scheduled for 2028, which would mark the first crewed lunar landing since 1972.

This represents the latest in a series of setbacks for Blue Origin. Last month, a payload from the third flight of New Glenn ended up in the wrong orbit, leading the Federal Aviation Administration to temporarily ground the rocket. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared the company to return to flight.

Blue Origin has invested heavily in new facilities at Cape Canaveral to support crewed and cargo missions with NASA. The company also operates its New Shepard program, which conducts suborbital flights carrying paying customers to the edge of space.

The explosion underscores the challenges of developing heavy-lift launch capability. Isaacman noted that spaceflight is "unforgiving" and developing new rockets is "extraordinarily difficult." The incident is expected to delay Blue Origin's timeline for beginning commercial operations with the New Glenn system and potentially impact other scheduled missions.

6:58 PM ET

The US government has proposed new funding rules that would allow it to cancel federal research grants at any time. The proposed changes would also make peer review optional for grant applications and introduce political staff screening of grants for forbidden topics.

The proposed rules represent a departure from current practices in federal research funding. Under existing procedures, peer review serves as a standard evaluation mechanism for grant applications, with awards typically protected from arbitrary cancellation once approved.

The new framework would give federal agencies broad discretion to terminate grants without the traditional academic review process that has guided research funding decisions. Political staff would gain authority to examine grant proposals and ongoing projects for content deemed forbidden.

The proposal affects how billions of dollars in federal research funding would be allocated and managed across universities, research institutions, and other recipients of government grants. Federal agencies fund a wide range of scientific research, from basic laboratory studies to large scale projects in medicine, physics, and other disciplines.

Research institutions and scientists have historically relied on peer review to ensure that funding decisions rest on scientific merit rather than political considerations. The peer review process involves evaluation by independent experts in the relevant field who assess the quality and potential impact of proposed research.

The proposed changes would alter this arrangement by making peer review optional rather than mandatory. This would give federal administrators more direct control over which projects receive funding and which can be terminated.

The screening process for forbidden topics would add another layer of review beyond scientific merit. Political staff would examine grant applications and active projects to identify research areas or subjects that federal officials want to exclude from funding.

The proposal has not yet been finalized or implemented. Federal rule changes typically go through a public comment period before taking effect, allowing affected parties to submit feedback on proposed regulations.

The proposed rules represent a departure from current practices in federal research funding. Under existing procedures, peer review serves as a standard evaluation mechanism for grant applications, with awards typically protected from arbitrary cancellation once approved.

The new framework would give federal agencies broad discretion to terminate grants without the traditional academic review process that has guided research funding decisions. Political staff would gain authority to examine grant proposals and ongoing projects for content deemed forbidden.

The proposal affects how billions of dollars in federal research funding would be allocated and managed across universities, research institutions, and other recipients of government grants. Federal agencies fund a wide range of scientific research, from basic laboratory studies to large scale projects in medicine, physics, and other disciplines.

Research institutions and scientists have historically relied on peer review to ensure that funding decisions rest on scientific merit rather than political considerations. The peer review process involves evaluation by independent experts in the relevant field who assess the quality and potential impact of proposed research.

The proposed changes would alter this arrangement by making peer review optional rather than mandatory. This would give federal administrators more direct control over which projects receive funding and which can be terminated.

The screening process for forbidden topics would add another layer of review beyond scientific merit. Political staff would examine grant applications and active projects to identify research areas or subjects that federal officials want to exclude from funding.

The proposal has not yet been finalized or implemented. Federal rule changes typically go through a public comment period before taking effect, allowing affected parties to submit feedback on proposed regulations.
