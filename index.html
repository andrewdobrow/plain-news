7:38 PM ET

Nine people have died following a chemical tank rupture at a paper mill in Longview, Washington, with two workers still unaccounted for. The disaster at Nippon Dynawave Packaging Co occurred on Tuesday morning when an industrial tank ruptured and released white liquor, a highly corrosive chemical mixture used in the paper industry.

Crews recovered the remains of victims throughout the week as search and recovery operations continued at the site. The initial incident claimed one life immediately, with nine others missing and presumed dead. Seven workers remained hospitalized following the blast, including a firefighter who responded to the scene.

Governor Bob Ferguson said authorities were "bracing ourselves for this being the deadliest industrial tragedy in modern Washington state history." Officials transitioned operations from rescue to recovery after determining there was no hope of finding additional survivors.

The chemical released during the rupture was white liquor, made mostly of sodium hydroxide and sodium sulfide. This substance is used with heat to break down wood in the production of kraft paper, a durable material used in packaging and shopping bags. The tank held approximately 600,000 gallons of the liquid before the rupture, with about 25,000 gallons remaining and slowly leaking afterward.

Following the rupture, the liquid spilled into a drainage ditch and contaminated the Columbia River. Dead carp were found in the water, and authorities urged the public to avoid ditches and dikes in the area while water testing continued. However, officials reported no issues with air quality or drinking water in Longview, a city of about 40,000 people.

The sprawling Longview plant employs approximately 1,000 people and produces material for tissues, printing paper, cups, plates and cartons. Matt Amos with the Longview fire department said recovery operations would be "slow, methodical and deliberate," with recovered victims undergoing decontamination before transportation to the coroner.

A team of investigators from the US Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board arrived to launch a federal investigation into the incident. The cause remained unknown at the time of reporting.

Safety complaints were filed against Nippon Dynawave in March and May, though the state's labor and industries department said both were unrelated to the current situation. The company had been fined $3,400 for three separate health and safety violations since the start of 2021.

During a news conference, Brian Wood with Nippon Dynawave said the company was focused on the victims and would "cooperate to the maximum extent" with the investigation. "These are our people," he said.

The incident marked the second notable chemical tank failure on the west coast in days, following evacuations of thousands of residents in southern California due to a damaged tank at an aerospace plant.

The drone impact represents a sharp escalation in tensions between Russia and NATO. Russian officials responded by warning European countries to prepare for more drone incidents. The warning followed the apartment building strike but offered no explanation for the increased threat.

Drones from the war in Ukraine have increasingly posed threats to Romania despite the country not being directly involved in the conflict. The incident adds to a pattern of drones crossing into Romanian airspace from the nearby conflict zone. 4:36 PM ET

A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded on the launch pad at Cape Canaveral in Florida during a scheduled test, destroying the launchpad and threatening NASA's ambitious timeline for lunar exploration. The massive fireball erupted seconds after the "hotfire" test began at 9pm ET on Thursday, with the orange glow visible more than 100 miles away in Fort Pierce.

The explosion sent shockwaves across Florida's space coast, with residents in Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach reporting that their homes shook from the blast. The incident was visible from nearby beaches, and observers hundreds of miles north in South Carolina reported seeing a glow in the sky. Emergency officials confirmed there was no threat from hazardous fumes, though flames continued burning at the launch complex for more than two hours after the explosion.

Blue Origin founder Jeff Bezos confirmed on social media that all employees were safe and accounted for. He described it as "a very rough day" and stated the company was already working to determine the root cause. The company referred to the incident as "an anomaly." Bezos said, "It's too early to know the root cause but we're already working to find it. We'll rebuild whatever needs rebuilding and get back to flying. It's worth it."

The explosion carries serious implications for NASA's lunar program. Blue Origin recently won a contract to launch the first of three planned missions this year to construct NASA's 20 billion dollar moon base. The New Glenn rocket is intended to launch landers to the moon for NASA, including those that will carry astronauts to the lunar surface. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman stated that the agency would conduct a full evaluation of the timeline for the Artemis program following the incident, pledging to work with partners on a thorough investigation.

This explosion represents another setback for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA's Artemis contracts. Last month, a payload from the third New Glenn flight ended up in the wrong orbit, leading the Federal Aviation Administration to temporarily ground the rocket. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared the vehicle to return to flight the previous week.

Blue Origin and SpaceX are both competing to provide lunar landers for upcoming NASA missions, with Artemis III scheduled for 2027 to test both companies' landing systems. The successful lander will then be used for the Artemis IV mission in 2028, which aims to return astronauts to the moon for the first time since 1972.

Elon Musk, whose SpaceX is Blue Origin's primary competitor, commented briefly on the explosion, saying "Most unfortunate" and adding "Rockets are hard."

Proposed federal rules would allow government to cancel research grants at any time

The changes represent a shift in how the government awards and oversees scientific resear...

6:58 PM ET

The federal government has proposed new funding rules that would allow officials to terminate research grants at any time without cause. The proposed changes would eliminate mandatory peer review for some grants and introduce political staff screening of applications for forbidden topics.

The changes represent a shift in how the government awards and oversees scientific research funding. Under current rules, grants go through peer review processes and carry protections against arbitrary cancellation.

The proposed rules would give federal officials discretion to cancel grants after they have been awarded. Political staff would screen grant applications to identify research on topics the administration considers off limits.

Peer review, the traditional method of evaluating grant applications through assessment by independent scientific experts, would become optional rather than required under the proposal. This would change practices that have been standard in federal research funding for decades.

The proposal applies to federal research grants, which fund work at universities, research institutions, and other organizations across the country. These grants support scientific research in fields including medicine, climate science, physics, and engineering.

Researchers rely on multi-year grants to plan and conduct studies. The ability to cancel grants at any time could affect ongoing research projects and make long-term scientific work harder to complete.

The rules would also establish a process for political appointees to review grant applications before funding decisions are made. This screening would look for research topics that conflict with administration policies or priorities.

Federal research funding has historically been insulated from direct political control through peer review and merit-based selection. The proposed changes would reduce those protections and give political officials more direct authority over which research gets funded and whether it continues.

7:20 PM ET

Marcia Lucas, the film editor who won an Academy Award for editing Star Wars, died Wednesday in Rancho Mirage, California. She was 80.

The family's attorney confirmed she died of cancer. Lucas edited the original 1977 Star Wars film directed by her former husband George Lucas.

Lucas shaped several influential films of the 1970s beyond Star Wars. Her editing work also included American Graffiti, contributing to the success of multiple landmark films from that era.

She won the Oscar for her editing work on Star Wars, which became one of the most successful and culturally significant films in cinema history. Her contributions to the film helped establish its pacing and narrative structure.

The family's attorney described her as a brilliant storyteller and a trailblazer in the film industry. Lucas worked during a time when few women held prominent editing positions in major Hollywood productions.

Lucas was married to George Lucas during the production of his early films. The couple later divorced, but her professional legacy remained tied to the films that defined a generation of moviegoers.

Her editing career demonstrated technical skill and narrative instinct that influenced how science fiction and adventure films were constructed. The success of Star Wars helped establish new standards for pacing and visual storytelling in Hollywood.

Lucas lived in Rancho Mirage in her later years. She had been battling cancer before her death on Wednesday.

Beyond her Oscar win, Lucas's work on American Graffiti also earned recognition. That film, released in 1973, became a critical and commercial success that helped launch George Lucas's directing career before Star Wars.

Her death marks the loss of a key figure from the creative team behind some of the most memorable films of the 1970s. The editing techniques she employed on Star Wars influenced generations of editors who followed.

Judge orders Trump's name removed from Kennedy Center

A federal judge in Washington, D.C. ruled that President Trump's name must be removed from the Kennedy Center, blocking the administration's planned renovation of the performing arts venue. U.S. District Judge signed the order requiring Trump's name to be stripped from the building and surrounding signage within two weeks. The ruling also halted the scheduled closure of the fa...

5:36 PM ET

A federal judge in Washington, D.C. ruled that President Trump's name must be removed from the Kennedy Center, blocking the administration's planned renovation of the performing arts venue.

U.S. District Judge signed the order requiring Trump's name to be stripped from the building and surrounding signage within two weeks. The ruling also halted the scheduled closure of the facility, which had been set to shut down later this year to begin work.

The judge wrote in a 94-page decision that it was crystal clear the arts complex was named for President John F. Kennedy. The court found that the Kennedy Center's board exceeded its legal authority by acting on its own to rename the facility.

The ruling prevents the center from winding down its programming and closing for what had been planned as a two-year renovation period. The decision represents a setback for the Trump administration's plans to overhaul the venue.

The Kennedy Center serves as the nation's cultural center and memorial to President Kennedy, who was assassinated in 1963. Congress established the center through legislation that designated it as a living memorial to the 35th president.

The court determined that the board lacked statutory power to unilaterally change the name of the federally chartered institution. The judge's order applies to all Trump branding that had been added to the building and its grounds.

The administration now faces a deadline to comply with the removal order while its renovation plans remain on hold. The ruling leaves open questions about whether and how the planned improvements to the facility might proceed under its original name.
