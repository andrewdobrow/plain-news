7:18 AM ET

The Department of Homeland Security is developing plans to restrict international arrivals at San Francisco International Airport and potentially Newark Liberty International Airport, according to multiple reports. The proposal represents an effort to pressure jurisdictions that limit local cooperation with federal immigration enforcement.

The potential restrictions have prompted warnings from airlines and business groups about widespread travel disruption. Industry representatives cautioned that blocking international flights at major airports could create chaos for travelers and commerce.

The measures appear aimed at sanctuary jurisdictions, cities and counties that have policies limiting how local law enforcement assists federal immigration authorities. San Francisco and Newark are both located in areas with such policies.

The proposal faces significant opposition and logistical challenges. Critics have raised concerns about the economic impact on affected regions and the practical difficulties of redirecting international air traffic. Airlines warned that rerouting flights would strain capacity at other airports and disrupt travel schedules.

Questions have also emerged about how the restrictions would affect the World Cup, though specific details about that interaction remain unclear based on discussions reported in media coverage.

The Department of Homeland Security confirmed it is drawing up plans for the restrictions but has not announced a final decision or timeline for implementation. The agency oversees Customs and Border Protection, which processes international arrivals at U.S. airports.

San Francisco International Airport serves as a major gateway for trans-Pacific travel and handles millions of international passengers annually. Newark Liberty is one of three major airports serving the New York metropolitan area and plays a similar role for transatlantic flights.

The restrictions would represent an unprecedented use of federal authority over air travel to enforce immigration policy. Previous administrations have not attempted to block international arrivals at specific airports as a pressure tactic against local governments.

Business groups expressed alarm at the potential economic consequences. International flight restrictions could affect tourism, business travel, and cargo operations at the targeted airports, with ripple effects throughout their regional economies.

6:53 AM ET

An Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo's Ituri province has spiraled into a public health crisis, with nearly 1,000 suspected cases and more than 220 suspected deaths reported. The head of the World Health Organization has called for an immediate ceasefire among armed groups to help contain the disease.

Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, the WHO director general, traveled to the DRC this week, describing the situation as a "catastrophic collision of disease and conflict." The outbreak was confirmed on May 15 in Ituri, the nation's most northeastern province, which borders South Sudan and Uganda. As of the latest updates, 101 cases had been confirmed with authorities investigating more than 3,000 possible contacts.

The death rate is substantial. WHO officials estimate the mortality rate at between 30 percent and 50 percent among confirmed cases. Aid group Save the Children highlighted that a quarter of confirmed deaths were children, calling for urgent scaling up of infection prevention measures.

The mining economy of the region has contributed to the outbreak's spread. Ituri attracts migrant workers to its gold mines, making the population transient and harder to track. This movement has facilitated transmission to neighboring provinces, with cases now appearing in rebel-held areas of North Kivu and South Kivu.

Armed conflict in the region has severely complicated containment efforts. Medical teams face attacks on health facilities and widespread community distrust. Health workers have reported critical shortages of essential supplies including personal protective equipment, body bags, soap, and chlorine. A doctor in Butembo reported having only two body bags remaining and said staff had purchased additional supplies with their own money.

Cultural practices have also hindered the response. Traditional burial rituals involve families washing and touching bodies, but Ebola victims remain highly contagious after death. When authorities have restricted bodies for safe disposal, community members have attacked health facilities. In Mongbwalu, unidentified individuals burned patient isolation tents on Saturday, causing 18 Ebola patients to flee. Seven more patients escaped on Sunday, with one suspected case dying during the escape.

Uganda has responded to the threat by closing its border with the DRC for four weeks, except for Ebola response teams and humanitarian operations. The country had reported seven confirmed cases as of Sunday, with one death. Uganda authorities require anyone authorized to enter from the DRC to undergo mandatory self-isolation for 21 days.

The WHO has urged all warring parties to agree to a ceasefire, emphasizing that stopping Ebola transmission depends entirely on humanitarian access. Without it, ongoing clashes will continue driving mass displacement and severing critical containment corridors.

A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded during a static fire test at Cape Canaveral in Florida on Thursday evening, destroying the launch pad and potentially disrupting NASA's plans to return astronauts to the Moon within the next two years.

The explosion occurred at 9pm ET during the scheduled "hotfire" test at Kennedy Space Center's Launch Complex 36. A massive fireball engulfed the pad seconds after the test began, creating an orange sky visible from Fort Pierce, 115 miles away. Shockwaves from the blast were felt across Florida's space coast, with residents in nearby Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach reporting that their homes shook. The explosion was even visible to observers hundreds of miles north in South Carolina.

Blue Origin described the incident as "an anomaly" and confirmed that all employees were safe and accounted for. Company founder Jeff Bezos acknowledged the setback on social media, stating "It's too early to know the root cause but we're already working to find it" and pledging to "rebuild whatever needs rebuilding and get back to flying."

The New Glenn rocket is central to Blue Origin's role in NASA's lunar program. The company won a contract to launch lunar landers for NASA missions, and is competing with SpaceX to provide the lander for the Artemis IV mission scheduled for 2028, which would represent the first crewed Moon landing since the Apollo program ended in 1972. Artemis III, planned for 2027, will test Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander against SpaceX's Starship Human Landing System to determine which vehicle will transport astronauts from the Orion capsule to the lunar surface.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman stated that the agency would conduct a full evaluation of program timelines following the explosion. "Spaceflight is unforgiving, and developing new heavy-lift launch capability is extraordinarily difficult," he posted, adding that NASA would work with partners to support an investigation and assess near-term mission impacts.

This explosion marks the latest challenge for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA contracts. The company faced previous setbacks, including a payload placement error during a recent flight that resulted in temporary grounding by the Federal Aviation Administration. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared the rocket to resume operations.

Flames continued burning at the launch pad more than two hours after the explosion. Emergency officials reported no immediate hazards to surrounding areas from fumes or other potential dangers. Blue Origin has constructed large new facilities near Cape Canaveral to support crewed and cargo missions in partnership with NASA, but the company now faces an investigation into the explosion's cause before resuming launch operations.

The Trump administration wants to shift coal ash monitoring and enforcement responsibilities to state governments and allow states to bypass national standards. Environmental groups turned out in force to oppose the proposed rollbacks.

Coal ash is the waste material left behind after burning coal at power plants. The substance has been regulated at the federal level to prevent contamination of groundwater and other environmental hazards.

The proposed changes would reduce federal oversight by relying on individual states to monitor and enforce coal ash disposal rules. States would also gain the ability to set their own standards instead of adhering to uniform national requirements.

Environmental advocates organized significant opposition to the proposal. The turnout reflects concerns about potential weakening of protections that currently exist under federal regulations.

Coal ash contains heavy metals and other toxic substances that can leach into water supplies if not properly contained. Federal standards were established to create consistent safeguards across the country, particularly for communities near coal-fired power plants and disposal sites.

The shift to state-level control raises questions about enforcement capacity and consistency. Different states would be able to implement different levels of protection, potentially creating a patchwork of standards across the nation.

The proposal comes as the Trump administration has pursued various rollbacks of environmental regulations. Coal ash management has been a point of contention between industry groups seeking flexibility and environmental organizations pushing for stronger protections.

Public comment periods typically allow citizens and organizations to weigh in on proposed regulatory changes before they are finalized. The strong turnout from environmental groups indicates this proposal will face organized resistance during the review process.

The split between the two senior defense officials has drawn attention to how the Iran conflict is affecting America's ability to support its allies.

Trump won the 2024 election largely on promises to reduce inflation.

Neither Hegseth nor Cao has provided specific details about current stockpile levels or the particular weapons systems included in the Taiwan package. The disagreement became public when Hegseth responded to Cao's Senate testimony.

The dispute underscores broader challenges facing the Pentagon as it balances immediate military needs in the Middle East with longer-term commitments to support allies in Asia. Taiwan's defense capabilities remain central to broader U.S. strategic interests in the region.
