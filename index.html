The House rejected a war powers resolution on Thursday that would have required President Trump to withdraw military forces from hostilities in Lebanon without congressional authorization. The chamber voted 324-92-2 against the measure, with significant Republican opposition.

Two hundred and seven Republicans and 117 Democrats voted to reject the resolution, along with one independent. Rep. Thomas Massie introduced the legislation seeking to limit the Trump administration's authority in Lebanon. The vote highlighted divisions within the Democratic Party, with party leadership opposing the effort.

War powers resolutions rely on the War Powers Act, a Vietnam War-era law that allows Congress to compel the president to remove armed forces from unauthorized hostilities abroad. The Constitution grants Congress the power to declare war, though presidents have frequently deployed troops without formal declarations or legislative approval.

The vote occurred as the United States maintains a military presence in Lebanon without explicit congressional authorization for military operations there. Those supporting the resolution argued that the deployment went beyond presidential authority. Opponents contended that the military engagement served national security interests and did not require additional approval from lawmakers.

The rejection of the measure leaves current deployments in place unchanged. This marks another instance in which Congress has declined to exercise its war powers authority to restrict presidential military action. The Trump administration has not publicly responded to the vote.

The outcome reflects broader patterns in Congress regarding war powers oversight. While some lawmakers have pushed back against military deployments, particularly those without prior authorization or formal declarations, Republicans have largely declined to impose checks on Trump through war powers votes.

The debate over the Lebanon resolution fits into a wider conversation about presidential power and military intervention that has intensified during Trump's second term. Democrats have attempted to force votes on multiple war powers resolutions as the administration has increased military activities in various regions and discussed potential military operations elsewhere.

Rep. Rashida Tlaib introduced the Lebanon measure, seeking to constrain executive authority in the region. Democratic leaders in the House opposed the resolution despite it being sponsored by a member of their party, signaling internal disagreement about how aggressively to challenge Trump on foreign policy matters.

The war powers votes reflect longstanding constitutional tensions over who controls military decisions. The Framers intended Congress to declare war and authorize military force, but modern presidents across parties have increasingly asserted independent authority to deploy troops. Congressional efforts to reclaim this power through war powers resolutions have met with limited success, particularly when one party controls the chamber.

The failed Lebanon resolution suggests that reasserting congressional war powers authority remains difficult in the current political environment, despite growing concerns among some lawmakers about military expansion overseas.

Public health departments lose enforcement powers following pandemic backlash

4:36 PM ET

Public health authorities across the United States have seen their powers curtailed as state and local governments respond to criticism of measures taken during the COVID-19 pandemic. The changes affect the ability of health departments to impose lockdowns, mandate masks, require vaccinations, and close schools during disease outbreaks.

The rollback comes after widespread public pushback against restrictions implemented to control the spread of COVID-19. Many jurisdictions have revised laws and regulations that previously granted broad emergency powers to public health officials. The changes vary by location but collectively represent a significant shift in how local and state governments can respond to infectious disease threats.

Public health experts warn that the weakened authorities could increase risks during future outbreaks. The ability to act quickly during health emergencies has traditionally been considered a core function of public health departments. Restrictions on these powers may slow response times when new infectious diseases emerge or existing diseases spread rapidly through communities.

The debate reflects ongoing tensions between individual liberty and collective public health measures. Critics of pandemic-era restrictions argued that lockdowns and mandates caused economic damage, disrupted education, and infringed on personal freedoms. Supporters of strong public health powers contend that such measures save lives during outbreaks and that elected officials should defer to health experts during medical emergencies.

The legal changes create a patchwork of public health authority across the country. Some jurisdictions now require legislative approval for measures that health officials could previously implement independently. Others have placed time limits on emergency declarations or restricted the types of interventions health departments can mandate. These variations mean that neighboring communities may have vastly different capacities to respond to the same health threat.

This valuation would give the company a market value of approximately $1.77 trillion, according to recent reporting. Such a scale underscores the significance of the transaction and explains why senior Wall Street executives are personally involved in the pitch process.

The company operates a fleet of reusable rockets that launch satellites, conduct government contracts with NASA, and resupply the International Space Station. The company's financial performance supports its enormous valuation. SpaceX reported annual revenues of $15.5 billion, with approximately $1.1 billion coming from NASA contracts. Recent private funding rounds valued the business at between $400 billion and $800 billion before this public offering.

The decision to go public represents a notable shift for Musk, who has previously expressed concerns about operating companies in public markets due to investor scrutiny and short-term pressure. SpaceX has remained private since its founding, relying on private capital raises to fuel its expansion and ambitious projects.

The timing aligns with broader expectations of significant technology sector IPOs in coming months. Other high-profile companies like OpenAI and Anthropic are also reportedly considering public listings, with valuations reaching into the hundreds of billions and trillions of dollars. Industry analysts refer to these mega-valued startups as "hectocorns" due to their valuations exceeding $100 billion.

For Wall Street banks, the SpaceX transaction represents extraordinary fee potential. Investment banks typically earn a percentage of capital raised in IPOs, meaning a $75 billion offering would generate substantial revenue for the managing institutions.

According to one analyst, while Musk's Mars colonization ambitions capture headlines, investors will focus on more concrete growth plans. These include expanding infrastructure such as solar farms and data centers in orbit, which could diversify SpaceX's revenue streams beyond traditional launch services.

The SpaceX IPO will serve as a bellwether for the broader technology sector, testing whether investor enthusiasm for transformative companies can sustain valuations in the trillions of dollars.

Helion, a fusion energy company backed by OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, has secured $465 million in funding to construct a power plant for Microsoft. The startup is working toward completing the facility by 2028 as part of an agreement with the technology company.

Fusion energy operates by combining atomic nuclei to produce large amounts of power, replicating the process that fuels the sun. This differs from conventional nuclear fission, which splits atoms. Fusion generates significantly less radioactive waste and carries lower safety risks compared to traditional nuclear energy. Helion is developing technology that uses pulsed magnetic fields to compress and heat plasma to the temperatures needed for fusion reactions.

The new funding will support construction of the power plant designed to supply electricity directly to Microsoft. The 2028 target represents an ambitious deadline within an industry where commercial fusion power has consistently remained distant despite decades of research and substantial investment. Several fusion companies have attracted billions in venture capital recently, wagering that progress in materials science, magnet technology, and computing power will enable the technology to become commercially practical.

As part of its strategy to meet increasing energy demands from data centers and artificial intelligence systems, Microsoft has committed to purchasing electricity from the Helion facility. Large technology companies have increasingly explored alternative energy sources, including small modular nuclear reactors and enhanced geothermal systems, to power growing infrastructure while advancing carbon reduction targets.

While Helion previously announced its partnership with Microsoft, the company had not revealed the financial details necessary for plant construction. The $465 million funding round ranks among the largest in the fusion sector, demonstrating investor confidence in both Helion's technical method and its capacity to achieve the Microsoft deadline.

The broader context for this investment reflects significant recent activity in fusion energy development. Multiple major technology companies and AI leaders have begun investing heavily in fusion startups. This surge in interest stems from growing electricity demands from artificial intelligence and data centers, creating pressure on power grids worldwide.

Whether Helion successfully meets its 2028 timeline will likely influence how investors view fusion energy's potential as a near-term power solution. The company joins other fusion developers working to transition the technology from experimental phases to commercial operations. Success or failure at this stage could shape the trajectory of investment and development across the entire fusion sector.

3:51 PM ET

Marjane Satrapi, the French-Iranian artist and graphic novelist whose acclaimed memoir Persepolis reshaped international understanding of Iran, has died at age 56. Her family stated that she "died of sadness" following the death of her husband, Swedish producer Mattias Ripa, who passed away on April 8, 2023. Later that month, messages posted on Satrapi's Instagram account included the phrase: "For I lost the love of my life."

Born in 1969 in Rasht, Iran, Satrapi grew up in Tehran with her father, an engineer, and her mother, a dress designer. As a teenager, her parents sent her to Europe to continue her education and escape the restrictions of the Islamic Republic. She eventually settled in France in 1994 and became a French citizen in 2006.

Satrapi published Persepolis in 2000, a four-volume graphic memoir that became an international publishing success. The work chronicled her childhood during Iran's 1979 revolution and the subsequent Iran-Iraq War, following a rebellious young girl as she navigated the country's violence and ideological control before being sent alone to Europe at age 14. The memoir sold millions of copies and established Satrapi as one of the most widely read Iranian authors globally. She initially had modest expectations for the project, telling El País in 2020: "With Persepolis, I didn't even think I'd find a publisher. I thought I'd make 50 photocopies for my friends to read."

Satrapi co-directed the animated film adaptation of Persepolis, which became an international success. She made history as the first woman nominated for the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature. She later directed five feature films, including Radioactive in 2019, starring Rosamund Pike as physicist Marie Curie.

Regarding her choice of medium, Satrapi said: "Drawing it's the first language of human beings, before writing, before even talking, before words." She described the purpose of her work as reassuring young Iranians they were heard and supported globally, stating: "If they kill you and the whole world doesn't care, how is that? This is the whole thing I'm asking: just recognise this."

In 2024, Satrapi returned to comics by coordinating Woman, Life, Freedom, a collaborative graphic work featuring 17 Iranian and international comic artists alongside academics. The book examined protests following the 2022 death in custody of Mahsa Amini, a 22-year-old Kurdish-Iranian woman detained for allegedly failing to comply with mandatory headscarf rules. Discussing the project, Satrapi said: "The only thing I can do is cultural work. This book is a message to the Iranian people to say, listen, you are not alone."

Her death prompted tributes from French political leaders and international figures. President Emmanuel Macron called her "a great artist who turned her Iranian childhood into a universal tale." Margaret Atwood noted that Satrapi "lived" experiences of repression that Atwood had only written about, describing her work as increasingly pertinent given Iran's current circumstances.

As a teenager, her parents sent her to Europe to continue her education and escape the restrictions of the Islamic Republic. She eventually settled in France in 1994 and became a French citizen in 2006.

Satrapi published Persepolis in 2000, a four-volume graphic memoir that became an international publishing success. The work chronicled her childhood during Iran's 1979 revolution and the subsequent Iran-Iraq War, following a rebellious young girl as she navigated the country's violence and ideological control before being sent alone to Europe at age 14. The memoir sold millions of copies and established Satrapi as one of the most widely read Iranian authors globally. She initially had modest expectations for the project, telling El País in 2020: "With Persepolis, I didn't even think I'd find a publisher. I thought I'd make 50 photocopies for my friends to read."

Regarding her choice of medium, Satrapi said: "Drawing it's the first language of human beings, before writing, before even talking, before words." She described the purpose of her work as reassuring young Iranians they were heard and supported globally, stating: "If they kill you and the whole world doesn't care, how is that? In 2024, Satrapi returned to comics by coordinating Woman, Life, Freedom, a collaborative graphic work featuring 17 Iranian and international comic artists alongside academics. The book examined protests following the 2022 death in custody of Mahsa Amini, a 22-year-old Kurdish-Iranian woman detained for allegedly failing to comply with mandatory headscarf rules. Discussing the project, Satrapi said: "The only thing I can do is cultural work. This book is a message to the Iranian people to say, listen, you are not alone."
