Senate Majority Leader John Thune and other Republican senators say the stalled budget reconciliation package can now move forward after the Trump administration abandoned its proposal to create a $1.8 billion anti-weaponization fund. The fund had become a significant obstacle to passing the legislation intended to finance immigration enforcement operations.

The controversial fund faced mounting legal and political challenges since its announcement two weeks ago. A federal court temporarily blocked the administration from transferring money from the fund after a lawsuit was filed to dissolve it. Judge Leonie Brinkema in Virginia issued the temporary order following a legal challenge by Democracy Forward.

Republicans expressed serious concerns about the fund's structure and potential uses. The fund lacked clear oversight mechanisms, and administration officials acknowledged that pardoned January 6 rioters could receive payments from it. The terms did not require disclosure of how much money would be paid to whom.

Senate Democrats mounted fierce opposition to the fund. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer called it "corruption in broad daylight" and described it as Trump's "most brazen act of self-dealing yet." Democrats vowed to force Senate Republicans to vote on measures to block payments from the fund and demanded that records be preserved.

The fund was established as part of a settlement in Trump's lawsuit against the Internal Revenue Service over the leak of his tax returns. The Justice Department did not defend itself in the lawsuit, raising accusations among critics of collusion and corruption.

Republican opposition extended beyond Congress. Mike Pence, Trump's first-term vice president, called the possibility of compensating January 6 rioters "deeply offensive" and said this sentiment was widely shared among Republicans and Americans.

State officials also responded. California's governor proposed a 100 percent income tax on any distribution of the fund to California residents. Similar proposals emerged in Illinois, New York, and Connecticut.

According to reporting, Trump was reconsidering whether to continue pressing for the fund as the Justice Department paused the program to comply with the court order. The department stated it "disagrees strongly" with the court's decision but complied with the ruling.

Thune publicly called on the White House to reverse course and abandon the fund entirely. "I do think the best way to handle it is if the administration decides to shut the fund down themselves," he told reporters.

The administration's decision to drop the fund removes what Republicans characterized as a major barrier to passing the reconciliation package through Congress. With this obstacle removed, Senate Republicans indicate they can move forward on the budget measure.

In the Obolon district, cars burned after being hit by falling debris, and fires broke out near a kindergarten. The city's electricity supply was cut for 140,000 residents, though utility workers restored power to 110,000 by morning. Two engineers were injured during repair work.

The pattern of advance threats combined with delayed action appeared designed to create psychological pressure on civilians.

Following the strikes, Zelenskyy appealed directly to Donald Trump for Patriot missiles, describing them as vital to saving lives. He emphasized that ballistic missiles represent Russia's "last major advantage on the battlefield" and warned that without adequate air defences, such strikes would continue.

Ukraine's foreign minister, Andrii Sybiha, countered that Russia was running out of military options. He pointed to Ukraine's increasingly successful drone campaign targeting Russian oil refineries and ports, arguing that Moscow was losing ground despite its missile arsenal.

5:51 AM ET

A divided panel of appeals court judges has ruled that a Trump administration policy illegally banned transgender troops from military service, marking a significant legal setback for the Pentagon's implementation of restrictions on transgender service members.

The three-judge panel of the US Court of Appeals for Washington DC found that the policy violated federal law through its categorical exclusion of transgender individuals from serving in the armed forces. The majority opinion called the ban "arbitrary, and based on animus," according to The Guardian's reporting on the split decision.

However, the ruling contains a significant complication. While the court determined that current transgender service members can remain in the military, it allowed the armed services to continue blocking new transgender individuals from enlisting. This split outcome deals a blow to the Trump administration's anti-diversity agenda while simultaneously preventing full reinstatement of transgender recruitment.

The decision comes after years of legal battles over military policies affecting transgender personnel. The Trump administration had implemented the ban as part of broader changes to military service eligibility requirements, sparking fierce debate over civil rights protections and military readiness standards.

Legal observers expect the government to challenge the ruling, and the case is likely to reach the US Supreme Court eventually. The outcome will have significant consequences for how courts evaluate military policies that categorically exclude specific groups from service based on gender identity.

Transgender service members affected by the ban's restrictions on recruitment may now seek to challenge their discharge status or limitations on their service. Military advocacy groups have called the ruling a step forward, though the mixed nature of the decision means full victory remains uncertain.

The legal dispute has involved complex questions about medical standards, military readiness concerns, and civil rights protections under federal law. Different presidential administrations have taken varying approaches to transgender military service eligibility, creating an inconsistent policy landscape.

The court's decision reflects broader tensions between civil rights law and military personnel policies. While the majority found the ban violated federal law, the panel stopped short of requiring the military to actively recruit transgender individuals, leaving room for practical restrictions at the enlistment stage.

The ruling represents one of several recent court decisions shaping military personnel policies. These cases increasingly require courts to balance military institutional concerns against constitutional protections and federal civil rights law.

How the Pentagon implements this decision remains unclear. The Department of Defense has not yet announced whether it will appeal to a higher court or adjust its recruitment and retention policies accordingly. The decision may prompt military leadership to reconsider how eligibility standards are applied to transgender service members seeking to join or continue their military careers.

The case underscores ongoing constitutional questions about discrimination in federal employment and the limits of military deference in policy decisions affecting fundamental civil rights.

Alphabet said it plans to raise up to $80 billion in new equity to finance its artificial intelligence infrastructure buildout. The move represents one of the largest equity fundraisings ever attempted by a technology company. Berkshire Hathaway will purchase $10 billion of the offering, marking a significant deepening of its investment position in the tech giant.

The company explained that strong demand for its AI solutions from both enterprises and consumers is exceeding available supply. "AI is driving an expansionary moment for Alphabet," the company said in a statement. "The company is experiencing strong demand for its AI solutions and services from enterprises and consumers, at levels that are exceeding the company's available supply."

Half of the $80 billion will be used to scale AI infrastructure and global computing capacity. The remaining $40 billion is designated to cover administrative changes related to how the company meets tax obligations associated with employee equity vesting. The funding structure includes an initial $30 billion raise alongside the $10 billion from Berkshire Hathaway, plus a $40 billion flexible mechanism that can be deployed gradually over time.

Berkshire Hathaway's substantial investment signals institutional confidence in Alphabet's AI strategy, though the massive equity issuance will dilute existing shareholders. The company has previously stated that capital expenditures are expected to reach between $180 billion and $190 billion this year, with another significant increase anticipated in 2027.

The offering comes as technology companies race to secure capital for AI development. Alphabet competes directly with Microsoft, Amazon, and other major players in providing AI services to businesses and consumers. The company has invested heavily in its Gemini AI models and infrastructure to support AI applications across its product line.

Matt Britzman, a senior equity analyst at Hargreaves Lansdown, called the fundraising a clear sign that "the AI arms race is moving into a more capital-hungry phase." He noted that while $80 billion represents a substantial sum, it amounts to less than 2 percent of Alphabet's $4.6 trillion market capitalization. Britzman added that the investment case carries more substance than some AI spending stories where the path to returns is less clear, noting that demand for AI compute is running ahead of supply and Google Cloud growth has accelerated sharply.

Jim Reid, a market strategist at Deutsche Bank, said Alphabet was reminding investors of the unprecedented scale of AI spending. "Funding of the AI capital expenditure boom is becoming an increasingly key topic for markets," he said.

The fundraising announcement comes as other AI companies move toward public markets. Anthropic, the maker of the Claude chatbot, recently filed confidentially for an initial public offering. The company is now valued at $965 billion after raising $65 billion in funding.

His coaching career also included stops with the Golden State Warriors, Houston Rockets, and Minnesota Timberwolves. While Adelman never won an NBA championship, his sustained excellence across multiple teams earned him induction into the Naismith Memorial Basketball Hall of Fame.

Adelman became recognized throughout the basketball community for his emphasis on disciplined offensive systems and unselfish team play. His approach prioritized fundamentals and teamwork, earning widespread admiration from players and colleagues. Beyond his win-loss record, he developed a reputation as a mentor who valued treating players with respect and fostering their development both professionally and personally.

The basketball world reflected on Adelman's influence on the sport and the generations of players he worked with throughout his career. His impact extended beyond statistics, as former teammates and colleagues frequently cited his character and approach to coaching. The Trail Blazers particularly honored his memory as a legendary figure in franchise history, acknowledging his role in building winning cultures during his eight seasons in Portland.

Adelman's coaching philosophy stood out in an era of basketball marked by varying approaches to offense and defense. His teams were consistently competitive and well-organized, reflecting his meticulous preparation and strategic understanding of the game. His success across different franchises and with different rosters demonstrated his ability to adapt while maintaining core principles centered on efficiency and player development.

The loss of Adelman marks the passing of one of the NBA's most respected figures, someone whose contributions shaped multiple franchises and influenced countless players who competed under his leadership. His Hall of Fame status recognized not only his winning percentage but also his significant and lasting impact on professional basketball.
