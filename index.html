5:15 PM ET

The Pentagon has begun positioning military assets and infrastructure that could support an invasion of Cuba, according to a new report. The moves include increased surveillance flights off the Cuban coast and naval deployments to the Caribbean region.

The USS Nimitz, the oldest active supercarrier in the US Navy fleet, has arrived in the Caribbean as part of the deployments. Multiple surveillance flights have been tracked operating near Cuban territorial waters.

The military preparations represent a significant escalation in US posture toward Cuba. The deployments involve multiple branches of the armed forces, with both Navy and Marine Corps assets now positioned in the region.

The infrastructure being established could provide the logistical foundation needed to support a large-scale military operation. The positioning of carrier strike groups and surveillance aircraft creates the capability for sustained operations in the area.

The Pentagon has not publicly announced the purpose of the deployments or confirmed plans for military action against Cuba. The movements come amid broader changes in US foreign policy under the current administration.

The last major US military intervention in Cuba was the failed Bay of Pigs invasion in 1961. The US has maintained an economic embargo against Cuba for decades, though diplomatic relations were briefly normalized before being rolled back.

The current military buildup appears to go beyond routine training exercises or show-of-force operations that the US regularly conducts in international waters. The involvement of the Nimitz carrier group and the establishment of persistent surveillance patterns suggest preparations for potential sustained operations.

Cuba has not issued a public response to the US military movements. The island nation has limited military capabilities compared to US forces and relies primarily on its relationship with other countries for security guarantees.

The Trump administration had imposed the tariffs, which were later ruled illegal.

The demand for the customs chief to testify represents an unusual judicial intervention in the refund process. Courts typically do not require agency heads to appear for hearings unless there are serious questions about compliance with legal obligations.

The $166 billion sum represents the full amount owed to businesses and importers who paid tariffs that were subsequently found to violate legal requirements. The refund process has been ongoing, but the court's action suggests concerns that the full amount may not be returned as required.

The hearing will focus on whether the customs agency is meeting its legal obligations to process and distribute the refunds. The court's order did not specify particular deficiencies but indicated that testimony from the agency's top official was necessary to address compliance questions.

Businesses that paid the tariffs have been waiting for refunds since the legal ruling. The amounts owed vary widely depending on the volume and type of goods imported during the period when the illegal tariffs were in effect.

The customs chief will need to provide detailed information about the status of refund processing, including how much has been paid out, how many claims remain pending, and what timeline exists for completing the remaining payments. The hearing date has not been publicly announced.

This development adds pressure on the administration to demonstrate progress on the refund program. Legal experts note that court-ordered appearances by agency heads are reserved for cases where judges need direct answers about agency actions or inactions.

Nvidia announced plans to invest $150 billion per year to establish Taiwan as a global artificial intelligence hub. The commitment represents a significant expansion of the chipmaker's operations in the region and signals a strategic shift away from domestic manufacturing goals.

The investment reflects broader geopolitical competition over AI development infrastructure. The United States has pursued policies designed to increase chip production domestically, including subsidies for new manufacturing facilities. Nvidia's decision to concentrate substantial resources in Taiwan instead represents a notable divergence from those domestic priorities.

Nvidia has become the dominant supplier of processors used to train and operate AI systems. The company's datacenter business reported 92 percent year-over-year growth, reaching a record $75.2 billion in revenue. This growth reflects surging demand as companies across industries adopt AI technologies and build out massive datacenter infrastructure.

CEO Jensen Huang characterized the current moment as "the largest infrastructure expansion in human history." He noted that "agentic AI has arrived, doing productive work, generating real value, and scaling rapidly across companies and industries." The Taiwan expansion aims to secure production capacity to meet accelerating demand for chips that power this infrastructure buildout.

Taiwan already serves as a critical hub for semiconductor manufacturing, hosting major contract chipmakers. Nvidia's decision reinforces the island's essential position in the global technology supply chain. The company is also expanding elsewhere in the region, with Singapore announcing a new Nvidia research hub focused on increasing the efficiency of AI infrastructure.

The geopolitical context adds complexity to Nvidia's expansion plans. Huang recently joined Elon Musk and Donald Trump on a trip to China, where he expressed hope that Nvidia could expand operations there. However, current restrictions limit such exports. In December, the Trump administration allowed Nvidia to export certain AI chips to China while collecting a 25 percent fee on sales. Despite these developments, Nvidia's CFO Colette Kress stated on a recent earnings call that the company has not generated any revenue from Chinese chip sales and remains unclear whether additional imports will be permitted.

The annual investment figure places Nvidia among the largest corporate commitments to a single geographic region in recent years. The funds will support AI-focused development and production capabilities, though the company has not detailed exactly how the resources will be allocated across facilities, research operations, and other infrastructure.

Huang expects Nvidia will grow faster than the overall capital expenditure of major datacenters. He also predicted that the company will remain "supply-constrained throughout the entire life" of its upcoming Vera Rubin platform, which is expected to roll out in the second half of 2026. The chipmaker has characterized this system as a "generational leap" that will kick off unprecedented infrastructure expansion.

A group of senators introduced legislation that would regulate how college athletes are paid, restrict their ability to transfer between schools, and limit when coaches can change jobs during the season.

The bipartisan bill aims to bring structure to college sports compensation after recent court decisions and policy changes allowed athletes to profit from their name, image, and likeness. The proposed legislation would establish a salary cap system for payments to college players.

The bill also seeks to restrict the transfer portal, which has allowed athletes to switch schools with increasing frequency in recent years. Under the current system, players can enter the portal and move to different programs, sometimes multiple times during their college careers.

Additionally, the legislation would place limits on when coaches can move between programs during the active season. Coaching changes have become more common mid-season or immediately following bowl games, disrupting team continuity.

The bill represents an attempt by federal lawmakers to create uniform rules for college athletics as individual states have passed conflicting laws regarding athlete compensation. Without federal legislation, schools in different states operate under different regulations regarding how much and in what ways they can pay athletes.

The introduction of the bill comes as college sports conferences have undergone significant realignment, with schools moving between leagues in pursuit of better media rights deals and revenue sharing. The financial stakes in college football and basketball have grown substantially, leading to debates about how athletes should share in that revenue.

Whether the legislation will advance through Congress remains uncertain. Previous attempts to regulate college sports at the federal level have stalled amid disagreements about how much control universities, conferences, and the NCAA should retain versus what rights athletes should have.

The bill's sponsors have not yet released details about specific salary cap amounts or the exact mechanisms that would govern transfers and coaching moves. Those details will likely emerge as the legislation moves through committee review and potential amendments.

Kenneth Iwamasa, Matthew Perry's live-in personal assistant, was sentenced to 41 months in federal prison on Wednesday for his role in the actor's death from a ketamine overdose in October 2023.

Iwamasa, 61, pleaded guilty to distributing ketamine that resulted in death or serious bodily injury. The sentence matched what federal prosecutors had requested. He had no medical training when he repeatedly injected Perry with the drug, including on the day the Friends actor was found dead in a hot tub at his Los Angeles home.

The sentencing concludes the criminal investigation into five people authorities say played roles in Perry's overdose. Other defendants received varying sentences. Jasveen Sangha, a drug dealer known as the "Ketamine queen," who supplied the fatal dose, was sentenced to 15 years in prison. Erik Fleming, a drug addiction counselor who served as a middleman, will spend two years behind bars. Salvador Plasencia, a former doctor who continued supplying ketamine after Perry had an adverse reaction more than two weeks before his death, received 30 months. Mark Chavez, another doctor who sold ketamine to Perry, was sentenced to eight months of home detention and three years of supervised release.

From 2022 to 2023, Iwamasa worked as Perry's personal assistant. The two had known each other for more than two decades. In fall 2023, Perry enlisted Iwamasa's help to obtain ketamine. The actor had sought ketamine infusion therapy at a California clinic for anxiety and depression but turned to outside sources to increase his dosage, according to federal authorities.

Prosecutors say Iwamasa paid Plasencia at least $55,000 to purchase ketamine on several occasions between September and October 2023. In the three days before Perry's death, Iwamasa injected the actor with six to eight shots of ketamine daily. Authorities reported that Iwamasa had found Perry unresponsive at least twice that month.

Perry's family condemned Iwamasa's actions in letters to the judge. His mother, Suzanne Morrison, said the family trusted Iwamasa to help Perry stay sober. His sister, Madeline Morrison, described observing Iwamasa behave in an unsettled manner after Perry's death, repeatedly volunteering his version of events without being asked.

Iwamasa's attorneys argued that the relationship between a celebrity and assistant involved a stark power imbalance, noting he "could not 'simply say no'." Perry, best known for playing Chandler Bing on Friends, had publicly discussed his decades-long struggles with opioid addiction and his recovery journey.

US to quarantine American Ebola patients in Kenya

"The facility is designed to p...

5:10 PM ET

The Trump administration is building a quarantine and treatment facility in Kenya for Americans exposed to Ebola, rather than bringing them back to the United States. A White House official confirmed the plan on Wednesday, saying the center will serve Americans who need to leave the Democratic Republic of the Congo after potential virus exposure.

"The facility is designed to provide access to high-quality care for Americans who would need to quickly get out of DRC and quarantine without the risks of a lengthy transport back to the US," the official told the Guardian. The center will handle both quarantine for exposed individuals and treatment for those who develop symptoms, including critical care when needed. The official noted that cases may be transferred elsewhere for more advanced treatment to improve outcomes, though they did not specify whether that would mean transport to the US or Europe.

The policy has drawn criticism from public health experts who question the approach. Jennifer Nuzzo, an epidemiologist and director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University, expressed concern about preventing Americans from returning home. "There are profound ethical concerns with this approach," she said, noting that US biocontainment units provide world-class care that the administration is essentially denying to citizens.

Nuzzo warned that the policy could backfire on containment efforts. Without assurance they can return home for treatment, people exposed to Ebola may not disclose their exposure, potentially driving cases underground and increasing transmission risk. This mirrors concerns from Jeremy Konyndyk, president of Refugees International and a former leader in the 2014-2015 Ebola response.

Konyndyk pointed out that the policy discourages American health workers from volunteering to help contain the outbreak. "It's basically telling any American health worker who might go and work on the effort to contain this outbreak that if they get sick, they can't come home," he said. During the 2014-2015 outbreak, officials specifically avoided strict travel bans because they knew such measures would undermine efforts to end the crisis.

The administration has also implemented broader travel restrictions, banning green card holders who recently visited the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Uganda, and South Sudan from returning home. Other recent travelers to these countries are also prohibited from entering the US.

Public health experts emphasize that transportation risks are extremely low for people who are not symptomatic, and the US has extensive experience evacuating people exposed to highly contagious pathogens. The White House official did not clarify whether Americans refusing quarantine in Kenya would be allowed to return to the United States instead.

6:00 PM ET

A group of senators introduced legislation that would regulate how college athletes are paid, restrict their ability to transfer between schools, and limit when coaches can change jobs during the season.

The bipartisan bill aims to bring structure to college sports compensation after recent court decisions and policy changes allowed athletes to profit from their name, image, and likeness. The proposed legislation would establish a salary cap system for payments to college players.

The bill also seeks to restrict the transfer portal, which has allowed athletes to switch schools with increasing frequency in recent years. Under the current system, players can enter the portal and move to different programs, sometimes multiple times during their college careers.

Additionally, the legislation would place limits on when coaches can move between programs during the active season. Coaching changes have become more common mid-season or immediately following bowl games, disrupting team continuity.

The bill represents an attempt by federal lawmakers to create uniform rules for college athletics as individual states have passed conflicting laws regarding athlete compensation. Without federal legislation, schools in different states operate under different regulations regarding how much and in what ways they can pay athletes.

The introduction of the bill comes as college sports conferences have undergone significant realignment, with schools moving between leagues in pursuit of better media rights deals and revenue sharing. The financial stakes in college football and basketball have grown substantially, leading to debates about how athletes should share in that revenue.

Whether the legislation will advance through Congress remains uncertain. Previous attempts to regulate college sports at the federal level have stalled amid disagreements about how much control universities, conferences, and the NCAA should retain versus what rights athletes should have.

The bill's sponsors have not yet released details about specific salary cap amounts or the exact mechanisms that would govern transfers and coaching moves. Those details will likely emerge as the legislation moves through committee review and potential amendments.

Jasveen Sangha, a drug dealer known as the "Ketamine queen," who supplied the fatal dose, was sentenced to 15 years in prison. Erik Fleming, a drug addiction counselor who served as a middleman, will spend two years behind bars. Salvador Plasencia, a former doctor who continued supplying ketamine after Perry had an adverse reaction more than two weeks before his death, received 30 months. Mark Chavez, another doctor who sold ketamine to Perry, was sentenced to eight months of home detention and three years of supervised release.

From 2022 to 2023, Iwamasa worked as Perry's personal assistant. The two had known each other for more than two decades. In fall 2023, Perry enlisted Iwamasa's help to obtain ketamine. The actor had sought ketamine infusion therapy at a California clinic for anxiety and depression but turned to outside sources to increase his dosage, according to federal authorities.

Prosecutors say Iwamasa paid Plasencia at least $55,000 to purchase ketamine on several occasions between September and October 2023. In the three days before Perry's death, Iwamasa injected the actor with six to eight shots of ketamine daily. Authorities reported that Iwamasa had found Perry unresponsive at least twice that month.

Perry's family condemned Iwamasa's actions in letters to the judge. His mother, Suzanne Morrison, said the family trusted Iwamasa to help Perry stay sober. His sister, Madeline Morrison, described observing Iwamasa behave in an unsettled manner after Perry's death, repeatedly volunteering his version of events without being asked.

Iwamasa's attorneys argued that the relationship between a celebrity and assistant involved a stark power imbalance, noting he "could not 'simply say no'."
