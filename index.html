A federal judge in Washington, D.C., has refused to temporarily block President Trump's executive order that restricts mail-in voting, allowing the policy to proceed while legal challenges continue. The decision came in response to an emergency request from opponents of the order seeking to halt its implementation.

The judge's ruling does not address whether the executive order is ultimately legal or constitutional. Instead, it only addresses the immediate question of whether the policy should be stopped while the underlying case proceeds through the courts.

Democrats and voting rights advocates had sought a preliminary injunction to prevent the order from taking effect, but the judge denied their request. This denial clears the path for the Trump administration to begin implementing the new restrictions on mail-in ballot procedures.

The specific details of the voting restrictions contained in the order were not detailed in court filings made available to the public. However, voting rights advocates have expressed concern that limiting mail-in voting could reduce voter participation and access to the ballot.

The Trump administration has defended the executive order, though the precise reasoning offered by officials was not included in the court documents. The White House views the order as an important policy change for election procedures.

The judge's decision represents a legal victory for the Trump administration in the immediate battle, even as the broader dispute over the order's validity continues. Multiple legal challenges are expected to move forward as opponents pursue further court action to overturn or modify the restrictions.

Mail-in voting has become an increasingly contentious issue in American politics and courts. Similar disputes over voting by mail have appeared in courts across the country in recent years, with outcomes varying depending on the specific policies being challenged and the jurisdictions involved.

The case reflects ongoing tensions over how voting access should be balanced against election security concerns. Democrats argue that mail-in voting should remain widely available, while the Trump administration argues that new restrictions are necessary.

With another judge potentially set to rule on the order soon, the legal fight over the mail-in voting restrictions is expected to intensify. The case could ultimately reach higher courts, including potentially the appeals courts or the Supreme Court, depending on how the litigation develops.

The ruling allows the executive order to take effect while the courts continue to examine its legality. This means the voting restrictions will be in place for elections and voting procedures going forward, at least unless a higher court intervenes to block the order at a later stage.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has directed the Israeli military to expand territorial control in Gaza to 70 percent, a move that violates the terms of a ceasefire agreement established in October. Under the original US-brokered ceasefire, Israeli forces withdrew to a demarcation line that gave Israel control of 53 percent of the territory.

Speaking at a conference in an occupied West Bank settlement, Netanyahu outlined Israel's territorial expansion. "We are currently squeezing Hamas. We now control 60% of the territory in the strip. You know, we were at 50, we moved to 60. My directive is to move to 70%," he said.

The expansion directly contradicts the October ceasefire agreement and the UN Security Council resolution that endorsed it. The Trump administration's 20-point peace plan, which the ceasefire framework was based on, established a temporary demarcation line splitting Gaza into Israeli and Hamas-controlled areas pending further negotiations. The plan explicitly stated that no one would be forced to leave Gaza.

Since the ceasefire began, Israeli forces have steadily advanced westward into Hamas-controlled territory and established an expanded no-man's land where they claim authority to decide who can enter. According to UN briefing materials, Israeli forces have killed more than 900 Palestinians within range of the demarcation line during the eight months of the supposed truce through continued ground operations and airstrikes.

Israeli-backed armed militias have played an increasing role in the territorial expansion. These groups have been ordering Palestinian residents near the demarcation line to evacuate their homes. One Gaza resident, Wael Nayef Abu al-Ajeen, described armed militiamen entering his area near Deir al-Balah and giving families until 10pm to leave their homes.

Israeli Defense Minister Israel Katz stated that the government's ultimate objective is for large numbers of Palestinians to depart Gaza through what he called "voluntary migration." Human rights activists describe this approach as ethnic cleansing, achieved by making living conditions inside Gaza intolerable.

The territorial expansion would leave Gaza's 2.2 million surviving Palestinians squeezed into less than a third of their original territory. Muhammad Shehada, a visiting fellow at the European Council on Foreign Relations, said Netanyahu's directive essentially nullifies the Trump peace framework. "The conditions there are already appalling," Shehada noted. "Every square metre has another displaced family, another makeshift tent, or some sort of improvised shelter on it. So it would be a death sentence for a lot of people who physically have no place to go."

An Israeli army spokesperson declined to comment on Netanyahu's directive, referring the matter to the political leadership instead.

The competitive landscape between major AI companies shifted dramatically this week following a breakdown in negotiations between Anthropic and the Pentagon over ethical restrictions on its technology.

The dispute centers on how AI systems should be deployed for military purposes. Anthropic has refused to loosen safety guidelines that prohibit its Claude system from being used for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons systems that can operate without human oversight. The Pentagon had pushed for broader access to Anthropic's capabilities, but the company maintained its position despite pressure from government officials.

In response, President Trump directed all federal agencies to cease using Anthropic technology, calling the company's stance a "disastrous mistake." Trump criticized Anthropic for attempting to impose its terms of service over what he characterized as constitutional authority.

OpenAI moved quickly to fill the void, announcing a new agreement with the Pentagon to supply AI for classified military networks. CEO Sam Altman emphasized that OpenAI's deal includes safeguards aligned with Anthropic's stated concerns. "Two of our most important safety principles are prohibitions on domestic mass surveillance and human responsibility for the use of force, including for autonomous weapon systems," Altman wrote on social media. He stated that the Pentagon agreed to these terms and incorporated them into the contract.

The situation has drawn unexpected support for Anthropic from its rivals. Nearly 500 employees from OpenAI and Google signed an open letter expressing solidarity, warning that the Pentagon was attempting to divide the companies by threatening to favor those willing to compromise on ethics. The letter suggested the government was negotiating separately with multiple AI companies to pressure each into accepting unfavorable terms.

Altman addressed OpenAI staff in a memo, acknowledging the broader implications for the industry. He stressed that maintaining ethical boundaries on surveillance and autonomous weapons represents a core commitment for OpenAI, regardless of competitive pressures. Altman proposed that the Pentagon's agreement with OpenAI should serve as a model for the entire sector.

Anthropic responded to the government's actions with a statement reaffirming its position. The company noted that it had attempted negotiations in good faith while clarifying its support for lawful national security applications of AI. The company indicated that its two exceptions regarding mass surveillance and autonomous weapons have not impeded any government missions to date.

Separately, OpenAI announced a $110 billion funding round that values the company at $840 billion, demonstrating continued investor confidence despite the recent political tensions. The funding underscores the substantial capital flowing into AI development as major players compete for technological and market dominance in a rapidly evolving sector.

US and Iran reach tentative agreement to extend ceasefire

The United States and Iran have reached a tentative agreement to extend their current ceasefire for an additional two months and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, according to White House confirmation. The deal requires approval from President Trump before taking effect. The arrangement takes the form of a 60-day memorandum of understanding that addresses both the ceasefire extensi...

1:52 PM ET

The United States and Iran have reached a tentative agreement to extend their current ceasefire for an additional two months and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, according to White House confirmation. The deal requires approval from President Trump before taking effect.

The arrangement takes the form of a 60-day memorandum of understanding that addresses both the ceasefire extension and reopening of the strategically vital waterway. The Strait of Hormuz connects the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea, serving as a critical passage for global oil trade. Control or disruption of this route typically raises concerns about energy markets and shipping security.

The original ceasefire was scheduled to expire on April 22. Indirect negotiations between Washington and Tehran have been ongoing, with Pakistan playing a significant mediation role. Pakistan's army chief traveled to Tehran to continue diplomatic efforts, while the country's prime minister launched a regional tour visiting Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Turkey to secure support for a peace agreement.

White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt stated that Washington remained engaged in negotiations and felt "good about the prospects of a deal." She noted that a second round of talks would likely occur in Islamabad, though she emphasized that nothing becomes official until announced by the White House.

Iran's foreign ministry spokesman said that views from both sides would be discussed in detail during ongoing diplomatic exchanges. However, sources in Tehran indicated that Iran had demanded an end to Israeli attacks on Lebanon as a precondition for further negotiations with the United States.

The tentative deal prompted a significant market response. Global oil prices fell following reports of the breakthrough, reflecting investor optimism about potential conflict resolution and renewed energy market stability.

The agreement comes amid heightened military tensions. The US military announced it had fully implemented a naval blockade of Iranian ports, with warships turning back nine vessels attempting to cross the Strait of Hormuz. Iran's military leadership warned that it could halt regional trade if the blockade continued.

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent indicated that the administration was prepared to escalate financial pressure on Iran, threatening secondary sanctions against countries and companies conducting business with Tehran. He described this as the "financial equivalent" of military operations.

President Trump has not yet publicly stated whether he will approve the tentative arrangement. The agreement requires his formal sign-off to proceed, though no timeline was provided for his decision. Trump previously suggested that a peace agreement might be reached even without extending the ceasefire, predicting swift resolution to the conflict.

The negotiations represent ongoing diplomatic efforts to prevent the resumption of hostilities between Washington and Tehran.
