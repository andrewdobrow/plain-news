A few minutes ago

The United States and Iran traded military fire on Thursday in the most serious test yet of their month-long ceasefire. Both nations accused each other of violating the agreement while insisting their own actions were justified responses to aggression.

The US military said it struck Iranian military facilities after Tehran launched what it called unprovoked attacks on three American destroyers in the Strait of Hormuz. According to US Central Command, Iranian forces fired multiple missiles, drones, and small boats at the USS Truxtun, USS Rafael Peralta, and USS Mason. The US military reported that no American assets were struck and that its forces eliminated the incoming threats while targeting Iranian military sites responsible for the attacks, including missile and drone launch locations.

Iran's military rejected this account, accusing the US of breaking the ceasefire by targeting two ships in the strait and attacking civilian areas along the Iranian coast. A military spokesperson said American airstrikes hit civilian regions near Bandar Khamir, Sirik, and the island of Qeshm, which has about 150,000 residents. Iran claimed its armed forces responded by attacking US military vessels and inflicted significant damage.

The exchanges occurred as both countries negotiate a potential end to the three-month conflict. Before Thursday's strikes, reports suggested the US and Iran were moving toward a temporary agreement, with a one-page memorandum circulating between Washington and Tehran through Pakistan. However, senior Iranian officials have recently rejected concessions, with some favoring extended negotiations to gain leverage before the US midterm elections.

President Trump characterized the incident as minor, describing the US response as a "love tap" during an interview with ABC News. "They trifled with us today. We blew them away," Trump said. He maintained that a ceasefire remained in place despite the strikes and suggested a deal could happen "any day," though he acknowledged it "might not happen."

The incident raised questions about the fragile ceasefire's viability. Regional diplomats suggested that Iran risks overplaying its hand, as the current moment offers an opportunity to end the war and claim victory. They warned that if negotiations collapse, Washington could unilaterally end the conflict and leave Iran under severe economic sanctions.

The Strait of Hormuz remains a critical flashpoint. The US has maintained a naval blockade of Iranian ports and deployed warships through the waterway in what Trump called "Project Freedom" to guide stranded tankers through the passage.

Financial markets reacted to the renewed hostilities. Brent crude oil rose to approximately 101 dollars per barrel following news of the attacks.

The ceasefire, which had largely held for the previous month, now faces serious questions about its sustainability. Diplomatic sources indicated the fresh strikes could complicate ongoing peace negotiations, though formal talks have not been suspended.

Oil prices rose after the United States military said it shot down an Iranian drone near a US aircraft carrier, raising fresh concerns about diplomatic efforts between Washington and Tehran.

An F-35C fighter jet from the USS Abraham Lincoln shot down an Iranian Shahed-139 drone in the Arabian Sea on Tuesday in what the US military described as a self-defense action. Captain Tim Hawkins, a navy spokesperson at US Central Command, said the drone had "aggressively" approached the carrier with "unclear intent." No US service members were harmed and no equipment was damaged, he added.

The incident occurred as diplomats were working to arrange nuclear talks between the two countries. Iran's president, Masoud Pezeshkian, announced on Tuesday that he had instructed his foreign minister to "pursue fair and equitable negotiations" with the US, marking a significant shift in Tehran's stance. The move indicated that Iran's supreme leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, supported talks that he had previously rejected.

A White House press secretary confirmed that Trump envoy Steve Witkoff still plans to hold talks with Iranian officials later in the week, despite the drone incident. However, Iran's foreign ministry said consultations were ongoing about the meeting location, with Tehran reportedly preferring Oman over Turkey and wanting discussions limited to nuclear issues.

On the same day as the drone shootdown, Iran's Revolutionary Guards also harassed a US-flagged merchant vessel in the Strait of Hormuz. Two guard boats and an Iranian drone approached the tanker at high speeds and threatened to board it, according to the US military.

Iran's UN mission declined to comment on the claims about the drone. Iran's Tasnim news agency reported that connection had been lost with a drone in international waters but said the reason was unknown. The semi-official Fars news agency reported that an Iranian drone had completed a "surveillance mission in international waters."

Oil traders reacted swiftly to news of the confrontation. Earlier on Tuesday, oil prices had fallen below $100 a barrel on hopes that negotiations would succeed. The drone incident reversed those gains as markets grew concerned about potential supply disruptions in a critical energy-producing region.

The shootdown represents another test of fragile US-Iran relations. President Trump has sent military forces to the region following recent unrest in Iran and has demanded nuclear concessions from Tehran. He previously suggested that "bad things" would happen unless a deal could be reached, but more recently said he believed Iran was "seriously talking."

The situation remains fluid as financial markets monitor developments for signs of further military action or diplomatic progress.

Yesterday, 8:45 PM ET

Federal authorities have charged a Google engineer with insider trading after the employee allegedly made over $1.2 million through wagers on the prediction market platform Polymarket.

According to the complaint filed by prosecutors, the engineer placed bets exceeding $2.7 million on outcomes related to Google's 2025 Year in Search campaign. The charges suggest the employee used confidential information about the campaign before it became public knowledge to gain an advantage on the betting platform.

Polymarket operates as a prediction market where users wager cryptocurrency on the outcomes of real-world events, ranging from political elections to corporate announcements. The platform has grown in popularity as an alternative to traditional betting markets, but has faced scrutiny over potential misuse of inside information.

The case marks a notable intersection of technology company employment, prediction markets, and securities law enforcement. While insider trading charges typically involve stock purchases based on confidential corporate information, prosecutors appear to be applying similar legal principles to prediction market wagers.

The complaint indicates the engineer risked substantial personal funds on bets tied to specific aspects of Google's annual Year in Search campaign, which typically highlights the most popular search queries and trending topics of the past year. The campaign generates significant public interest and media coverage when released.

Google has not publicly commented on the charges or whether the engineer remains employed at the company. The technology giant maintains strict policies regarding the use of confidential information and typically cooperates with law enforcement investigations involving current or former employees.

Polymarket has also not issued a statement about the case. The platform requires users to comply with applicable laws and prohibits trading based on material nonpublic information, though enforcement of such rules on decentralized prediction markets presents unique challenges.

The charges highlight ongoing questions about how insider trading laws apply to emerging financial technologies and prediction markets, where traditional securities regulations may not have been designed to address novel trading mechanisms.

Oil prices rose after the United States military said it shot down an Iranian drone near a US aircraft carrier, raising fresh concerns about diplomatic efforts between Washington and Tehran.

An F-35C fighter jet from the USS Abraham Lincoln shot down an Iranian Shahed-139 drone in the Arabian Sea on Tuesday in what the US military described as a self-defense action. Captain Tim Hawkins, a navy spokesperson at US Central Command, said the drone had "aggressively" approached the carrier with "unclear intent." No US service members were harmed and no equipment was damaged, he added.

The incident occurred as diplomats were working to arrange nuclear talks between the two countries. Iran's president, Masoud Pezeshkian, announced on Tuesday that he had instructed his foreign minister to "pursue fair and equitable negotiations" with the US, marking a significant shift in Tehran's stance. The move indicated that Iran's supreme leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, supported talks that he had previously rejected.

A White House press secretary confirmed that Trump envoy Steve Witkoff still plans to hold talks with Iranian officials later in the week, despite the drone incident. However, Iran's foreign ministry said consultations were ongoing about the meeting location, with Tehran reportedly preferring Oman over Turkey and wanting discussions limited to nuclear issues.

On the same day as the drone shootdown, Iran's Revolutionary Guards also harassed a US-flagged merchant vessel in the Strait of Hormuz. Two guard boats and an Iranian drone approached the tanker at high speeds and threatened to board it, according to the US military.

Iran's UN mission declined to comment on the claims about the drone. Iran's Tasnim news agency reported that connection had been lost with a drone in international waters but said the reason was unknown. The semi-official Fars news agency reported that an Iranian drone had completed a "surveillance mission in international waters."

Oil traders reacted swiftly to news of the confrontation. Earlier on Tuesday, oil prices had fallen below $100 a barrel on hopes that negotiations would succeed. The drone incident reversed those gains as markets grew concerned about potential supply disruptions in a critical energy-producing region.

The shootdown represents another test of fragile US-Iran relations. President Trump has sent military forces to the region following recent unrest in Iran and has demanded nuclear concessions from Tehran. He previously suggested that "bad things" would happen unless a deal could be reached, but more recently said he believed Iran was "seriously talking."

The situation remains fluid as financial markets monitor developments for signs of further military action or diplomatic progress.
