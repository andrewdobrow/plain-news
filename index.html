The United States and Iran traded military blows late Thursday in the most serious test yet of their month-long ceasefire, threatening delicate peace negotiations aimed at ending their conflict.

The exchange began when Iran accused the US of violating the ceasefire by targeting ships at the Strait of Hormuz and striking civilian areas. The US military said it attacked in retaliation for what it characterized as unprovoked Iranian hostilities against three American destroyers transiting the strait. According to US Central Command, Iranian forces launched multiple missiles, drones, and small boats at the vessels, though no US assets were struck.

Iran's military spokesperson countered that the aggressive US strikes violated the ceasefire agreement. They claimed American airstrikes targeted civilian areas near Bandar Khamir, Sirik, and the island of Qeshm, which is home to approximately 150,000 people and a water desalination plant. Iran said its armed forces responded by attacking US military vessels.

Following the strikes, Iran's Press TV reported that the situation along Iranian islands and coastal cities by the Strait of Hormuz had returned to normal after several hours of fire. The United Arab Emirates said it had intercepted Iranian missile and drone attacks.

Despite the escalation, President Trump maintained the ceasefire remained intact. He described the strikes as a "love tap" during an interview with ABC News and told reporters the US "blew them away." When asked about prospects for a negotiated resolution, Trump said a deal "might not happen, but it could happen any day," while claiming Iran wanted an agreement more than he did.

The latest skirmishes cast doubt on the viability of the fragile ceasefire that had largely held for the previous month. Prior to Thursday's exchanges, reports suggested the US and Iran were close to a temporary agreement, with a one-page memorandum being shared between Washington and Tehran through Pakistan.

Regional diplomats suggested Iran could overplay its hand by dragging out negotiations. They noted the current moment offered an opportunity to end the war and claim victory, something that might prove harder if full-scale fighting resumes. They also warned that if no agreement is reached, Washington could unilaterally end the war and withdraw, leaving Iran subject to suffocating economic sanctions.

Oil markets reacted swiftly to the military action. Brent crude oil rose to trade around $101 a barrel on news of the attacks, reflecting investor concerns about potential supply disruptions from the region.

The confrontation also affected broader Middle Eastern diplomacy. A US State Department official confirmed that new Israel-Lebanon talks would take place May 14 and 15, marking the third meeting in recent months between the two countries.

A few minutes ago

The European Union fined Chinese online retailer Temu 200 million euros, equivalent to more than $232 million, for allowing the sale of illegal and unsafe products on its platform.

The European Commission said Temu failed to account for risks posed by baby toys and faulty chargers sold through its marketplace. The fine represents one of the largest penalties imposed on an e-commerce platform for product safety violations.

Temu, owned by Chinese parent company PDD Holdings, operates as an online marketplace connecting sellers directly with consumers across Europe and other markets. The platform has grown rapidly in recent years by offering deeply discounted products shipped from China.

The Commission's investigation found that Temu did not adequately assess or mitigate consumer safety risks before products appeared on its platform. Baby toys and electrical chargers were specifically cited as product categories where dangerous items reached European buyers.

Under EU regulations, online marketplaces bear responsibility for ensuring products sold through their platforms meet safety standards. The rules require platforms to implement systems that identify and remove unsafe goods before they can harm consumers.

The penalty comes as European regulators increase scrutiny of Chinese e-commerce platforms operating in the bloc. Authorities have raised concerns about whether fast-growing marketplaces adequately screen products for compliance with EU safety, environmental, and consumer protection standards.

Temu has not yet publicly responded to the fine or outlined any changes to its product screening procedures. The company faces similar regulatory pressure in other jurisdictions where it operates.

The Commission's enforcement action signals that EU authorities will hold online platforms accountable for safety failures regardless of where the platform or its sellers are based. European officials have emphasized that all companies selling to EU consumers must comply with the bloc's product safety requirements.

The fine follows a pattern of increased regulatory action against major technology and e-commerce companies in Europe. The EU has positioned itself as a global leader in platform regulation, often imposing standards that influence company practices worldwide.

Federal authorities have charged a Google engineer with insider trading after he allegedly made $1.2 million through wagers on the prediction market platform Polymarket.

According to the criminal complaint, the engineer risked over $2.7 million on bets related to Google's 2025 Year in Search campaign. The charges suggest the engineer used non-public information about the company's internal marketing plans to place bets on Polymarket, a platform where users wager on the outcomes of real-world events.

Polymarket operates as a decentralized prediction market where participants buy and sell shares in the outcomes of various events, from political elections to corporate announcements. The platform has grown in popularity as a tool for forecasting, but it has also attracted scrutiny from regulators concerned about potential misuse.

The case represents one of the first insider trading prosecutions involving a prediction market platform. Traditional insider trading cases typically involve stock purchases or sales based on material non-public information. This case extends that legal framework to a newer form of speculative activity.

The engineer's alleged trades centered on information about Google's Year in Search campaign, an annual feature that highlights the most popular search queries. Access to advance knowledge about the campaign's content and timing would have provided a significant advantage in predicting related outcomes on Polymarket.

Federal prosecutors have not disclosed the engineer's name or current employment status at Google. The complaint does not specify whether the engineer has been arrested or when initial court proceedings will take place.

Google has not issued a public statement about the charges. The company maintains strict policies against employees using confidential information for personal gain, and violations can result in termination and legal action.

The case highlights the expanding regulatory focus on prediction markets and the legal risks facing employees at technology companies who have access to valuable non-public information. As platforms like Polymarket continue to grow, authorities appear to be treating them with the same seriousness as traditional financial markets when it comes to insider trading enforcement.
