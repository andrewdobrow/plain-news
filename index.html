Supreme Court allows Alabama to use six Republican-leaning congressional districts for midterms

9:45 PM ET

The Supreme Court has approved Alabama's new congressional map that eliminates one of the state's two majority-Black districts, clearing the way for Republicans to use the redrawn plan in the upcoming midterm elections. The 6-3 decision represents another significant victory for Republicans following the court's landmark April ruling that weakened a critical provision of the Voting Rights Act.

Alabama's map now contains six Republican-leaning districts and one Democratic-leaning district. The state had challenged a lower court's decision to block the map, arguing that it should be allowed to proceed. The Supreme Court sided with Alabama's position.

This ruling marks the first major test of whether the court's April decision in Louisiana v Callais, which struck down key protections of the Voting Rights Act, would permit states to implement maps that intentionally discriminate against Black voters. The Alabama case demonstrates how that earlier ruling has reshaped the legal landscape surrounding congressional redistricting.

The state's efforts to redraw its map were part of a broader Republican campaign across the South following the April decision. Tennessee implemented a new map eliminating a majority-Black district in Memphis. Louisiana is also preparing to remove a majority-Black district, and South Carolina may follow. Florida's new congressional map, which could provide the GOP four additional seats in the midterms, has also survived initial court challenges.

The Alabama dispute has been contentious. Black voters sued the state in 2021, arguing that its original congressional map diluted their voting power. Federal judges ordered the state to create a new plan with an additional majority-Black district. However, the legislature passed a 2023 map that again eliminated that protection. The Supreme Court ultimately upheld a court-appointed special master's map that included the two majority-Black districts.

The latest Supreme Court decision allows Alabama to move forward with its map that reduces the number of districts where Black voters have a meaningful opportunity to elect candidates of their choice.

The decision has drawn sharp criticism from voting rights advocates. Davin Rosborough, deputy director of the voting rights project at the American Civil Liberties Union, stated that the court's earlier ruling "recognized what we already knew: the Alabama legislature's repeated refusal to provide Black Alabamians with fair representation in Congress is racial discrimination."

Alabama's Republican Attorney General Steve Marshall said he anticipated the outcome. "I am disappointed, but not at all surprised," he said in a statement, adding that he viewed the case as a matter of "when" the state would prevail, not "if."

Civil rights groups have expressed concern that the redistricting efforts across the South represent a return to voter suppression tactics and deliberately deny Black voters a voice in the political process.

The US military subsequently reported that it repelled Iranian attacks in the region and struck targets on Iran's Qeshm Island.

The US military's Central Command denied that this Iranian attack succeeded. The latest military exchange underscores the lack of political progress in resolving the broader Middle East crisis, despite optimistic claims from US officials. The Strait of Hormuz, where the tanker incident occurred, is a strategically vital global shipping route and energy corridor. The targeting of American forces in Bahrain and the US response on Iranian territory demonstrates the widening geographic scope of the confrontation. The United States and Iran have resumed military strikes against each other after negotiations aimed at establishing a lasting ceasefire broke down. The escalation marks a critical failure in diplomatic efforts to ease tensions between Washington and Tehran.

The breakdown came as Iran suspended participation in peace talks, citing Israel's military operations in Lebanon as a violation of ceasefire terms. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi stated that "the ceasefire between Iran and the US is unequivocally a ceasefire on all fronts, including in Lebanon. Its violation on one front is a violation of the ceasefire on all fronts."

US forces fired a Hellfire missile to disable a tanker attempting to break through the American blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. The US military then reported repelling Iranian retaliatory attacks in the region and striking Iranian sites on Qeshm Island.

Iran responded by announcing it had targeted a military base in Kuwait that it claimed was involved in US operations. The Iranian military command issued warnings to residents of northern Israel, threatening attacks if Israel proceeded with strikes on Beirut and its southern suburbs.

The collapse of negotiations represents a significant setback after discussions had appeared to approach agreement on Friday. President Donald Trump pulled back from a preliminary deal, demanding revisions to the outline agreement. Trump stated he was not opposed to halting talks, telling NBC News that "going silent would be very good." However, he indicated the US would maintain its blockade on Iranian ports rather than launch immediate military escalation.

Iran's chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, contended that the US blockade and Israeli actions in Lebanon constituted "clear evidence of US non-compliance" with the ceasefire terms. He warned that "every choice has a price, and the bill comes due."

The ceasefire had technically been in effect since early April, though the US and Iran had exchanged strikes several times during this period. The tensions have raised global concerns, with oil prices climbing close to 100 dollars per barrel.

European leaders expressed alarm at the deteriorating situation. French President Emmanuel Macron spoke with Iran's President Masoud Pezeshkian, Trump, and major Arab leaders on Sunday, urging recognition of the necessity for a ceasefire in Lebanon and reopening of the Strait of Hormuz to resume oil transit.

Iran's military command warned that if attacks on Beirut continued, it would request the Houthis in Yemen to establish a second shipping blockade at the Bab al-Mandab strait at the southern entrance to the Red Sea.

The situation now stands at a critical juncture, with both nations demonstrating willingness to conduct military operations and raising the risk of broader regional conflict involving multiple actors with interests in the Middle East.

SpaceX announced plans for a major initial public offering, with the company targeting a valuation of approximately $1.75 trillion. The space company intends to sell 555.6 million shares at $135 per share in an all-primary offering, meaning shares will come directly from SpaceX rather than from existing investors selling their stakes.

The proposed valuation has raised concerns among market analysts. Morningstar assessed SpaceX's worth at $780 billion, significantly less than half the company's IPO target. This substantial gap between SpaceX's asking price and independent valuations presents a challenge for potential investors evaluating the stock ahead of trading.

Over the past two decades, SpaceX has reshaped the aerospace sector. The company now dominates commercial satellite launch services and holds contracts to transport astronauts to the International Space Station. SpaceX operates Starlink, its satellite internet service, and continues developing Starship, a rocket system designed for lunar and Martian missions. The company's reusable rocket technology has significantly lowered launch expenses and increased the frequency of missions.

The IPO raises strategic questions about SpaceX's direction as a public company. Currently, SpaceX generates revenue from government contracts, commercial launches, and Starlink subscriptions. Industry observers are uncertain whether the company will maintain its focus on launch services or shift toward alternative revenue sources under public ownership.

Becoming a publicly traded company will fundamentally change how SpaceX operates. The firm will face mandatory quarterly earnings reports and shareholder expectations that may conflict with the extended development timelines SpaceX pursued as a private entity. Founder Elon Musk has historically been reluctant to take his companies public, previously expressing concerns that short-term investor pressure undermines ambitious technological goals. His decision to pursue an IPO at this time suggests either a capital requirement for ongoing projects or pressure from early investors seeking liquidity opportunities.

The timing of the IPO also reflects broader market dynamics. Other major technology and energy companies have recently pursued significant capital raises. Alphabet announced plans to raise up to $80 billion through equity sales to fund artificial intelligence infrastructure investments. Anthropic, an AI company, plans to offer public share sales later this year with a valuation approaching $1 trillion.

SpaceX's historic IPO comes at a moment when large technology companies are aggressively pursuing capital to support emerging business priorities, particularly in artificial intelligence and space technology. The company's transition from private to public ownership will test whether SpaceX can balance investor expectations with its ambitious long-term development goals in space exploration and satellite communications.

Microsoft introduced Scout, a new AI assistant designed to operate continuously in the background of users' work lives. Internal documents obtained by 404 Media reveal the company's goal is to make people dependent on the technology, which functions as an always-on personal agent. The assistant was announced at Microsoft's Build developer conference alongside other AI initiatives.

Scout operates differently from traditional software by working like an executive assistant that never stops monitoring and responding to user needs. The system draws on Microsoft's partnership with OpenAI, using technology the company describes as OpenClaw-inspired. Unlike conventional applications that users open and close, Scout runs persistently to anticipate needs and automate tasks.

The company also announced Work IQ APIs, which allow developers to integrate Scout's capabilities into their own applications. Microsoft is positioning the assistant as a coworker rather than a tool, representing a shift in how the company envisions people interacting with their computers. Bloomberg reported the assistant functions similarly to executive support staff, handling scheduling, communications, and task management.

The internal documents showing Microsoft's intention to create user dependence on Scout have raised questions about the company's approach to AI adoption. The phrasing suggests Microsoft views habitual reliance on the assistant as a feature rather than a concern. WIRED described Scout as an AI coworker that never logs off, emphasizing its persistent presence in the digital workspace.

The announcement comes as Microsoft seeks to reduce its reliance on OpenAI and lower costs for developers. CNBC reported the company unveiled new AI models at Build 2026 designed to give developers more options beyond OpenAI's technology. Microsoft also used the conference to promote Windows as a trusted development platform, suggesting Scout and related AI tools will be tightly integrated with the operating system. The assistant represents Microsoft's bet that the future of computing involves constant AI mediation rather than discrete application use.

8:02 PM ET

The Los Angeles Rams acquired star edge rusher Myles Garrett from the Cleveland Browns in a major trade on Monday, sending linebacker Jared Verse and three draft picks to Cleveland in the deal.

Garrett, 30, is a two-time NFL Defensive Player of the Year and seven-time Pro Bowl selection. Since being drafted first overall by the Browns in 2017, he has established himself as one of the league's premier defensive players. He holds the record for most sacks (125.5), pressures (413), and tackles for loss (149) among players selected in that draft class. In 2025, Garrett set the single-season sacks record with 23.

The star pass rusher had requested a trade from the Browns in 2024 after the team finished with a 3-14 record. Seeking a fresh start with a championship contender, Garrett signed a four-year, $160 million extension with Cleveland a month later that made him the highest-paid non-quarterback in league history at the time. The deal includes $122.8 million in guaranteed money and averages $40 million annually.

Despite Garrett's individual excellence, the Browns finished 2025 with a 5-12 record and missed the playoffs for the second consecutive year. General manager Andrew Berry said the team faced a difficult decision. "As discussions intensified we were stuck at a legitimate crossroads: do we hold on to a truly generational player who has become the identity of our team, or do we make the difficult decision that we think is best for the organization over the long run?" Berry said after announcing the trade.

The contract modifications proved essential to completing the deal. The Browns and Garrett agreed to defer option payments over the 2026-28 seasons, pushing a $10 million payment due in March to the start of the regular season.

The Rams, coming off a loss to the eventual champion Seattle Seahawks in the NFC championship game, add another elite defensive weapon to their roster. This marks the first time the reigning AP NFL MVP and Defensive Player of the Year will be teammates, with quarterback Matthew Stafford winning his first MVP award last season.

Verse, the 25-year-old linebacker sent to Cleveland, was a first-round pick by the Rams in 2024. He earned Defensive Rookie of the Year honors while recording 12 career sacks and 22 tackles for loss. The Browns also received a 2027 first-round selection, a 2028 second-round pick, and a 2029 third-round selection.

The Rams have a track record of ambitious trades. In 2021, they swapped Jared Goff to the Detroit Lions to acquire Matthew Stafford, who led Los Angeles to a Super Bowl victory in his first season with the team.

Peabo Bryson, the Grammy-winning singer known for his soaring ballads and Disney film duets, has died at 75. The soul and R&B artist passed away, according to multiple reports citing CBS News and Variety.

Bryson achieved widespread fame for his performances on two Disney animated film soundtracks. He recorded "Beauty and the Beast" with Celine Dion for the 1991 film and "A Whole New World" with Regina Belle for the 1992 film Aladdin. Both songs became international hits and earned him Grammy Awards. His rich tenor voice and romantic delivery made him a defining artist of ballad-driven R&B.

Beyond his Disney work, Bryson built a career spanning decades with hits including "Im So Into You" and "Let the Feeling Flow." The New York Times described him as the "Voice of Love" in recognition of his signature romantic style. His music crossed over from R&B charts to mainstream pop, establishing him as one of the most recognizable voices in American popular music from the 1970s through the 1990s.

Atlanta Mayor Andre Dickens led public tributes following news of Bryson's death, according to CBS News. The singer's connection to Atlanta and the broader music community prompted an outpouring of remembrances from fans and fellow artists. His influence on R&B vocal performance and his role in bringing soul music to film soundtracks left a lasting mark on American entertainment.

Bryson's death removes one of the most distinctive voices from the generation of soul and R&B singers who defined the sound of romantic ballads in late 20th-century American music. His Disney recordings introduced his vocal style to global audiences and remain among the most recognized songs in animated film history.
