The United States carried out fresh military strikes against Iranian targets, marking the second such action within three days. The strikes occurred while both nations maintain a fragile ceasefire and continue diplomatic negotiations aimed at ending a three-month conflict.

President Trump stated that Iran is "negotiating on fumes" following the latest military action. According to U.S. officials, the new strikes were conducted in response to Iranian attack drones that U.S. forces had previously downed.

Iran responded to the American strikes by targeting a U.S. airbase. This exchange of military fire has tested the durability of the ceasefire agreement that both sides have been attempting to maintain while pursuing peace talks.

The renewed hostilities have triggered significant reactions in global financial markets. Oil prices jumped 3 percent after Iran announced its strike on U.S. forces. Asian stock markets declined as investors assessed mixed signals from the ongoing U.S.-Iran conflict.

The situation has created broader pressure on the Trump administration regarding the war. The president expressed indifference to midterm political considerations, suggesting that domestic electoral concerns would not influence his approach to the conflict.

The three-month war has now entered a particularly unstable phase, with both military operations and diplomatic efforts proceeding simultaneously. The ceasefire remains technically in place despite the exchange of strikes, though its fragility has become increasingly evident.

Neither country has indicated plans to completely abandon the negotiating process, even as military operations continue. Both governments face the challenge of pursuing meaningful diplomatic talks while managing domestic and international pressure following the renewed military engagement.

Analysts and officials have emphasized the critical importance of safeguarding international maritime routes to ensure the uninterrupted flow of energy. Iran's effective closure of the strait of Hormuz has severely constrained global oil distribution. The narrow waterway typically handles approximately 100 tankers daily, with roughly 20 percent of the world's total crude oil passing through this vital trade route.

The conflict has resulted in substantial damage to Middle Eastern oil infrastructure. Iranian and Israeli military operations have struck petrochemical plants and production facilities across the region, disrupting energy supplies significantly.

The price of Brent crude has soared dramatically since the start of the year, rising more than 50 percent and hitting a peak of 119.50 dollars per barrel in March. Current trading prices remain around 109 dollars per barrel.

These elevated energy costs have directly impacted consumers globally. In the United States, average fuel prices recently passed 4 dollars per gallon for the first time in four years, while UK motorists have also faced substantially higher petrol and diesel prices.

The latest strikes mark an escalation in tensions following the initial ceasefire announcement. Both governments now face mounting pressure to manage renewed military engagement while continuing their pursuit of a diplomatic resolution to the conflict.

Yesterday, 10:47 PM ET

The Australian government has filed a lawsuit against US manufacturing giant 3M over alleged contamination from firefighting foam at defense installations. The case seeks damages of A$2 billion, marking the largest legal action ever brought by the Australian government.

The lawsuit centers on the use of firefighting foam containing so-called forever chemicals at military and defense sites across Australia. These chemicals, known as PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances), persist in the environment and have raised health and environmental concerns.

3M manufactured firefighting foams used at Australian defense facilities over several decades. The company has faced similar litigation in other countries over PFAS contamination from its products.

The Australian case represents an escalation in government action against manufacturers of PFAS-containing products. The chemicals have been detected in soil and groundwater at numerous defense bases and firefighting training areas.

Forever chemicals earn their nickname because they do not break down naturally in the environment. They accumulate over time and can enter drinking water supplies and the food chain.

The A$2 billion figure reflects the government's estimated costs for cleanup and remediation at affected defense sites. Australia operates military installations across the continent, many of which used firefighting foams for training and emergency response.

3M announced in 2022 that it would stop producing PFAS chemicals by the end of 2025. The company has set aside billions of dollars to address PFAS-related claims globally.

The Australian lawsuit follows years of testing and investigation at defense sites. Environmental regulators have documented PFAS contamination at locations where firefighting foam was regularly deployed.

The case will proceed through Australian courts. The government has not disclosed a timeline for resolution, though complex environmental litigation typically takes years to conclude.

Yesterday, 10:32 PM ET

The Trump administration has launched a criminal investigation into E. Jean Carroll, the writer who accused President Trump of sexual assault, according to reports from CNN and the New York Times.

Prosecutors are examining whether Carroll, 82, committed perjury during a 2022 deposition in her civil lawsuits against Trump. In that deposition, Carroll stated she had not accepted outside financial support for her legal battles. However, approximately six months later, before trial proceedings began, Carroll's attorneys disclosed to the judge and Trump's legal team that a nonprofit funded by Reid Hoffman, the billionaire LinkedIn co-founder, had paid some of her legal fees and expenses.

Trump's lawyers argued that Carroll concealed Hoffman's financial backing and that this concealment undermined her credibility in the case. Carroll's legal representatives countered that she never met or spoke with anyone from the nonprofit organization. Following this disclosure, Trump's attorney Alina Habba was permitted to conduct a second deposition with Carroll to address the funding issue.

In 2024, a three-judge federal appeals court panel in New York dismissed claims that Carroll had committed perjury in her initial deposition.

Carroll, a longtime advice columnist, first made her allegations public in her 2019 book "What Do We Need Men For?" She claimed Trump assaulted her in a New York department store dressing room. Trump has denied the allegations, stating Carroll was "totally lying" and was "not my type."

Carroll's legal battles against Trump have already resulted in significant outcomes. A jury found Trump liable for sexual abuse and defamation, awarding Carroll five million dollars in damages. A separate jury subsequently ordered Trump to pay eighty-three million dollars in a related defamation case. Trump has appealed both judgments.

Todd Blanche, the acting attorney general, has recused himself from the Justice Department investigation due to his prior role representing Trump in Carroll's civil cases.

The investigation represents the latest in a series of criminal inquiries initiated by Trump's Justice Department into political opponents. Federal prosecutors have also investigated former FBI Director James Comey, New York Attorney General Letitia James, and Democratic members of Congress including Adam Schiff and Ilhan Omar. None of these investigations have resulted in criminal convictions to date.

Neither the Justice Department nor Carroll's legal representatives provided immediate comment on the investigation.
