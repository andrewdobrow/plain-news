A few minutes ago

The United States and Iran conducted military strikes against each other around the Strait of Hormuz, according to reports from both governments. The latest exchange marks a continuation of escalating tensions in the region and raises questions about the stability of a ceasefire that appears fragile.

US Central Command forces shot down four Iranian attack drones that posed a threat around the strait. A ground control station in Iran's Bandar Abbas, which was preparing to launch a fifth drone, was also targeted in the strikes. A US official said the actions were "measured, purely defensive, and intended to maintain the ceasefire."

Two hours after the American strikes, Iran's Revolutionary Guards responded with an apparent retaliatory attack on an American air base in Kuwait. The Iranian state broadcaster claimed the base had "served as the source of the attack." Kuwait confirmed that its air defenses intercepted hostile missiles and drone threats early in the morning. American personnel were injured in the Iranian strike on Kuwaiti territory, marking a significant expansion of the conflict beyond direct US-Iran military installations.

The tit-for-tat strikes occurred as Iranian forces also fired at four ships attempting to cross the strait. Iran's state broadcaster reported the incident happened without coordination with security forces, though it provided no details about the vessels.

The exchange of strikes underscores how far apart the US and Iran remain in negotiations aimed at ending the war. Donald Trump has sent mixed signals about his approach, threatening to "finish the job" if Iran does not agree to a deal while also claiming both sides want to reach an agreement. "Either that or we'll have to just finish the job," Trump said at a White House cabinet meeting, adding that Iran was "negotiating on fumes."

Trump also appeared to direct a warning at Oman, a US ally, regarding potential arrangements for managing the Strait of Hormuz. He stated the waterway would be "open to everybody" and suggested Oman would need to cooperate or face consequences. The White House did not immediately clarify his remarks.

A key unresolved issue is whether a US-Iran ceasefire will cover Israel's operations against Hezbollah in Lebanon. Iran has insisted that Lebanon must be included in any ceasefire agreement with the United States. Meanwhile, Israel has intensified its military campaign in Lebanon, with the Israeli military striking over 550 targets since the beginning of the week. More than 1.2 million Lebanese have been displaced since March, and Israeli strikes have killed more than 3,200 people according to Lebanon's health ministry.

A US-brokered ceasefire announced in April now appears close to collapse, with fighting continuing despite the agreement. The escalating conflict continues to affect global oil markets and inflation concerns in the United States.

The acquisition marks the first major deal under newly announced CEO Greg Abel, signaling his willingness to pursue large-scale transactions as he prepares to take over the conglomerate at year-end 2025.

Warren Buffett revealed his retirement plans at Berkshire's annual shareholder meeting, announcing that Abel will become CEO when Buffett steps down after leading the company for 60 years. "I think the time has arrived where Greg should become the chief executive officer of the company at year end," Buffett told thousands of investors gathered in Omaha, Nebraska. The announcement surprised many, as Buffett had previously insisted he had no plans to retire. The crowd responded with a lengthy standing ovation.

Abel, 62, has served as Berkshire's vice chairman and has been the designated successor since 2001. As a 25-year veteran of the company, he already oversees all non-insurance operations, managing dozens of businesses spanning fossil energy, chemicals, real estate, and retail. The Taylor Morrison acquisition demonstrates Abel's approach to deploying Berkshire's substantial cash reserves into sectors aligned with the conglomerate's existing interests.

The homebuilder operates as a national builder with projects across multiple states. Under Berkshire's ownership, Taylor Morrison is expected to maintain operational independence while accessing the parent company's financial resources. The transaction requires regulatory approval and is projected to close later this year.

The deal reflects confidence in residential construction demand despite economic uncertainties. Berkshire has previously invested in housing-related sectors, including building materials and manufactured homes, making this acquisition a natural extension of the company's portfolio.

Buffett also addressed broader economic concerns during the shareholder meeting. He warned about the consequences of tariff policies, stating that "there's no question that trade can be an act of war." He criticized trade restrictions for creating global instability and damaging international relationships. "We should be looking to trade with the rest of the world. We should do what we do best and they should do what they do best," Buffett said.

In February, Berkshire reported record annual operating profits of $47.44 billion in 2024, representing a 27 percent increase. The company operates 189 businesses, and the impact of current trade policies on these operations remains unclear.

Buffett reiterated his long-term commitment to Berkshire, saying he has "zero" intention of selling shares. He plans to eventually donate 99.5 percent of his wealth to charitable causes through a trust managed by his children. According to Forbes, his current net worth stands at $165.3 billion.

NVIDIA announced a new chip designed to bring artificial intelligence capabilities to personal computers, marking its first entry into the PC processor market. The company unveiled the Arm-based processor in partnership with Microsoft, with laptops from Dell and HP also planned to use the chip.

Microsoft introduced the Surface Laptop Ultra as the first device featuring the new NVIDIA processor. The collaboration between NVIDIA and Microsoft aims to integrate AI processing directly into Windows PCs, allowing users to run AI applications locally rather than relying on cloud-based services. The chip architecture uses Arm technology, the same instruction set used in most smartphones, rather than the x86 architecture that has dominated PCs for decades.

The move puts NVIDIA in direct competition with Intel and AMD in the PC processor market. NVIDIA has been the dominant supplier of graphics processors for AI data centers, but this represents its expansion into consumer computing. The company has positioned the chips as purpose-built for what it calls the age of personal AI, suggesting capabilities for running AI agents and applications on individual devices.

Major PC manufacturers have committed to using the new processors. Dell and HP plan to release laptops with the NVIDIA chip, joining Microsoft in the initial product lineup. The announcements indicate a broader industry shift toward incorporating dedicated AI processing hardware into consumer devices.

The timing of the launch follows growing demand for AI capabilities in consumer technology. Tech companies have been racing to integrate AI features into their products, but most current implementations require cloud connectivity and remote servers. Local AI processing could enable faster response times and better privacy by keeping data on the user's device. The success of the new chips will depend on software support, pricing, and whether the AI features prove valuable enough to consumers to justify switching from established Intel and AMD processors.

Yesterday, 8:31 PM ET

Former Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell has issued a stark warning that the central bank faces a critical credibility test, delivering the message during his first public remarks since stepping down from his position. Powell made the comments while accepting the John F. Kennedy Profiles in Courage award on Sunday evening.

Though Powell did not explicitly mention the Trump administration in his remarks, his warning clearly targeted recent efforts to undermine the Fed's independence from political pressure. He described the current moment as a stress test for the central bank, framing threats to its autonomy as a danger to an institution vital to American economic strength.

Powell's concerns reference several specific challenges facing the Fed. These include a criminal investigation into cost overruns on the central bank's headquarters renovation project and attempts by the Trump administration to remove Fed governor Lisa Cook from her position. Powell characterized the investigation into the building renovation as a pretext for pressuring the central bank to lower interest rates.

The Federal Reserve operates as an independent agency responsible for setting monetary policy and interest rates without direct government interference. This independence has long been considered essential to effective economic management and protecting the institution from short-term political manipulation.

Powell's term as chair ended on May 15, when Kevin Warsh officially took over leadership of the central bank following his Senate confirmation on May 14. The confirmation vote was highly contentious, passing 54-45 along party lines with one exception. Democratic Senator John Fetterman of Pennsylvania joined the Republican majority in supporting Warsh's appointment. The vote marked the most divisive confirmation for a Fed chair in history.

Warsh, a 54-year-old economist and former Wall Street banker who previously served as a Fed governor from 2006 to 2011, will lead the central bank during a period of intense scrutiny over its independence. The Trump administration has pressed the Fed to lower interest rates despite inflation currently standing at 3.8 percent. Critics, including Senator Elizabeth Warren, have expressed concern that Warsh will function as Trump's "sock puppet" in controlling monetary policy decisions.

During his Senate banking committee hearing, Warsh pledged to maintain Fed independence and remove politics from monetary policy. However, he declined to answer questions about whether Trump lost the 2020 election, raising alarms among Democrats about his commitment to institutional norms.

Powell announced last month that he would remain on the Fed board as a voting governor rather than retiring after his chair term concluded. He indicated he would stay in this position until the Trump administration ends its scrutiny of the Fed's renovation project.
