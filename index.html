Iran's counter-proposal suggested shorter timelines and more limited concessions. The country offered a shorter enrichment moratorium, partial export of enriched uranium, and refusal to accept facility dismantling.

Yesterday, 9:18 PM ET

At least two people are dead and nine workers remain unrecovered after a chemical tank implosion at a paper mill in Longview, Washington.

The disaster involved white liquor, a caustic chemical used in paper production. Contaminated water from the implosion is being flushed through Longview's ditch system as part of the response effort.

Washington's governor said the incident could be the deadliest industrial tragedy in modern state history. The statement underscores the severity of the accident at the facility.

Emergency responders are working to locate the nine workers who remain unaccounted for. The implosion occurred at a tank containing the chemical solution, which is part of the paper manufacturing process.

White liquor is a mixture of sodium hydroxide and sodium sulfide dissolved in water. The substance is highly alkaline and used to break down wood chips into pulp during papermaking. Contact with the chemical can cause severe burns and other injuries.

Authorities have not disclosed what caused the tank to implode or the full extent of injuries to workers at the scene. The paper mill is a major employer in the Longview area.

The contaminated water cleanup effort involves directing the chemical-laden runoff through existing drainage infrastructure. Officials have not provided details on environmental impacts or risks to nearby waterways.

Search and recovery operations continue for the nine missing workers. The two confirmed deaths add to what is already one of the most serious workplace accidents in recent Washington state history.

The facility remains closed as investigators work to determine the cause of the implosion and assess damage to the tank and surrounding structures.

Yesterday, 8:45 PM ET

Federal prosecutors in New York charged a Google employee with insider trading after he allegedly used confidential company information to make $1.2 million on prediction market bets.

The engineer, identified as Michele Spagnuolo, worked at Google and allegedly accessed nonpublic data about search trends through his position. He then used that information to place wagers on Polymarket, a platform where users bet on real-world events including political outcomes, economic indicators, and other measurable occurrences.

According to the unsealed complaint, Spagnuolo placed bets related to Google's 2025 Year in Search campaign and risked more than $2.7 million on the wagers. Prosecutors say he knew the outcome of these bets before the general public because he had accessed Google's confidential information about search-related trends.

The charges represent a notable application of insider trading law to prediction markets. While insider trading statutes traditionally apply to securities markets, federal authorities argue the same legal principles extend to any market where nonpublic information provides an unfair advantage. The case marks only the second known instance of the federal government filing criminal charges against someone for allegedly using insider information to profit on a prediction market platform.

Search trend data represents particularly valuable information because it can reveal shifting public interest before it becomes widely known. Spagnuolo's access to Google's internal systems gave him an advantage unavailable to other bettors on Polymarket.

Prediction markets like Polymarket have grown in popularity as a forecasting tool based on collective predictions. Users can profit when their predictions prove correct. The platform has gained attention recently for allowing bets on various high-stakes events, raising concerns about potential misuse of classified or confidential information.

The $1.2 million in alleged profits suggests Spagnuolo made multiple successful bets over a period of time. Prosecutors have not disclosed which specific events or search trends the bets covered or the duration of the alleged scheme.

Insider trading charges carry potential prison sentences and substantial financial penalties. The defendant faces prosecution under federal laws designed to ensure fair markets by preventing individuals with privileged access to information from exploiting it for personal gain.

Google has not publicly commented on the charges. The company employs thousands of engineers who have access to various types of internal data as part of their regular work responsibilities. The case highlights the challenges companies face in monitoring employees' personal financial activities, particularly as prediction markets and cryptocurrency-based betting platforms continue to expand.

Yesterday, 10:47 PM ET

The Australian government filed a lawsuit against American manufacturing giant 3M over contamination from chemicals used in firefighting foam at defense sites across the country. The case seeks A$2 billion in damages and represents the largest legal action ever brought by the Australian government.

The lawsuit centers on contamination caused by per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, commonly known as PFAS or forever chemicals, which were used in firefighting foam at military installations. These synthetic chemicals earned their nickname because they do not break down naturally in the environment and can persist for thousands of years.

3M, a multinational conglomerate headquartered in Minnesota, manufactured the firefighting foam products that contained these chemicals. The foam was widely used at defense facilities for its effectiveness in suppressing fuel fires.

PFAS chemicals have raised health and environmental concerns globally because they accumulate in soil, water, and human bodies over time. Scientists have linked exposure to these substances with various health problems, though the Australian government has not specified the extent of contamination at the affected defense sites or the number of locations involved.

The Australian legal action follows similar lawsuits against 3M in other countries. In the United States, 3M agreed to pay more than $10 billion to settle claims related to PFAS contamination of public water systems. The company also faced litigation over contamination at military bases and airports where the firefighting foam was used.

3M stopped manufacturing PFAS-based firefighting foam in 2002 and announced in 2022 that it would phase out all PFAS production by the end of 2025. However, the legacy contamination from decades of use remains a persistent problem at sites around the world.

The Australian case marks an escalation in government efforts to hold manufacturers accountable for environmental damage from forever chemicals. The A$2 billion figure reflects the scale of cleanup costs and potential remediation required at contaminated defense installations.

Neither 3M nor Australian government officials provided additional details about which specific defense sites are affected or the timeline for legal proceedings.

Crystal Palace secured their first European trophy after Jean-Philippe Mateta scored the decisive goal in their Conference League final victory over Rayo Vallecano in Leipzig. The triumph represents a historic moment for the club, which had never previously won a continental competition.

The win proved particularly significant given the circumstances surrounding it. Palace had been denied a place in this season's Europa League despite winning the FA Cup last year, after being judged to have breached European football's multi-club ownership rules. This Conference League success now grants them automatic qualification for next season's Europa League.

It was a fitting finale for manager Oliver Glasner, who departs after transforming the club's fortunes. The Austrian performed a full-length dive on the pitch in celebration before collecting his winners' medal. With this victory, Glasner has now won three trophies in 12 months, including last season's FA Cup and Community Shield.

Palace struggled in the opening half, with Rayo dominating possession and creating the better chances. The Spanish side, competing in their first final in 102 years, posed constant threats down their flanks. However, Glasner's tactical adjustments proved decisive after the interval.

The decisive moment came early in the second half when Mateta showed instinctive finishing to convert from close range after Adam Wharton's shot was parried by Rayo goalkeeper Augusto Batalla. The French striker's rebound finish sparked wild celebrations among Palace's supporters.

The match remained tense as Rayo pressed for an equalizer. Batalla was called upon to make crucial saves, while Palace's defence, anchored by Maxence Lacroix, held firm under sustained pressure. Rayo manager Iñigo Pérez used all his substitutes in attempting to breach Palace's resistance, but they could not find a way through.

Palace's chairman Steve Parish attended the match alongside Uefa president Aleksander Ceferin, witnessing the club's greatest night since he rescued them from administration 16 years ago. The victory resonated deeply with the travelling support, with thousands of Palace fans gathering in Leipzig to witness the historic achievement.

Approximately 15,000 Palace supporters made the journey to Germany, replicating the marches that had preceded their FA Cup final appearance against Manchester City the previous year. Both sets of supporters displayed large tifos before kick-off, with Palace's banner referencing their remarkable journey from the brink of extinction to winning their first major trophy.

The Conference League triumph concludes an extraordinary period under Glasner's management and provides Palace with entry into Europe's second-tier competition for the coming season, elevating the club to the level where ambitions can now be properly realised.

US forces intercepted four Iranian one-way attack drones that posed a threat to a commercial ship in the strait. The military then struck a ground control station in Bandar Abbas, a port city on Iran's southern coast, that was preparing to launch a fifth drone, a US official told reporters on condition of anonymity.

This marks the second military clash between US and Iranian forces in 48 hours, even as the two countries are negotiating an agreement to end their conflict. The escalation occurred while diplomatic talks aimed at resolving the broader war continue.

President Trump addressed the Iran situation during a Cabinet meeting Wednesday, where his remarks differed from statements he made over the weekend suggesting an agreement was close. Trump held to what officials described as maximum demands on Iran during the meeting.

The commercial vessel targeted by the Iranian drones was in the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway through which significant global oil shipments pass. US Central Command confirmed its forces shot down the Iranian drones but did not provide additional details about the commercial ship or potential damage.

The Iranian military had fired the four one-way drones at the ship before US intervention, the senior official said. The ground station strike in Bandar Abbas was intended to prevent the launch of additional drones.

The incident highlights ongoing tensions between Washington and Tehran despite active diplomatic efforts. US and Iranian negotiators have been working toward a deal to end the conflict, though the timing and terms of any agreement remain uncertain following these latest military actions.

The lawsuit centers on contamination caused by per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, commonly known as PFAS or forever chemicals, which were used in firefighting foam at military installations. These synthetic chemicals earned their nickname because they do not break down naturally in the environment and can persist for thousands of years.

3M, a multinational conglomerate headquartered in Minnesota, manufactured the firefighting foam products that contained these chemicals. The foam was widely used at defense facilities for its effectiveness in suppressing fuel fires.

PFAS chemicals have raised health and environmental concerns globally because they accumulate in soil, water, and human bodies over time. Scientists have linked exposure to these substances with various health problems, though the Australian government has not specified the extent of contamination at the affected defense sites or the number of locations involved.

The Australian legal action follows similar lawsuits against 3M in other countries. In the United States, 3M agreed to pay more than $10 billion to settle claims related to PFAS contamination of public water systems. The company also faced litigation over contamination at military bases and airports where the firefighting foam was used.

3M stopped manufacturing PFAS-based firefighting foam in 2002 and announced in 2022 that it would phase out all PFAS production by the end of 2025. However, the legacy contamination from decades of use remains a persistent problem at sites around the world.

The Australian case marks an escalation in government efforts to hold manufacturers accountable for environmental damage from forever chemicals. The A$2 billion figure reflects the scale of cleanup costs and potential remediation required at contaminated defense installations.

Neither 3M nor Australian government officials provided additional details about which specific defense sites are affected or the timeline for legal proceedings.
