Yesterday, 5:06 PM ET

A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded during a static fire test at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station's Launch Complex 36 in Florida on Thursday night at 9pm ET. The massive fireball destroyed the launchpad and was visible from more than 100 miles away, with residents in South Carolina reporting seeing a glow in the sky.

Blue Origin described the incident as "an anomaly" and confirmed that all employees were safe and uninjured. Jeff Bezos, the company's founder, acknowledged the setback on social media, saying "It's too early to know the root cause but we're already working to find it. Very rough day, but we'll rebuild whatever needs rebuilding and get back to flying. It's worth it."

The timing of the explosion created significant complications for NASA's lunar ambitions. Just one day before the incident, NASA announced that Blue Origin had won a contract to launch the first of three planned missions in 2026 to begin construction of NASA's 20 billion dollar moon base. The New Glenn rocket is the vehicle Blue Origin intends to use for launching lunar landers to support NASA's Artemis program, which aims to return humans to the moon.

The explosion threatens NASA's timeline for the Artemis missions. Jared Isaacman, NASA's administrator, stated that "a full evaluation of that timeline will be conducted after the explosion." He noted that "spaceflight is unforgiving, and developing new heavy-lift launch capability is extraordinarily difficult."

This represents the latest setback for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA contracts. Last month, a payload from the third flight of New Glenn ended up in the wrong orbit, leading the Federal Aviation Administration to temporarily ground the rocket. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared the rocket to return to flight the previous week.

The explosion also impacts the broader competition for lunar lander contracts. Both Blue Origin and SpaceX are in competition to provide a lunar lander for the Artemis IV mission planned for 2027, which will determine which company's vehicle will ferry astronauts from the Orion capsule to the lunar surface.

Shockwaves from the explosion were felt across Florida's Space Coast, with homes shaking in Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach. Flames continued burning at the launchpad more than two hours after the blast. Emergency officials reported no threat from fumes or other hazards.

The incident contrasts sharply with SpaceX's recent successes in establishing itself as the dominant player in the commercial launch market. For Blue Origin, the explosion presents a major challenge as the company works to demonstrate the reliability needed to secure both commercial and government contracts.

6:00 AM ET

The Trump administration has proposed requiring federal workers to sign nondisclosure agreements, a move that has drawn criticism from those who say the White House is attempting to silence its workforce.

The Office of Personnel Management proposed the policy on Tuesday. The proposal would apply to both new and existing federal employees.

Critics say the nondisclosure agreements would restrict federal workers from speaking to the press. The proposal has generated backlash from multiple quarters over concerns about limiting government transparency.

The Office of Personnel Management stated the agreements were necessary, though the specific justification provided in the proposal was not detailed in available information. The policy would represent a significant change in how federal employees operate, potentially limiting their ability to share information about government operations.

The proposal comes as the administration faces ongoing scrutiny over its relationship with the federal workforce. Federal employees have traditionally operated under different disclosure rules than private sector workers, given the public nature of government work.

The nondisclosure agreements would need to go through a formal process before implementation. The proposal marks the latest effort by the administration to exert control over information flow from federal agencies.

Opponents argue the agreements could prevent whistleblowers from reporting waste, fraud, or abuse. Supporters of the measure have not yet publicly outlined their full rationale for the policy change.

The Office of Personnel Management oversees human resources policy for the federal government and manages the civilian workforce. Its proposals typically undergo review and comment periods before taking effect.

The reaction to the proposal has been swift, with various groups expressing concern about the implications for government accountability and the public's right to know about federal operations.
