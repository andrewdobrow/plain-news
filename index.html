The House of Representatives voted to limit presidential authority to conduct military operations against Iran without congressional approval. The measure passed 215 to 208, with four Republicans joining Democrats in support of the resolution.

The vote represents a bipartisan rebuke to President Trump following recent military strikes on Iran. The resolution seeks to prevent the president from ordering further military action against Iran without explicit authorization from Congress. The measure is largely symbolic, as it faces uncertain prospects in the Senate and potential veto by the president.

The four Republicans who crossed party lines to support the measure joined all voting Democrats in backing the war powers resolution. The vote split largely along partisan lines, with the majority of Republicans opposing the effort to constrain executive military authority. The close vote margin reflects ongoing divisions within Congress over presidential war powers and the extent of legislative oversight on military operations.

The resolution comes amid heightened tensions between the United States and Iran. By passing this measure, the House asserted its constitutional role in decisions about military engagement. War powers resolutions aim to require congressional authorization before presidents can commit U.S. forces to sustained military operations.

The vote demonstrates growing congressional resistance to unilateral executive military action. Even as a symbolic gesture, the resolution signals that a bipartisan group of lawmakers wants to establish boundaries on presidential authority to initiate hostilities. The Trump administration has not yet formally responded to the House vote, and the measure's future remains uncertain as it moves to the Senate, where its passage is not guaranteed.

12:01 AM ET

Nvidia has announced a partnership with Microsoft to introduce new laptop chips designed to run artificial intelligence models directly on personal computers without relying on cloud connectivity. The collaboration centers on the RTX Spark chip and the Vera central processing unit, paired with Microsoft's Windows software on devices like the Surface Laptop Ultra.

The RTX Spark represents Nvidia's bid to shift AI computing from remote data centers to consumer devices. Developed over three years with help from Taiwan's MediaTek, the chip combines microprocessor and graphics capabilities to run AI agents locally on laptops and desktops. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang described this as reinventing the PC for the AI era, noting the technology could eventually replace traditional mouse and keyboard interactions by allowing AI agents to navigate computers autonomously.

The move marks Nvidia's expansion beyond its dominant position in data center AI chips into consumer hardware. Huang positioned the announcement as part of a strategy to compete across every layer of the AI technology stack. Computer makers including Dell, Lenovo, Asus and HP plan to use the RTX Spark chip in their devices.

According to Neil Shah, co-founder of Counterpoint Research, the RTX Spark represents a transformational moment comparable to the iPhone or ChatGPT launch. He predicted the chip could transform traditional app-focused computers into useful AI personal computers with autonomous agents that would eventually become common in homes worldwide.

Despite the strategic significance, analysts suggest this venture represents a longer-term growth opportunity rather than an immediate earnings driver for Nvidia. The company's near-term fortunes remain heavily dependent on continued global demand for data center AI infrastructure and computing power.

Huang also announced plans for at least two additional generations of RTX Spark chips, signaling Nvidia's long-term commitment to the consumer PC market. The Vera CPU is already being evaluated by companies including OpenAI, Anthropic and SpaceX for early adoption.

The announcement intensifies competition in the AI hardware market. Intel plans to launch its own AI graphics chip, the Xe3P codenamed Crescent Island, later this year, positioning it as purpose-built for the upcoming generation of AI agents. The move pits Nvidia against established competitors including Intel, Apple, Qualcomm and AMD.

When asked about concerns that AI technology might eliminate software engineering jobs, Huang dismissed such worries as "complete nonsense," arguing that AI would increase productivity and lead to more hiring of software engineers rather than fewer.

The companies have not yet provided specific release dates or pricing for the new hardware. Nvidia will supply the specialized AI processing chips while Microsoft manufactures the Surface Laptop Ultra.

Russell Wilson, the veteran quarterback who led the Seattle Seahawks to their first Super Bowl championship in 2014, has retired from professional football at age 37. The announcement ends a 13-year career that established Wilson as one of the most successful quarterbacks of his generation.

Wilson spent the first decade of his career with the Seahawks after being drafted in the third round of the 2012 NFL Draft. He quickly became the face of the franchise, leading Seattle to back-to-back Super Bowl appearances in the 2013 and 2014 seasons. The Seahawks defeated the Denver Broncos 43-8 in Super Bowl XLVIII, securing the franchise's first championship. Wilson's dual-threat ability as both a passer and scrambler revolutionized expectations for quarterback play in the modern NFL.

During his time in Seattle, Wilson earned nine Pro Bowl selections and was known for his ability to orchestrate dramatic fourth-quarter comebacks. His leadership helped establish one of the most dominant stretches in Seahawks history, with the team consistently competing for playoff berths throughout his tenure. After leaving Seattle, Wilson played for other NFL teams in the latter portion of his career.

Wilson's retirement comes at a time when the NFL continues to see generational shifts at the quarterback position. At 37, he joins a wave of veteran signal-callers from the 2010s era who have stepped away from the game in recent years. His career statistics and championship pedigree make him a likely candidate for future Hall of Fame consideration.

The retirement marks the end of an era for Seahawks fans and NFL observers who watched Wilson transform from a overlooked third-round pick into one of the league's most recognizable stars. His impact on the Seahawks franchise and his contributions to changing how teams evaluate mobile quarterbacks will be part of his lasting legacy in professional football.

Trump signs order allowing thousands of federal employees to be fired without due process

President Trump signed an executive order that removes job protections from approximately 8,000 high-ranking federal workers, making it simpler for the administration to terminate them without following standard procedures. The order strips protections from senior government employees earning up to nearly $200,000 annually who are deemed to be "influencing" government policy....

A few minutes ago

President Trump signed an executive order that removes job protections from approximately 8,000 high-ranking federal workers, making it simpler for the administration to terminate them without following standard procedures.

The order strips protections from senior government employees earning up to nearly $200,000 annually who are deemed to be "influencing" government policy. These workers previously enjoyed civil service protections that required documented reasons and formal proceedings before dismissal. Under the new order, supervisors can fire them at will, similar to private sector employees.

The reclassification applies to a mostly senior group of federal workers across various government agencies. These positions have traditionally been insulated from political pressure and operate under rules designed to ensure they serve multiple administrations impartially. The executive order fundamentally changes this arrangement by making these roles subject to immediate termination.

This action represents part of the Trump administration's broader effort to reshape the federal workforce. The White House and the Office of Personnel Management released the order on Wednesday as part of what administration officials describe as a necessary overhaul of government operations.

Supporters of removing these job protections argue that doing so increases accountability and allows administrations greater flexibility to implement their policy goals. They contend that streamlining the firing process makes government more responsive and efficient.

Opponents, including federal employee unions and advocacy groups, argue that stripping these protections politicizes the civil service. They contend that career government workers should remain independent from political pressure to ensure they make decisions based on merit and expertise rather than political loyalty. Critics warn that removing safeguards could lead to partisan purges of qualified professionals.

The affected employees work in positions traditionally involving policy influence, senior analysis, and comparable roles that shape how agencies operate. The practical consequences will depend on how individual agencies implement the new classification system and whether affected workers mount legal challenges.

Federal employee unions are expected to contest the order, arguing it violates civil service laws enacted specifically to prevent political interference in the career workforce. They may pursue legal action or file administrative complaints challenging the reclassification.

The order builds on previous Trump administration initiatives aimed at expanding executive control over federal agencies and reducing the size of government. Earlier actions included revoking a Biden-era executive order related to AI standards, reflecting the administration's broader deregulatory approach.

The timing and scope of how agencies apply this new classification system remain unclear. Implementation will likely trigger significant legal and administrative challenges as unions and employee advocacy groups respond to what they view as a threat to the civil service system.
