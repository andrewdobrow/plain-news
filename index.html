U.S. military personnel have been identified and tracked using location information purchased from the advertising technology industry, federal officials have confirmed. The disclosure has prompted lawmakers to escalate calls for treating the adtech sector as a national security threat.

The revelation underscores mounting congressional anxiety about how location data harvested from smartphones and mobile applications can be bought and weaponized to monitor the movements of military personnel and other sensitive populations.

The advertising technology industry routinely collects precise location information from mobile apps and websites, then packages and sells this data to brokers who distribute it widely. This information can reveal movement patterns and identify individuals based on their homes, workplaces, and travel routes. Security experts have long flagged the potential for foreign adversaries to exploit such data for harmful purposes.

The targeting of U.S. troops through commercially purchased location information demonstrates that these theoretical dangers have materialized into concrete security risks. Military installations and service members face particular vulnerability because their movements can expose sensitive operational details.

The industry has previously contended that its data collection practices are sufficiently anonymized to pose minimal privacy concerns. However, researchers have consistently shown that location data enables individual identification even when names and obvious identifiers are removed. The geographic patterns of people's daily lives, particularly visits to residences and offices, frequently provide sufficient detail to determine identity.

This incident has energized legislative momentum to regulate data brokers and constrain the sale of Americans' location information. Recent congressional proposals would prohibit data brokers from selling sensitive location data, particularly information that could track military personnel, intelligence officials, or other government employees.

One prominent privacy advocate stated it was time to begin "treating the adtech industry as a national security threat," reflecting the seriousness with which lawmakers now view the issue.

The Pentagon has implemented limited protective measures, including issuing guidance to service members about applications that collect location data. However, officials acknowledge that voluntary approaches may prove inadequate if the underlying data marketplace continues operating without meaningful restrictions.

The incident parallels recent enforcement actions against companies that mishandle consumer data. In a separate case, General Motors agreed to pay 12.75 million dollars to resolve claims it sold hundreds of thousands of Californians' location and driving information to data brokers without consent. The company had collected the data through its OnStar subscription service while generating approximately 20 million dollars from these sales between 2020 and 2024.

The GM settlement included a five-year prohibition on selling consumer data to data brokers, demonstrating regulators' willingness to impose strict penalties for unauthorized data sales.

White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt said the administration feels "good about the prospects of a deal," though she emphasized that nothing is official until confirmed by the White House. She noted that a second round of negotiations would likely be held in Islamabad.

Pakistan's army chief, Field Marshal Asim Munir, traveled to Tehran to convey messages from Washington and help arrange further ceasefire talks. Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif simultaneously embarked on a four-day diplomatic tour through Saudi Arabia, Qatar, and Turkey to build regional support for a peace agreement.

Iran's foreign ministry spokesperson said views from both sides would be discussed in detail during these visits. However, sources in Tehran indicated Iran has demanded an end to Israeli attacks on Lebanon as a precondition for additional negotiations.

The agreement addresses two critical issues: extending the ceasefire to prevent renewed conflict and reopening the strategic waterway that handles global oil shipments.

Trump has made aggressive statements regarding the Strait of Hormuz. During a cabinet meeting, he threatened to "blow up" Oman if the country failed to "behave," referencing reports that Iran and Oman had discussed jointly charging a toll for ships passing through the waterway.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent reinforced this position, stating that "the United States Government will not tolerate any effort to impose a tolling system in the Strait of Hormuz." Bessent also warned of escalating financial pressure on Iran, including secondary sanctions against countries and companies purchasing Iranian oil or holding Iranian funds.

The U.S. military has already implemented a naval blockade of Iranian ports, with American warships turning back nine ships that attempted to cross the strait, including a Chinese-owned tanker under U.S. sanctions.

Iran's military leadership has responded to the blockade, with a senior commander warning that Iran could halt trade in the Gulf region if the U.S. did not lift its port restrictions.

Despite the tentative agreement, Trump has not yet indicated whether he will approve the arrangement. The agreement represents a potential diplomatic shift after months of heightened regional tensions, though officials describe the current ceasefire as fragile. The 60-day timeframe would provide both sides an opportunity to negotiate longer-term solutions.

U.S. military personnel have been identified and tracked using location information purchased from the advertising technology industry, federal officials have confirmed. The disclosure has prompted lawmakers to escalate...

1:00 PM ET

U.S. military personnel have been identified and tracked using location information purchased from the advertising technology industry, federal officials have confirmed. The disclosure has prompted lawmakers to escalate calls for treating the adtech sector as a national security threat.

The revelation underscores mounting congressional anxiety about how location data harvested from smartphones and mobile applications can be bought and weaponized to monitor the movements of military personnel and other sensitive populations.

The advertising technology industry routinely collects precise location information from mobile apps and websites, then packages and sells this data to brokers who distribute it widely. This information can reveal movement patterns and identify individuals based on their homes, workplaces, and travel routes. Security experts have long flagged the potential for foreign adversaries to exploit such data for harmful purposes.

The targeting of U.S. troops through commercially purchased location information demonstrates that these theoretical dangers have materialized into concrete security risks. Military installations and service members face particular vulnerability because their movements can expose sensitive operational details.

The industry has previously contended that its data collection practices are sufficiently anonymized to pose minimal privacy concerns. However, researchers have consistently shown that location data enables individual identification even when names and obvious identifiers are removed. The geographic patterns of people's daily lives, particularly visits to residences and offices, frequently provide sufficient detail to determine identity.

This incident has energized legislative momentum to regulate data brokers and constrain the sale of Americans' location information. Recent congressional proposals would prohibit data brokers from selling sensitive location data, particularly information that could track military personnel, intelligence officials, or other government employees.

One prominent privacy advocate stated it was time to begin "treating the adtech industry as a national security threat," reflecting the seriousness with which lawmakers now view the issue.

The Pentagon has implemented limited protective measures, including issuing guidance to service members about applications that collect location data. However, officials acknowledge that voluntary approaches may prove inadequate if the underlying data marketplace continues operating without meaningful restrictions.

The incident parallels recent enforcement actions against companies that mishandle consumer data. In a separate case, General Motors agreed to pay 12.75 million dollars to resolve claims it sold hundreds of thousands of Californians' location and driving information to data brokers without consent. The company had collected the data through its OnStar subscription service while generating approximately 20 million dollars from these sales between 2020 and 2024.

The GM settlement included a five-year prohibition on selling consumer data to data brokers, demonstrating regulators' willingness to impose strict penalties for unauthorized data sales.
