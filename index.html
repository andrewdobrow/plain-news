A Russian drone struck a residential apartment building in Romania, wounding two civilians, according to Romanian officials. Romania is a member of NATO, and the incident has sharply escalated tensions between the security alliance and Moscow.

NATO condemned what it called Russia's recklessness following the strike. Romania's foreign minister said the country could invoke Article 4 of NATO's founding treaty, a clause that allows member states to request consultations when they believe their territorial integrity, political independence, or security is threatened.

Romanian authorities stated that the drone was likely hit over Ukraine by Ukrainian air defenses, which altered its trajectory and caused it to enter Romanian airspace before striking the residential building. The incident represents a rare case of Russian military hardware causing casualties on NATO territory.

The European Union also condemned the strike. Both NATO and EU officials emphasized the serious nature of an incident involving a weapon from an ongoing conflict crossing into the territory of a member state.

Article 4 is distinct from NATO's Article 5, which covers collective defense and treats an attack on one member as an attack on all. Article 4 has been invoked multiple times in NATO's history, most recently by Turkey and Poland in situations involving security concerns along their borders.

The strike comes as Russia continues its military operations in Ukraine. The incident highlights the risks of the conflict spilling beyond Ukraine's borders, particularly for neighboring NATO members. Romania shares a border with Ukraine and has been directly affected by the war through refugee flows and occasional incursions of military debris.

Russian officials have not yet commented on the incident. The two wounded civilians were being treated for their injuries, though the severity was not immediately disclosed by Romanian authorities.

A few minutes ago

U.S. companies have avoided at least $40 billion in taxes since the beginning of 2025 through schemes in Malta, Bermuda, Cyprus, and other jurisdictions, following a policy change by the Trump administration.

The administration cleared the way for companies to use these tax havens, allowing corporations to shift profits and reduce their U.S. tax obligations through structures established in low-tax jurisdictions.

Malta, Cyprus, and Bermuda are among the primary locations companies have used to implement these tax strategies. These jurisdictions typically offer minimal corporate tax rates or favorable tax treatment for certain types of income.

The $40 billion figure represents taxes that would have been paid to the U.S. Treasury under previous rules. The amount covers the period from January 2025 through the present.

The policy change reversed restrictions that had limited how companies could use foreign subsidiaries and special corporate structures to reduce their tax bills. Under the new approach, corporations gained expanded ability to route income through these jurisdictions.

Companies using these schemes operate within the framework approved by the administration. The structures typically involve establishing subsidiaries or special purpose entities in the tax haven jurisdictions, then arranging transactions that allow profits to be attributed to those low-tax locations rather than the United States.

The tax reduction strategies affect federal revenue at a time when the government faces ongoing budget considerations. The $40 billion represents funds that did not flow into the U.S. Treasury over approximately 17 months.

Business groups have long advocated for more favorable treatment of overseas profits and foreign operations, arguing that U.S. tax rules put American companies at a disadvantage compared to foreign competitors. Critics counter that tax haven schemes allow profitable corporations to avoid contributing their fair share to government operations.

The administration has not announced plans to reverse the policy or close access to these tax structures. Companies continue to have access to the Malta, Bermuda, and Cyprus routes for tax planning purposes.

The board of Universal Music Group has unanimously rejected a takeover proposal from Bill Ackman's Pershing Square Capital Management. The offer valued the world's largest music company at approximately 55 billion euros, or $64 billion.

Pershing Square presented the unsolicited bid as a combination of cash and stock that would relocate UMG's listing from Amsterdam to the New York Stock Exchange. Under the terms, shareholders would receive 9.4 billion euros in cash plus 0.77 shares in the new combined company for each Universal share held. This represented a 78 percent premium over UMG's closing share price on the day before the offer was announced.

The rejection came one day after Vincent Bollore, the French billionaire and UMG's largest shareholder, publicly opposed the deal. Bollore and his family control approximately 28 percent of UMG through direct ownership and his Vivendi media conglomerate. His swift and public opposition likely shaped the board's rapid decision to reject the proposal.

In his announcement, Ackman acknowledged that UMG's management team, led by Sir Lucian Grainge, had performed well. However, he attributed the company's depressed stock performance to issues unrelated to its music business operations. He cited several factors including delays in establishing a US listing, underutilization of the company's balance sheet, and uncertainty surrounding Bollore's stake as reasons the market had undervalued the company.

Ackman also pointed to what he considered inadequate investor credit for UMG's 2.7 billion euro stake in Spotify. Under the proposed deal, the music company would merge with a blank-check company established by Pershing Square before listing on the New York Stock Exchange.

As part of the proposal, Ackman indicated plans to restructure UMG's leadership. He suggested adding Michael Ovitz, a veteran talent agent, as chair and two Pershing Square representatives to the board. The proposal also included negotiating new employment terms with Grainge, who earned over 41 million euros in the previous year through salary and bonuses.

Pershing Square had previously acquired a 10 percent stake in UMG in 2021, though Ackman resigned from the company's board the following year, citing other commitments. When announcing the bid, Ackman stated he had discussed the proposal with Bollore and claimed the shareholders were intrigued. He expressed confidence the offer addressed stakeholder concerns.

UMG's roster includes major artists such as Taylor Swift, Adele, Drake, and Ariana Grande. The company ranks among the "big three" record labels alongside Sony Music Entertainment and Warner Music Group. UMG's shares had declined more than 25 percent over the previous year before the offer announcement triggered a 13 percent price increase.
