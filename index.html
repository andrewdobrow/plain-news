12:06 AM ET

Uganda has closed its border with the Democratic Republic of the Congo following an outbreak of Ebola caused by the Bundibugyo virus. The outbreak has affected both countries, with health officials confirming cases on both sides of the border.

In the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the outbreak has proven more severe than initially reported. Health officials confirmed 65 deaths and 246 suspected cases in Ituri province, which borders Uganda and South Sudan. The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention detected the Ebola virus in 13 of 20 samples tested. Uganda's health ministry confirmed its own outbreak, reporting that a 59-year-old man died in a Kampala hospital after traveling from the DRC.

The Bundibugyo strain is particularly concerning because no licensed vaccine exists for it. The Democratic Republic of the Congo has experienced 16 Ebola outbreaks since the virus was first identified there in 1976, but most have involved the Zaire strain, for which vaccines are available. The current outbreak marks only the third known occurrence of the Bundibugyo variant, with previous cases reported in 2007 and 2012.

The situation is complicated by geographic and social factors. Ituri province contains mining towns with constant movement of people, making disease control challenging. The area also experiences ongoing conflict, which has weakened health infrastructure and hindered response efforts. The World Health Organization chief called for an immediate ceasefire in eastern Congo to address what he described as a "catastrophic collision of disease and conflict."

The World Health Organization estimates the death rate among confirmed cases at between 30 and 50 percent. A WHO epidemiologist stated this means "up to five out of 10 people are likely to die." Early detection and isolation remain the primary tools for controlling the outbreak, along with contact tracing and isolation facilities.

Ebola spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals, including blood and vomit, as well as through contact with dead bodies during funeral preparations. The virus causes severe illness with a high fatality rate, though supportive care can improve survival chances.

The Africa CDC convened an urgent meeting with authorities from the DRC, Uganda, and South Sudan, along with the World Health Organization and pharmaceutical companies. Officials emphasized that rapid regional coordination is essential given the high population movement between affected areas and neighboring countries.

Uganda has experience managing Ebola outbreaks, having dealt with multiple incidents over the past two decades. The country has developed protocols for rapid response to contain spread and protect public health.

A few minutes ago

A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket detonated on a launchpad at Cape Canaveral in Florida during a scheduled test on Thursday evening, creating a massive fireball that destroyed the launch facility and threatening NASA's ambitious plans for lunar exploration.

The explosion occurred at 9pm ET during a "hotfire" test at Launch Complex 36. The blast was so powerful that an orange sky was visible in Fort Pierce, over 115 miles away. Shockwaves rippled across Florida's space coast, causing homes to shake in nearby Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach. Residents in South Carolina, hundreds of miles north, reported seeing a glow in the sky. Flames continued burning at the launchpad for more than two hours after the incident.

Blue Origin founder Jeff Bezos confirmed that all company employees were safe and uninjured. He described it as a "very rough day" in a social media post, saying the company was already investigating the cause and would rebuild and return to flight operations. The company referred to the incident as an "anomaly."

The explosion creates significant complications for NASA's lunar program. Just two days before the blast, NASA announced that Blue Origin had won a contract to launch the first of three planned uncrewed lunar missions this year to begin construction of a $20 billion moon base. NASA administrator Jared Isaacman stated that the agency would conduct a full evaluation of its timeline for the Artemis program following the explosion.

The New Glenn rocket was designed to launch Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander, which NASA selected for the Artemis III mission scheduled for 2027. This mission will test both Blue Origin's lander and SpaceX's Starship system to determine which will carry astronauts to the lunar surface for the Artemis IV mission planned for 2028, the first crewed moon landing since 1972.

The explosion marks another setback for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA contracts. Last month, a payload from the rocket's third flight ended up in the wrong orbit, leading the Federal Aviation Administration to temporarily ground the vehicle. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared the rocket to return to flight.

Isaacman emphasized that developing heavy-lift launch capabilities is "extraordinarily difficult" and pledged NASA's support for a thorough investigation. He indicated the agency would provide updates on any impacts to the Artemis and lunar base programs as information became available.

SpaceX CEO Elon Musk responded briefly to the incident on social media, calling it "most unfortunate" and noting that "rockets are hard."

Emergency officials reported no threat from fumes or other hazards from the explosion.
