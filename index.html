Oil Prices Rise After U.S. and Iran Exchange Military Strikes

4:16 PM ET

The United States carried out new strikes on Iranian military targets, including a ground control station in Bandar Abbas, while also shooting down four Iranian attack drones near the Strait of Hormuz. The military action prompted what appeared to be a retaliatory strike from Iran on an American airbase in Kuwait, marking another escalation in the ongoing conflict between the two nations.

U.S. Central Command forces intercepted the Iranian drones that posed a threat around the strait. According to U.S. officials, the strikes were "measured, purely defensive, and intended to maintain the ceasefire." Two hours after the American strikes, Iran's Revolutionary Guards claimed they had targeted the American base in Kuwait in response. Kuwait confirmed its air defenses were intercepting hostile missile and drone threats early Thursday morning.

The confrontation also extended to commercial shipping. Iranian forces fired at four vessels attempting to cross the Strait of Hormuz without coordination with security forces, according to Iranian state broadcaster IRIB, though no details were provided about the ships or any damage.

Oil prices spiked in reaction to the military exchange as traders assessed risks to global energy supplies flowing from the Persian Gulf region. However, markets showed some restraint after President Trump indicated that negotiations with Iran were continuing, creating volatility throughout the trading session. Bond yields also climbed as investors repositioned portfolios in response to the geopolitical uncertainty.

The fresh strikes came as Trump made combative statements about Iran and the strait. At a White House cabinet meeting, Trump said "Either that or we'll have to just finish the job" regarding Iran negotiations, claiming Iran was "negotiating on fumes." He also appeared to direct a warning at Oman, a U.S. ally, regarding control of the Strait of Hormuz, saying it would be "open to everybody" and that "Oman will behave just like everybody else or we'll have to blow them up."

A key unresolved issue in ceasefire talks involves whether any agreement will cover Israel's military operations against Hezbollah in Lebanon. Iran has insisted Lebanon must be included in any ceasefire agreement. Meanwhile, Israeli forces have intensified operations in Lebanon, with the military striking 550 targets since the beginning of the week and Israeli troops pushing farther north into the country.

Stock markets ultimately closed higher despite the oil price surge, with major indexes reaching record levels. Technology stocks helped offset concerns about rising energy costs, and the Nasdaq also hit new highs. Investors appeared to take some comfort from Trump's indication that negotiations remained active, even as both countries continued military posturing.

The situation remains uncertain as the U.S. and Iran maintain both military confrontation and diplomatic channels. Energy markets are likely to remain sensitive to developments in both military movements and negotiation status as traders weigh the risks of further escalation against the possibility of diplomatic resolution.

Russian forces launched a massive assault on Kyiv using hypersonic and ballistic missiles, killing at least four people and wounding approximately 100 across the country, according to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy.

The attack involved around 600 drones and 90 missiles of various types, including 36 ballistic missiles. Russia employed its Oreshnik hypersonic missile for the third time in the conflict, along with Iskander, Kinzhal and Zircon missiles. The Russian defense ministry claimed the strikes targeted military command facilities, airbases and weapons factories, but most damage occurred in civilian areas.

Kyiv bore the brunt of the assault, with Zelenskyy noting that not all ballistic missiles were intercepted. The capital's mayor, Vitali Klitschko, reported two people killed and 56 wounded in the city itself. Two additional deaths occurred in the surrounding Kyiv region, with nine wounded. Damage extended across every district of Kyiv, affecting residential buildings, schools and commercial centers.

The attack damaged several significant structures. Ukraine's National Art Museum, which houses one of the country's largest collections, sustained damage including broken windows and debris scattered throughout. The culture ministry stated the actual collection remained safe. Ukraine's foreign ministry building, struck for the first time since World War II, suffered light damage from nearby explosions. Government headquarters also had windows blown out but no injuries resulted.

A fire broke out at a school hit during the strikes. Another attack on a business center left people trapped in a shelter. In Bila Tserkva in the Kyiv region, Russian missiles destroyed a market and damaged a water supply facility. One market worker told the Associated Press she planned to leave Kyiv after 22 years of employment, saying "everything has burned down."

A 74-year-old Kyiv resident described being thrown back by shock waves when explosions occurred near his apartment. "My apartment was blown to pieces," he said, though he and his dog survived.

Russia's defense ministry claimed the attack was retaliation for Ukrainian strikes on Russian territory. Ukraine's ambassador rejected accusations of war crimes at a UN Security Council emergency meeting called by Russia, describing the session as "pure propaganda."

International leaders condemned the strikes. French President Emmanuel Macron said the Oreshnik missile use signaled "the dead end of Russia's war of aggression." German Chancellor Friedrich Merz called it "reckless escalation." European Union foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas described the attacks as "abhorrent acts of terror meant to kill as many civilians as possible," adding that the Oreshnik use represented "political scare tactic and reckless nuclear brinkmanship."

Culture Minister Tetyana Berezhna stated Russia was "systematically attacking civilian infrastructure and cultural institutions" as an attempt "to intimidate and destroy our identity."

NASA has confirmed that a meteor exploded over the northeastern United States during the weekend, with a blast force equivalent to 230 tons of TNT. The fireball prompted reports of loud booms and sightings from Delaware to Montreal.

The meteor was approximately 3 feet wide as it entered Earth's atmosphere near the New Hampshire and Massachusetts border, north of Boston. Residents across the region reported hearing loud booms or witnessing the bright fireball streaking across the sky before the explosion occurred.

According to NASA's statement, the meteor became visible at 49 miles above Stagecoach, northwest of Houston. It traveled southeast at 35,000 miles per hour before breaking apart 29 miles above Bammel, just west of Cypress Station. The fragmentation of the meteor created a pressure wave that produced the audible booms heard by people in the area.

The American Meteor Society confirmed the event, noting that this type of occurrence is exceptionally rare. Eyewitness accounts came from multiple states and extended into Canada, with sightings reported as far north as Montreal.

Meteors of this size entering Earth's atmosphere are uncommon events. When they do occur, friction generated by passing through the atmosphere at high speeds causes them to heat up and often break apart or explode before reaching the ground. The energy released in such explosions can be substantial, as demonstrated by the equivalent force in this case.

NASA's confirmation provides scientific validation of the numerous eyewitness accounts from across the northeastern region. The agency's ability to calculate the explosive force indicates sophisticated monitoring systems tracked the meteor's entry and destruction.

This recent event comes amid a series of meteor sightings across North America. Just days before the northeastern explosion, a meteor in Ohio created a sonic boom heard as far away as Pennsylvania. That asteroid, measuring about 2 meters in diameter and weighing approximately 6 tons, traveled at 45,000 miles per hour.

Additionally, a suspected meteorite crashed into a home in suburban Houston on Saturday evening. A local resident reported finding a hole in the ceiling and discovering what appeared to be a meteor inside. The fire department received multiple reports of a meteor that had exploded into multiple pieces over the northern Houston area.

While spectacular to witness, such events typically pose little danger to populated areas. The northeastern explosion occurred over water, which likely minimized any potential ground impact or damage. However, the Houston incident demonstrates that meteorites can occasionally strike residential areas, making these events noteworthy for both scientists and the general public who experience them.

Adelman's coaching career spanned from the late 1980s through the 2010s, during which he became known for his offensive systems and player development. He guided the Trail Blazers to multiple playoff appearances and deep postseason runs, establishing himself as one of the franchise's most successful coaches. His teams were characterized by ball movement and teamwork rather than reliance on individual stars.

The Kings period represented some of Adelman's finest work, as he transformed Sacramento into one of the most exciting teams in basketball during the early 2000s. His coaching philosophy emphasized pace, spacing, and unselfish play, which became influential across the league. Adelman finished his career with over 1,000 regular season victories, placing him among the winningest coaches in NBA history.

Adelman was inducted into the Basketball Hall of Fame in recognition of his sustained excellence and contributions to the sport. Beyond his tactical acumen, he was respected for his calm demeanor and ability to manage diverse personalities in the locker room. His coaching tree influenced numerous assistants who went on to lead their own teams.

The basketball community mourned Adelman's passing, with former players and colleagues remembering his impact on their careers and the game itself. His legacy extends beyond wins and losses to the style of basketball he championed and the professionals he mentored throughout his decades in coaching.

Fans and impersonators of Marilyn Monroe gathered in Palm Springs to mark what would have been the icon's 100th birthday on June 1. The celebration brought together Monroe lookalikes and admirers to honor the legacy of one of Hollywood's most enduring stars.

Monroe, born Norma Jeane Mortenson in 1926, became one of the most recognizable figures in American cinema during the 1950s and early 1960s. Her films including "Some Like It Hot," "The Seven Year Itch," and "Gentlemen Prefer Blondes" helped establish her as a cultural phenomenon. She died in 1962 at age 36.

Beyond her on-screen presence, Monroe's personal relationships reveal a more complex figure than her public image suggested. According to those who knew her, Monroe maintained deep friendships with both men and women throughout her life. Amy Greene, widow of photographer Milton Greene, shared her home with Monroe during the mid-1950s and described their bond as genuine friendship rather than merely professional association.

Monroe's film roles often reflected female camaraderie. In "Gentlemen Prefer Blondes," she starred alongside Jane Russell, another prominent sex symbol of the era. Despite being paid significantly less than Russell, the two developed a lasting mutual affection that extended beyond their time on set. Years later, Russell reflected on wishing Monroe could have been present at a gathering with close female friends, only to learn of Monroe's death the following day.

Monroe's relationships extended to her professional collaborators, particularly photographers who documented her image. Lawrence Schiller, who worked with Monroe on a notable 1962 photoshoot for her final unfinished film "Something's Got to Give," remembered her as someone with exceptional understanding of photography and lighting. Schiller noted that Monroe took an active role in controlling her image, even destroying negatives she disliked after a session at Schwab's Pharmacy on Sunset Boulevard.

Another significant friendship was with photographer Sam Shaw, known for capturing one of Monroe's most iconic images: the famous shot of her standing above a subway grate in a billowing white dress. Shaw credited Monroe's "elegance and clean sense of fun" in his posthumously published book "Dear Marilyn."

The centennial observance has sparked renewed interest in Monroe's life beyond her film roles and public persona. The Palm Springs gathering featured fans dressed in Monroe's signature style, recreating her most famous looks and celebrating her contributions to film and popular culture. This event underscores how Monroe's legacy has transcended generations, maintaining relevance in contemporary discussions about fame, image, and Hollywood history. Her influence continues to set standards for stardom decades after her death.

Appeals court blocks Trump transgender military ban as unconstitutional

A federal appeals court ruled on Monday that the Trump administration is unconstitutionally expelling active transgender troops under a policy implemented by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. The divided three-judge panel of the US Court of Appeals for Washington DC delivered a significant legal setback to the administration's efforts to remove transgender service members from th...

A few minutes ago

A federal appeals court ruled on Monday that the Trump administration is unconstitutionally expelling active transgender troops under a policy implemented by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. The divided three-judge panel of the US Court of Appeals for Washington DC delivered a significant legal setback to the administration's efforts to remove transgender service members from the military.

The majority opinion characterized the ban as "arbitrary, and based on animus," according to the court's decision. However, the split ruling also allows the armed services to continue blocking transgender individuals from enlisting in the military, creating a distinction between active duty personnel and new recruits.

The court's decision is expected to face challenges from the government, with legal experts predicting the case will ultimately reach the Supreme Court. The ruling represents a complex outcome that restricts the scope of the administration's transgender military policy while leaving room for future legal battles over enlistment restrictions.

Hegseth responded to the ruling by signaling his intention to appeal to the nation's highest court. In a statement following the decision, he wrote "see you at SCOTUS," indicating his willingness to pursue the case further through the judicial system.

The case has become a focal point in the broader debate over the Trump administration's anti-diversity agenda. Civil liberties advocates have argued that the policy unconstitutionally targets transgender service members, while the administration has maintained that such restrictions are necessary for military readiness and operations.

This ruling comes as part of a larger pattern of legal challenges to Trump administration policies across multiple fronts. The appeals court decision suggests that while the judiciary may restrict some aspects of the transgender military ban, the ultimate resolution of the issue remains uncertain pending potential Supreme Court review.

The divided nature of the appellate panel underscores the contentious nature of the issue, with judges disagreeing about how far the military can go in restricting transgender participation. The majority's focus on calling the ban arbitrary and based on animus indicates concerns about the motivations behind the policy, even as the court allowed restrictions on new enlistment to remain in place.

For active transgender troops currently serving, the ruling provides protection against involuntary discharge based on the Hegseth policy. However, the decision leaves unresolved the question of whether new transgender recruits can be denied entry to the military based on gender identity.

Legal observers note that the path to the Supreme Court appears likely given the administration's stated intent to appeal and the constitutional questions at stake. The high court's eventual ruling could have far-reaching implications for military policy and transgender rights more broadly.
