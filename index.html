A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded during a static fire test at Cape Canaveral's Launch Complex 36 in Florida on Thursday night, destroying the launchpad in a massive fireball visible across the space coast. The explosion occurred at 9pm ET and was powerful enough to shake homes in nearby Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach, with an orange sky visible as far south as Fort Pierce, 115 miles away.

The incident poses a significant threat to NASA's lunar ambitions. Just days before the explosion, NASA had selected Blue Origin to conduct the first of three uncrewed lunar missions planned for this year to begin construction of a $20 billion moon base. The New Glenn rocket is the same vehicle Blue Origin intends to use to launch lunar landers for NASA, including the landers that will carry astronauts to the moon's surface under the Artemis program.

Blue Origin confirmed that all employees were safe and unaccounted for following the explosion. Company founder Jeff Bezos described it as "a very rough day" on social media, stating the company would work to identify the root cause and rebuild as needed. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman called spaceflight "unforgiving" and pledged that NASA would work with Blue Origin to investigate the incident and assess impacts to the Artemis and Moon Base programs.

The explosion represents another setback for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA contracts. The company had recently faced grounding by the Federal Aviation Administration after a payload from a New Glenn flight ended up in the wrong orbit last month. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared the rocket to return to flight the previous week.

The incident carries significant implications for NASA's timeline. The space agency had planned to test both Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander and SpaceX's Starship Human Landing System during the Artemis III mission in 2027 to determine which system will transport the Artemis IV crew from the Orion capsule to the lunar surface in 2028. The explosion threatens these schedules.

News of the disaster reverberated through the financial markets. Stock prices for space companies AST SpaceMobile and Rocket Lab declined following reports of the explosion, reflecting investor concerns about the broader commercial space sector and confidence in space operations.

The launchpad remained aflame more than two hours after the initial explosion. Emergency officials reported no threats from fumes or other potential hazards to surrounding areas. The extent of damage to Launch Complex 36 will likely determine when operations can resume at the facility.

Nine people have been confirmed dead following a catastrophic chemical tank rupture at a paper mill in Longview, Washington. Two additional workers remain missing and presumed dead, bringing the expected total death toll to eleven.

The disaster occurred Tuesday morning at the Nippon Dynawave Packaging Co facility. Recovery crews retrieved the remains of six victims by Friday. Authorities have indicated there is no realistic hope of finding additional survivors among those still unaccounted for, and search operations have shifted from rescue to recovery efforts.

The ruptured tank contained approximately 600,000 gallons of white liquor, a highly corrosive chemical mixture primarily composed of sodium hydroxide and sodium sulfide. This substance is used in the kraft paper production process to break down wood fiber. The chemical spill has resulted in environmental contamination, with dead fish discovered in the Columbia River following the rupture.

Recovery operations proceeded carefully due to safety concerns about the structural integrity of the damaged tank. Recovery crews worked in chemical protective equipment, with all recovered victims undergoing decontamination before being transported to the coroner. Recovery officials described the operation as slow, methodical and deliberate, prioritizing both responder safety and dignified treatment of victims.

The Longview fire department stated that approximately 25,000 gallons of the chemical remained in the tank and continued to leak slowly. The spill entered drainage ditches and eventually reached the river. Testing revealed water contamination, and authorities advised the public to avoid ditches and dikes in the area. However, officials reported no threats to air quality or drinking water supplies in Longview.

The sprawling paper mill employs approximately 1,000 workers and produces materials for tissues, printing paper, cups, plates and cartons. The facility is located along the Columbia River in an area with significant timber and chemical industries.

Washington Governor Bob Ferguson called the incident potentially the deadliest industrial tragedy in modern Washington state history. The US Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board announced it was launching a federal investigation into the tank failure.

Safety complaints were filed against Nippon Dynawave in March and May, though state labor authorities said these were unrelated to the current incident. Records show the company has faced three separate health and safety violations since 2021, resulting in a $3,400 fine.

Nippon Dynawave, a subsidiary of Japan-based Nippon Paper Group, stated the company would cooperate fully with the investigation. A company representative said the firm was focused on assisting victims and supporting first responders.

The names of the deceased and missing workers have not been released. The cause of the tank rupture remains under investigation.

6:58 PM ET

The United States government has proposed new rules for research funding that would allow federal agencies to cancel grants at any time. The proposed changes would also make peer review optional in the grant approval process.

Under the new rules, political staff would screen grant applications for forbidden topics. The changes represent a shift in how federal research funding operates, moving away from the traditional peer review system that has been standard practice for evaluating scientific merit.

The proposal eliminates the requirement for independent expert review before grants are awarded. Instead, political appointees would have authority to evaluate applications based on topic restrictions. The rules would also give agencies discretion to terminate funding agreements without the constraints that currently govern grant relationships.

Federal research grants currently operate under agreements that protect ongoing work from arbitrary cancellation. The proposed rules would remove those protections, allowing agencies to end funding at their discretion regardless of a project's status or the terms under which it was originally awarded.

The peer review process has traditionally served as a buffer between political considerations and scientific evaluation. Experts in relevant fields assess grant applications based on methodology, feasibility, and potential contribution to knowledge. The proposed optional status for this review would allow grants to be approved or denied without that independent assessment.

The topic screening provision directs political staff to identify and potentially reject proposals that address subjects deemed forbidden, though the proposal does not specify which topics would fall into that category. This screening would occur before scientific review, if such review takes place at all.

Researchers rely on federal grants as a primary funding source for work across scientific disciplines. The ability to cancel grants at any time could affect ongoing projects that have already allocated resources and hired personnel based on multi-year funding commitments. The rules would apply to new grants issued after the regulations take effect.

The decision blocks Trump's effort to renovate the facility, which serves as the national cultural center. The board had planned to close the venue to the public while construction took place.

The ruling also prevents the center from winding down its programming, meaning performances and events will continue as scheduled. The judge determined that closing the facility for renovations would exceed the board's legal powers under its governing statute.

Trump has not yet commented on the order. The Kennedy Center board must now decide whether to appeal the decision or comply with the two-week deadline to remove all references to the president's name from the property.

The facility's status as a presidential memorial has been central to the legal dispute. Congress created the center as a living memorial to President Kennedy, and the judge's ruling reinforces that original designation.
