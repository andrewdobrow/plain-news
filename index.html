5:21 PM ET

The Securities and Exchange Commission moved to eliminate a rule requiring publicly traded companies to report greenhouse gas emissions and climate-related risks. The proposal marks a reversal of a Biden-era regulation that aimed to standardize climate disclosures across American corporations.

The rule had required companies to disclose their carbon emissions and detail how climate change could affect their business operations and financial performance. Major corporations and investor groups had debated the regulation since its introduction, with some arguing it provided necessary transparency while others said it imposed excessive compliance burdens.

The SEC's action comes as the Trump administration has moved to roll back various environmental regulations. Wall Street's top regulator framed the proposal as reducing regulatory requirements on American businesses.

If finalized, the repeal would remove mandatory reporting standards that applied to companies listed on U.S. stock exchanges. Firms had been preparing compliance systems to track and report emissions data under the original rule. The proposal will now go through a public comment period before the SEC makes a final decision.

The original climate disclosure rule had been modeled after frameworks used in Europe and had support from institutional investors who argued that climate risks represented material financial information. Environmental groups and some investment managers had pushed for the requirements, saying standardized climate data would help investors make informed decisions.

Industry groups had challenged the rule in court, arguing the SEC exceeded its authority by mandating environmental disclosures. The agency's proposal to scrap the regulation addresses those concerns by removing the requirement entirely rather than attempting to defend it through litigation.

The proposed repeal affects thousands of publicly traded companies that would have been required to begin reporting under the rule. Some large corporations had already begun voluntary climate disclosures using various reporting frameworks, and those practices could continue even without a federal mandate.

7:38 PM ET

Nine people have been confirmed dead following a chemical tank rupture at a paper mill in Longview, Washington, with two workers still unaccounted for. The disaster occurred at the Nippon Dynawave Packaging Co facility on Tuesday morning when an industrial tank ruptured and released white liquor, a highly corrosive chemical mixture used in paper production.

The death toll has been rising as recovery operations continue at the site. According to the Longview fire department, one person died after being transported to the hospital following the initial incident, while seven workers remain hospitalized. The operation has transitioned from rescue to recovery, with authorities stating there is no hope of finding additional survivors.

"We're bracing ourselves for this being the deadliest industrial tragedy in modern Washington state history," said Governor Bob Ferguson at a news conference Wednesday.

Recovery efforts began Wednesday following a delay caused by safety concerns about the structural integrity of the damaged tank. Crews are working in chemical protective equipment, conducting what officials described as a "slow, methodical and deliberate" operation. According to Matt Amos with the Longview fire department, recovered victims will undergo decontamination before being transported to the coroner.

White liquor is a chemical solution made primarily of sodium hydroxide and sodium sulfide and is used with heat to break down wood in the kraft paper production process. The tank held approximately 600,000 gallons of the liquid before the rupture, with about 25,000 gallons remaining and slowly leaking from the site.

Following the tank's rupture, the chemical spilled into a drainage ditch and contaminated the Columbia River. Dead carp were found in the water, and officials urged the public to avoid ditches and dikes while the area undergoes water testing. However, authorities reported no issues with air quality or drinking water in Longview.

A team from the U.S. Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board has been launched to investigate the incident. The Washington state department of ecology and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency are overseeing operations to assess environmental impacts.

Nippon Dynawave, a subsidiary of Japan-based Nippon Paper Group, has faced previous safety complaints. The state's labor and industries department received safety complaints in March and May, though officials said both were unrelated to the current situation. Since 2021, the company has been fined $3,400 for three separate health and safety violations.

Company representative Brian Wood said during a news conference that Nippon Dynawave would "cooperate to the maximum extent" with the investigation. "These are our people," he said, emphasizing the company's focus on victims and assisting first responders.

The sprawling Longview plant employs about 1,000 people and manufactures material for tissues, printing paper, cups, plates and cartons. The facility is located along the Columbia River alongside other timber, paper and chemical businesses.
