6:42 AM ET

The United States and Iran are negotiating toward an agreement to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, though significant obstacles remain and the situation continues to deteriorate. U.S. Vice President JD Vance said the two nations are "very close" to a deal but "not there yet."

The Strait of Hormuz is a critical waterway between the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman. Roughly one-fifth of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas passes through the strait during normal times, making it essential to global energy supplies and international trade.

The tentative agreement would extend the current ceasefire and restart nuclear negotiations that have stalled. However, President Trump has not yet signed off on the deal, and both governments must grant final approval before any accord takes effect.

The negotiations face mounting challenges. Iranian forces seized two commercial ships in the strait this week, marking the first such seizures since fighting began on February 28. The United States responded by seizing an Iranian cargo vessel and boarding an Iranian oil tanker in the Indian Ocean. Iran's parliamentary speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf declared that reopening the strait would be "impossible" while the U.S. and Israel continued what he called "flagrant" ceasefire breaches, including the U.S. naval blockade.

Both countries have now imposed separate blockades on the waterway. The White House said Trump is "satisfied" with the U.S. blockade and believes Iran is in a weak position, losing approximately $500 million daily from economic pressure. Trump extended the ceasefire unilaterally this week, though he had threatened violence against Iran hours before making that announcement.

Planned peace talks in Islamabad failed to materialize. Pakistan had prepared for negotiations led by the U.S. delegation, but Iran never publicly accepted the invitation and the American delegation never departed Washington. A Pakistani official told Reuters the cancellation was "a setback we were not expecting."

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has created a global economic crisis. About 20,000 seafarers and 2,000 ships remain stranded in the Gulf. Germany halved its 2026 growth forecast to 0.5 percent, while Greece announced 500 million euros in emergency aid for households and farmers struggling with fuel shortages and higher costs. The United Nations maritime agency has appealed for assistance for stranded seafarers.

The broader conflict remains unresolved. Israel and the Iranian proxy group Hezbollah continue fighting on a second front in Lebanon, where a fragile 10-day ceasefire is set to expire. Israeli strikes killed five people in Lebanon on Wednesday, including two journalists.

Trump withdrew from Iran's nuclear agreement during his first term and was discouraged from renewed diplomacy by Israel. Years of tension have now escalated into a wider regional conflict that neither nation has successfully contained through military action.

A Russian drone struck an apartment building in Galati, Romania on Friday during a broader overnight attack on neighboring Ukraine, injuring two people. The incident represents the first time a Russian drone has caused civilian casualties in the NATO member state since the war began.

Romanian authorities confirmed the strike and moved swiftly to respond. President Nicusor Dan convened an emergency meeting of the country's supreme council of national defense and the foreign ministry summoned Russia's ambassador. NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte stated the alliance is "ready to defend every inch" of its territory following the incident.

"Russia's reckless behaviour is a danger to us all," Rutte wrote on social media after speaking with the Romanian president. He reaffirmed that NATO stands ready to defend allied territory, underscoring the seriousness with which the security alliance views the attack on one of its 31 member states.

Romania shares a border with Ukraine and has become a key transit point for military aid flowing to Ukraine from Western allies. The country's location near the conflict zone has made it increasingly vulnerable to the spillover effects of the war. Drones and missiles from the fighting have repeatedly strayed into Romanian airspace as Russia continues its military operations in Ukraine.

Previous incidents have involved debris and stray munitions falling in Poland and other neighboring states, but Friday's strike marked the first instance to cause civilian injuries within Romanian territory. The attack occurred near the Ukrainian border, an area where unmanned aircraft from the nearby conflict pose a growing threat to civilian populations.

The European Union condemned the incident, stating that Russia had crossed another line with the strike on civilian infrastructure. Officials characterized it as the most serious security event in Romania since the start of the Ukraine war.

The timing and nature of the drone strike raises complex questions about NATO's response protocols. While Romania is protected under the alliance's collective defense provisions, which could theoretically trigger broader security responses, the incident appears to result from stray weapons rather than deliberate attacks on NATO members. This distinction tests the boundaries of how the alliance responds to the war's spillover effects.

Romanian officials have taken concrete steps to strengthen security along the border with Ukraine. Romania deployed F-16 fighter jets in response to the strike on civilian infrastructure, signaling a more active defensive posture.

Russian authorities have not commented on the strike. As investigations continue, the incident underscores the ongoing risks faced by NATO members bordering Ukraine and highlights the challenges of containing the conflict to Ukrainian territory as fighting intensifies.

3:29 AM ET

A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded on its launch pad at Cape Canaveral, Florida, during a static fire test on Thursday evening at 9 p.m. ET. The massive fireball destroyed the launchpad at Kennedy Space Center, with the blast visible across the state and shockwaves felt along Florida's Atlantic coast.

Jeff Bezos, Blue Origin's founder, confirmed that all company employees were safe and uninjured. He described it as "a very rough day" but expressed commitment to investigating the cause and resuming operations. The company called the incident "an anomaly."

The explosion carries significant implications for NASA's lunar ambitions. The agency had just announced on Tuesday that Blue Origin won a contract to launch the first of three planned missions this year to construct its 20 billion dollar moon base. New Glenn is the heavy-lift rocket Blue Origin intends to use for lunar lander launches that will support the Artemis program, which aims to return astronauts to the moon.

This setback threatens NASA's timeline for lunar operations. Jared Isaacman, the NASA administrator, said the agency would conduct a full evaluation of the impact on its schedule. He noted that developing new heavy-lift launch capability is "extraordinarily difficult" and committed to working with Blue Origin to investigate the incident and assess mission impacts.

The explosion marks another challenge for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA contracts. The company recently experienced a payload mishap when a payload from a New Glenn flight ended up in the wrong orbit last month. The Federal Aviation Administration subsequently grounded the rocket before clearing it to return to flight last week. Thursday's static fire test was the first since that clearance.

The blast was dramatic enough to be captured on livestreams and visible from significant distances. Residents in nearby Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach reported homes shaking, while an orange glow was visible in Fort Pierce, 115 miles away. Emergency officials reported no threat from fumes or other hazards, though flames continued burning at the launchpad hours after the explosion.

Blue Origin is competing with SpaceX in multiple areas of the Artemis program. Both companies have built large facilities near Cape Canaveral to support crewed and cargo missions with NASA. Artemis III, planned for 2027, will test Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander alongside SpaceX's Starship Human Landing System to determine which company's vehicle will carry astronauts from the Orion capsule to the lunar surface in the subsequent Artemis IV mission scheduled for 2028.

Elon Musk commented on the incident, saying "Rockets are hard," while Bezos pledged to rebuild whatever needs rebuilding and return to flying.

A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded on a launch pad at Cape Canaveral in Florida on Thursday night during a static fire test, creating a massive fireball visible across the region and threatening NASA's ambitious lunar timeline.

The explosion occurred at approximately 9pm ET at Launch Complex 36 during a scheduled hotfire test. A massive fireball engulfed and destroyed the launchpad, with an orange sky visible in Fort Pierce, 185 kilometers to the south. Shockwaves from the blast were felt across Florida's space coast, and residents reported seeing a glow in the sky hundreds of miles away in South Carolina.

Blue Origin confirmed that all employees were safe and unaccounted for. The company described the incident as "an anomaly" but has not yet revealed the root cause of the explosion.

The New Glenn rocket was supposed to play a central role in NASA's Artemis Program. The rocket is intended to launch lunar landers to the moon for NASA, including landers that will carry astronauts to the lunar surface. The timing of the explosion is particularly significant, as NASA announced just two days earlier that Blue Origin had won the contract to launch the first of three planned missions this year to begin construction of a 20 billion dollar moon base.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman stated that a full evaluation of the timeline for the Artemis Program would be conducted following the explosion. He acknowledged that developing new heavy-lift launch capability is extraordinarily difficult and committed to working with Blue Origin to support a thorough investigation.

Jeff Bezos, Blue Origin's founder, acknowledged the setback in a social media post, saying "very rough day, but we'll rebuild whatever needs rebuilding and get back to flying." He emphasized that the company is already working to identify the root cause.

This represents the latest in a series of setbacks for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA contracts. Last month, a payload from the third flight of New Glenn ended up in the wrong orbit, leading to the rocket being temporarily grounded by the Federal Aviation Administration. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared the rocket to return to flight.

Blue Origin and SpaceX are both competing to support NASA's lunar missions. Under current plans, Artemis III in 2027 will test Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander and SpaceX's Starship Human Landing System to determine which vehicle will ferry astronauts from the Orion capsule to the lunar surface during the Artemis IV mission in 2028.

The explosion raises significant questions about whether NASA's goal of returning humans to the moon within the next two years can be achieved on schedule.

1:02 AM ET

Claude Lemieux, a four-time Stanley Cup champion known as one of hockey's most competitive playoff performers, has died at 60. Police confirmed that Lemieux died by suicide.

Lemieux won Stanley Cup championships with three different teams during his NHL career. He captured titles with the Montreal Canadiens, New Jersey Devils twice, and Colorado Avalanche. His playoff intensity and scoring ability made him one of the most effective postseason players of his generation.

The right winger played 21 seasons in the NHL, appearing in 1,215 regular season games. He scored 379 goals and added 406 assists during his career. Lemieux elevated his performance in the playoffs, recording 80 goals in 234 postseason games.

Lemieux became a central figure in one of hockey's most notable rivalries. His hit on Kris Draper during the 1996 Western Conference Finals sparked a years-long feud between the Colorado Avalanche and Detroit Red Wings. The collision sent Draper to the hospital with severe facial injuries and led to multiple fights and confrontations between the teams over subsequent seasons.

He won the Conn Smythe Trophy as playoff MVP in 1995 when he led the New Jersey Devils to their first Stanley Cup championship. That postseason, Lemieux scored 13 goals in 20 games. His ability to raise his performance level during the most important games earned him respect throughout the league despite his physical playing style.

Lemieux played for six NHL teams during his career, including the Canadiens, Devils, Avalanche, Phoenix Coyotes, Dallas Stars, and San Jose Sharks. He retired after the 2008-09 season. After his playing career ended, he remained involved in hockey in various capacities.

The NHL and multiple former teammates have expressed condolences following news of his death.

At least six of the nine musical acts announced for a Trump-backed concert series celebrating America's 250th anniversary have withdrawn, just one day after the lineup was revealed. The rapid exodus of performers has severely undermined the event scheduled for the National Mall in Washington, D.C.

Morris Day was the first to pull out, calling his participation a baseless "rumor" hours after Wednesday's announcement. Young MC followed, stating that he had informed his agents he would not perform and that artists were never told about the political involvement with the event.

By Thursday, the withdrawals accelerated. The Commodores issued a statement saying they would not perform, explaining that their music has always been their voice and they choose not to publicly affiliate with any single political party. C+C Music Factory's lead rapper Freedom Williams recorded a video statement explaining he was blindsided by texts from friends horrified about his involvement with what they called the Trump Freedom show. He said his agent never mentioned any Trump connection when pitching the opportunity.

"I know who the fuck I am," Williams said in the video, adding that he did not support Trump and knew the type of "anarchy he creates" as a New Yorker. He expressed particular anger at Democrats who threatened to cancel him if he did not withdraw.

Country singer Martina McBride also dropped out, saying she was presented with what she believed was a nonpartisan event but was misled. She stated she had asked numerous questions and was assured the event would celebrate all 50 states.

Milli Vanilli's current iteration also distanced itself from the event. Jodie Rocco told the Associated Press that no one had asked her, her sister Linda, or anyone else in the current group to perform. She expressed shock at seeing Milli Vanilli listed as a performer, noting that the poster included an image of former frontman Fab Morvan, who performs separately.

Vanilla Ice remained one of the few committed performers, posting an Instagram video saying he was honored to participate. The rapper has performed multiple times at Trump's Mar-a-Lago club and appeared in footage last December with federal officials Stephen Miller and Kristi Noem as they sang along to his 1990 hit.

Freedom 250, the organization Trump launched to manage the nation's 250th anniversary celebration, stated that it is a nonpartisan 501(c)(3) dedicated to uniting Americans. A spokesperson said the group focuses on celebrations that honor America's history and engage all Americans.

With more than half the announced performers now withdrawn, serious questions remain about whether the concert series can proceed as planned.

6:00 AM ET

The Trump administration has opened a criminal investigation into E. Jean Carroll, the writer who accused the president of sexual assault. Federal prosecutors are examining whether Carroll, 82, committed perjury during a 2022 deposition in her civil lawsuits against Trump, according to reports from the New York Times and CNN.

The investigation centers on Carroll's statements during the deposition that she did not accept outside financial support for her legal battles. Nearly six months later, before the trial began, Carroll's attorneys informed the judge and Trump's legal team that a nonprofit funded by Reid Hoffman, the billionaire LinkedIn co-founder, had paid some of her legal fees and expenses. Trump's lawyers argued that Carroll concealed Hoffman's funding and that this deception damaged her credibility. Carroll's legal representatives stated she never met or spoke with anyone from the nonprofit. The judge permitted Trump's attorney, Alina Habba, to question Carroll again in a second deposition.

In 2024, a federal appeals court panel in New York dismissed the claim that Carroll had lied in her deposition.

The investigation creates significant political difficulty for Senate Republicans, who will likely need to address it on the Senate floor next month. These senators must navigate the complicated optics of the Justice Department pursuing criminal charges against someone who won a civil case against the president.

Carroll, a longtime advice columnist, claimed in her 2019 book that Trump forced himself upon her in a New York department store's dressing room. Trump denied the allegations, saying she was "totally lying" and "not my type." Carroll filed a federal civil lawsuit against Trump for sexual abuse and defamation. One jury found Trump liable on both counts and awarded Carroll 5 million dollars. A year later, a separate jury ordered Trump to pay 83 million dollars in another defamation case. Trump has appealed both judgments.

Todd Blanche, the acting attorney general, has recused himself from the investigation due to his role representing Trump in Carroll's civil case.

The investigation represents the latest instance of Trump's Justice Department launching criminal probes into political opponents. Federal prosecutors have investigated former FBI chief James Comey, New York attorney general Letitia James, and Democratic members of Congress including Adam Schiff and Ilhan Omar. None of these investigations have resulted in convictions so far.

Republican senators face the task of balancing support for the Trump administration with concerns about the appearance of retaliatory prosecution. Democrats are expected to use floor time to criticize the probe as political retaliation, while Republicans must articulate their position on the appropriateness of the Justice Department's actions.
