12:42 PM ET

Oil prices rose after the United States and Iran conducted a fresh exchange of military strikes, adding volatility to global energy markets as investors assessed both the immediate military risk and ongoing diplomatic efforts.

The U.S. military struck an Iranian military facility, targeting a ground control station in Bandar Abbas that was preparing to launch attack drones. American forces shot down four Iranian drones near the Strait of Hormuz. A U.S. official characterized the action as "measured, purely defensive, and intended to maintain the ceasefire."

Within two hours, Iran's Revolutionary Guards launched retaliatory strikes against an American military base in Kuwait. Kuwait's army confirmed it intercepted hostile missile and drone threats early Thursday morning. The Iranian state broadcaster reported the strikes targeted the base that had served as the source of the U.S. attack.

The tit-for-tat strikes coincided with Iranian forces firing at four ships attempting to cross the Strait of Hormuz. An Iranian official stated the vessels had crossed without coordination with security forces.

The military escalation underscores how fragile negotiations between Washington and Tehran remain. While both sides have maintained diplomatic channels, President Trump delivered harsh rhetoric aimed at forcing a deal. "Either that or we'll have to just finish the job," Trump said at a White House cabinet meeting, claiming Iran was "negotiating on fumes." He insisted the midterm elections would not pressure him into accepting unfavorable terms.

Trump also appeared to direct a warning at Oman, a U.S. ally, when discussing proposals for Iran and Oman to jointly manage the Strait of Hormuz. Trump said the strait would be "open to everybody" and "Oman will behave just like everybody else or we'll have to blow them up." The White House did not immediately clarify his remarks.

Iran's state television claimed to possess an unofficial draft agreement that would restore commercial shipping through the strait within a month, with Iran and Oman managing traffic. The White House dismissed this as a "complete fabrication."

A significant unresolved issue concerns whether any U.S.-Iran ceasefire will cover Israeli operations against Hezbollah in Lebanon. Iran has insisted Lebanon must be included in ceasefire agreements. Netanyahu said his military is "deepening its operation" in Lebanon, with Israeli forces clashing with Hezbollah fighters and striking over 550 targets this week alone.

The price spike reflects market concerns about potential disruptions to oil supplies from the Middle East. Iran controls the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly one-fifth of the world's oil passes. For oil-dependent economies worldwide, prices will likely remain elevated until military exchanges cease or diplomatic efforts successfully de-escalate tensions.

Anthropic, the artificial intelligence company behind the Claude chatbot, has filed confidentially for an initial public offering on the US stock market, the company announced Monday. The confidential filing marks a significant milestone in the AI startup's path toward becoming a publicly traded company.

The company did not disclose its target valuation for the stock market or other specific terms of the offering. However, Anthropic recently announced a major funding round that valued the company at $965 billion post-money, following a $65 billion investment. This valuation represents a substantial increase from February, when the company was valued at $380 billion.

The confidential filing process allows Anthropic to begin its IPO journey while keeping financial details private until closer to the actual offering. Under securities regulations, companies can file confidentially and later make their prospectus public, typically a few weeks before starting their roadshow to market shares to investors. This approach has become increasingly common among technology companies.

The Claude AI assistant has become popular with software engineers and other business clients, competing directly with ChatGPT and other large language models in both consumer and enterprise markets.

The company has developed what it calls a constitutional AI approach, which aims to build AI systems with built-in ethical guidelines and safety constraints. Anthropic has raised billions of dollars in private funding from major investors including Google and Salesforce.

The timing of the filing comes as the financial stakes of the AI industry continue to rise. Anthropic's IPO would make it the world's most valuable AI startup, eclipsing its competitor OpenAI, which is expected to file for a public offering in the coming weeks. The AI race has intensified with multiple major players preparing to go public this year.

Elon Musk's SpaceX has also filed for a stock market float at a valuation of about $1.75 trillion while seeking $75 billion in investment. The convergence of these major offerings reflects the significant investor interest in artificial intelligence and space technology sectors.

The confidential filing positions Anthropic to join the public markets at a time when investor interest in artificial intelligence companies remains elevated. Many AI companies have seen their valuations rise substantially over the past two years as businesses across industries adopt AI tools. A successful IPO would give public market investors direct access to one of the leading independent AI companies, separate from the AI divisions embedded within larger technology corporations.

Raymond Berry, a Pro Football Hall of Fame receiver who starred for the Baltimore Colts and later coached the New England Patriots to a Super Bowl victory, has died at age 93.

Berry played 13 seasons in the NFL, all with the Colts, leading the league in receptions three times during his career. He was known for his precise route running and sure hands, becoming one of quarterback Johnny Unitas's favorite targets during Baltimore's championship era. Berry's performance in the 1958 NFL Championship Game, often called the greatest game ever played, helped establish the NFL as a major American sport. He caught 12 passes for 178 yards in that overtime victory against the New York Giants.

After retiring as a player, Berry transitioned to coaching. He served as head coach of the New England Patriots from 1984 to 1989, leading the team to Super Bowl XX following the 1985 season. Though the Patriots lost that game to the Chicago Bears, Berry's coaching tenure marked an important period in the franchise's development. He finished his coaching career with a 51-41 regular season record.

Berry was inducted into the Pro Football Hall of Fame in 1973, recognition of his impact on the game during an era when passing attacks were becoming central to NFL offenses. He finished his playing career with 631 receptions for 9,275 yards and 68 touchdowns. His partnership with Unitas formed one of the most successful quarterback-receiver combinations in NFL history.

Berry's methodical approach to the game, including his attention to detail in preparation and execution, influenced generations of receivers who followed. He was known for running precise routes and studying defensive tendencies, traits that became standard for elite receivers. His contributions to professional football extended across multiple decades as both a player and coach.

Hall of Fame receiver Raymond Berry dies at 93

2:00 PM ET

Raymond Berry, a Pro Football Hall of Fame receiver who starred for the Baltimore Colts and later coached the New England Patriots to a Super Bowl victory, has died at age 93.

Berry played 13 seasons in the NFL, all with the Colts, leading the league in receptions three times during his career. He was known for his precise route running and sure hands, becoming one of quarterback Johnny Unitas's favorite targets during Baltimore's championship era. Berry's performance in the 1958 NFL Championship Game, often called the greatest game ever played, helped establish the NFL as a major American sport. He caught 12 passes for 178 yards in that overtime victory against the New York Giants.

After retiring as a player, Berry transitioned to coaching. He served as head coach of the New England Patriots from 1984 to 1989, leading the team to Super Bowl XX following the 1985 season. Though the Patriots lost that game to the Chicago Bears, Berry's coaching tenure marked an important period in the franchise's development. He finished his coaching career with a 51-41 regular season record.

Berry was inducted into the Pro Football Hall of Fame in 1973, recognition of his impact on the game during an era when passing attacks were becoming central to NFL offenses. He finished his playing career with 631 receptions for 9,275 yards and 68 touchdowns. His partnership with Unitas formed one of the most successful quarterback-receiver combinations in NFL history.

Berry's methodical approach to the game, including his attention to detail in preparation and execution, influenced generations of receivers who followed. He was known for running precise routes and studying defensive tendencies, traits that became standard for elite receivers. His contributions to professional football extended across multiple decades as both a player and coach.
