Israel launched a new wave of strikes across Tehran shortly after Donald Trump announced he was pausing US attacks on Iranian energy infrastructure, signaling what he described as "productive" talks between Washington and Tehran.

The Israeli Defense Forces said it had "just begun another wave of strikes targeting infrastructure of the Iranian terror regime across Tehran," approximately 40 minutes after Trump extended his deadline to strike Iran's power plants by five days. According to al Jazeera Arabic's correspondent in Tehran, the scale of explosions in the Iranian capital was "unprecedented."

Trump said on social media that the US and Iran "have had, over the last two days, very good and productive conversations." He instructed the Department of War to postpone strikes against Iranian power plants and energy infrastructure for five days, pending the success of ongoing negotiations.

An Israeli Defense Forces official told the Times of Israel that the military could not comment on US negotiations with Iran, describing it as a "political echelon matter." The IDF said it was operating according to Israeli government directives and would continue striking until instructed otherwise. However, the IDF also indicated that energy infrastructure would be spared, suggesting Israel may align with Washington in suspending attacks on Iranian power plants.

According to Israeli military statements released Monday, strikes targeted Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps air defense and ground forces headquarters, a Quds Force base and intelligence site, a defense ministry missile production facility, and other research and manufacturing centers. The Guardian could not independently verify these claims.

The Israeli military claimed it had destroyed or disabled approximately 330 of Iran's estimated 470 ballistic missile launchers since the war began. More than half were destroyed in airstrikes, while others were disabled after entrances to underground storage sites were targeted. Missile fire on Israel has declined to about 10 per day recently, down from roughly 90 at the start of the conflict.

Israel has also continued military operations in Lebanon, striking Beirut's southern suburbs on Monday in what the military said was the first attack on the Hezbollah stronghold in several days. Lebanon's health ministry reported one person killed in the strikes.

Israel's far-right finance minister Bezalel Smotrich called Monday for the annexation of areas in southern Lebanon, saying Israel should "apply sovereignty" over areas under its control. He stated the new Israeli border should be the Litani River.

A few minutes ago

Oil prices rose and stock markets fell after the United States said it shot down four Iranian attack drones. The strikes occurred despite an ongoing ceasefire and peace talks between Washington and Tehran.

The military action cast doubt on the prospects for a peace agreement between the two countries. Oil markets reacted immediately to the news of the confrontation, with investors responding to the heightened risk in the region by moving into oil and away from equities.

The incident marks a significant setback after recent progress in negotiations. Just days earlier, oil prices had tumbled below the $100-a-barrel mark on hopes that the U.S. and Iran were moving closer to a deal. That optimism was quickly reversed by the drone strikes.

The U.S. president had announced he was holding off on threatened attacks on Iran, subject to Tehran agreeing to a two-week ceasefire and reopening the Strait of Hormuz for shipping. Iran's national security council had confirmed it accepted a two-week ceasefire if attacks against Iran were halted. Peace negotiations were set to begin in Islamabad on Friday.

The ceasefire had been welcomed by financial markets. The pan-European Stoxx 600 index had jumped 3.7% in its biggest single-day rise in a year. Wall Street had rallied strongly, with the S&P 500 gaining 2.5% and the Dow Jones industrial average climbing more than 1,300 points. Asian markets had also surged, with Japan's Nikkei 225 gaining more than 5% and South Korea's Kospi soaring by 7.5%.

Oil prices had fallen sharply on ceasefire hopes, with Brent crude dropping 16% and U.S. crude futures sinking 17.6%. Brent crude had reached $94.36 a barrel, heading for its biggest one-day fall since the Covid-19 lockdowns. However, the drone strikes triggered a reversal of those gains.

The incident comes at a sensitive moment in U.S.-Iran relations, with analysts warning that the fragile ceasefire remains vulnerable. Kathleen Brooks, a research director at XTB, noted that markets were watching closely. "Only if the US or Iran walk away from the ceasefire completely and bombing restarts do we see the oil price potentially surging back to the highs of this week above $110 per barrel for Brent crude, which could also cause stocks and bonds to slump," she said.

Jim Reid, a markets strategist at Deutsche Bank, cautioned that investors faced uncertainty ahead. "Will the ceasefire hold? We saw some strikes by Israel and Iran overnight, though these may have been in the works before the conditional ceasefire. We've also seen conflicting commentary on whether the ceasefire will extend to Israel's action in Lebanon."

Investors are now watching to see whether the drone strikes will derail the peace process or prove to be an isolated incident.

Yesterday, 10:47 PM ET

The Australian government filed a lawsuit against US manufacturing company 3M seeking A$2 billion in damages over contamination caused by firefighting foam containing chemicals known as PFAS.

The case centers on contamination at Australian defense sites where the foam was used. The lawsuit represents the largest legal action ever brought by the Australian government.

PFAS, or per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, are synthetic chemicals commonly called forever chemicals because they do not break down naturally in the environment. They have been used in firefighting foams for decades due to their ability to suppress fuel-based fires.

The chemicals can persist in soil and water for extremely long periods and have been detected at sites where firefighting foam was regularly used or stored. Defense installations have been among the most affected locations due to routine firefighting training exercises.

The A$2 billion figure represents the scale of alleged damages the government is seeking to recover. 3M is a major US-based manufacturer that produces thousands of products across multiple industries.

Australian authorities have been investigating PFAS contamination at defense sites for several years. The chemicals have raised health concerns in communities near affected installations.

Firefighting foam containing PFAS was widely adopted by fire services and military organizations globally because of its effectiveness on petroleum fires. However, environmental and health concerns about the chemicals have led many jurisdictions to seek alternatives.

The lawsuit marks a significant legal confrontation between the Australian government and one of America's largest manufacturing corporations. Legal proceedings in cases of this size typically extend over multiple years before resolution.

The triumph was particularly poignant as it marked Oliver Glasner's final match in charge. The Austrian manager has now won three trophies in just 12 months, following last season's FA Cup and Community Shield successes. Glasner celebrated enthusiastically on the pitch, performing a full-length dive before collecting his winners' medal.

Palace were the stronger side throughout the second half after a cagey first 45 minutes where little quality football was on display. Rayo dominated possession early on and came close to scoring when Alemão failed to hit the target from Palazón's cross. Mitchell also had a header saved for Palace just before half-time.

The transformation came after the interval. Mateta struck in the 58th minute with an instinctive finish after Wharton's shot was parried by goalkeeper Augusto Batalla. Moments later, Pino's free-kick struck both posts before Mateta again hit the woodwork with a follow-up attempt, with Batalla then denying the striker once more.

Rayo mounted sustained pressure as their manager Iñigo Pérez made several substitutions, but Palace's defence, led by Maxence Lacroix, held firm to secure the victory. This was Rayo's first final in their 102-year history, making the defeat particularly difficult for their supporters, though they unveiled a second tifo after full-time that read: "I know no greater victory than to be with you in defeat."

Steve Parish, Palace's chairman who rescued the club from administration in 2010, watched the victory from beside Uefa president Aleksander Ceferin. The occasion represented the biggest night in the club's history.

Glasner praised his team's resilience and character throughout the campaign. Despite his impending departure, the manager expressed no regrets about his decision to leave after delivering European silverware to south London. Adam Wharton played a starring role in midfield despite being omitted from England's squad, while Glasner notably selected Chadi Riad in defence ahead of injured Chris Richards.

The victory represents remarkable progress for a club that finished 15th in the Premier League, earning them European football next season. Palace's supporters celebrated wildly both in Leipzig and at Selhurst Park, where thousands watched on a big screen and invaded the pitch as the final whistle sounded.

Yesterday, 10:32 PM ET

The Trump administration has launched a criminal investigation into E. Jean Carroll, the writer who accused President Trump of sexual assault, according to reports from CNN and the New York Times on Wednesday.

Federal prosecutors are examining whether Carroll, 82, committed perjury during a 2022 deposition as part of her civil lawsuits against Trump. The investigation centers on statements Carroll made under oath in which she said she did not receive outside financial support for her legal battles.

Nearly six months after that deposition, Carroll's attorneys informed the judge and Trump's legal team that a nonprofit funded by Reid Hoffman, the billionaire LinkedIn co-founder, had covered some of her legal fees and expenses. Trump's lawyers argued that Carroll had deliberately concealed Hoffman's financial backing, claiming this deception undermined her credibility. Carroll's legal team stated she had never met or spoken with anyone from the nonprofit.

The judge permitted Trump's attorney, Alina Habba, to question Carroll again in a second deposition to address the funding disclosure. However, in 2024, a three-judge federal appeals court panel in New York dismissed the claim that Carroll had lied in her initial deposition.

Carroll, a longtime advice columnist, first made her allegations public in her 2019 book "What Do We Need Men For?" She claimed Trump forced himself upon her in a dressing room at a New York department store in the 1990s. Trump has denied the allegations, stating Carroll was "totally lying" and claiming she was "not my type."

Carroll filed a federal civil lawsuit against Trump for sexual abuse and defamation. A jury found Trump liable on both counts and awarded Carroll 5 million dollars in damages. A separate jury subsequently ordered Trump to pay 83 million dollars in another defamation case. Trump has appealed both judgments.

Todd Blanche, the acting attorney general, has recused himself from the DOJ investigation due to his previous role representing Trump in Carroll's civil cases, according to reports from CNN and the New York Times.

The investigation represents the latest instance of Trump's Justice Department launching criminal probes into political opponents. Federal prosecutors have opened investigations into former FBI director James Comey, New York Attorney General Letitia James, and Democratic members of Congress including Adam Schiff and Ilhan Omar. None of these investigations have resulted in convictions so far.

The Justice Department and Carroll's legal representatives did not immediately respond to requests for comment regarding the investigation.
