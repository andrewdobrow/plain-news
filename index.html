3:50 PM ET

NASA has officially ended the MAVEN mission after the spacecraft lost contact with Earth following an orbital anomaly. The orbiter, which has studied Mars for more than a decade, stopped communicating with ground stations over a weekend and has not resumed contact despite extensive recovery efforts.

MAVEN, which stands for Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution, launched in 2013 and reached Mars orbit in 2014. According to NASA, all subsystems were working normally before the spacecraft passed behind Mars. When it reappeared on the other side of the planet, the spacecraft had stopped transmitting data back to Earth.

"Telemetry showed all subsystems working normally before it orbited behind Mars," NASA said in a statement. "The spacecraft and operations teams are investigating the anomaly to address the situation."

Throughout its operational life, MAVEN studied the upper Martian atmosphere and its interaction with the solar wind. The research helped scientists understand a fundamental question about Mars: why the planet lost most of its atmosphere over billions of years. Scientists determined that the sun itself was responsible, as solar wind gradually stripped away much of the planet's air. This atmospheric loss transformed Mars from a potentially wet and warm world capable of supporting life into the cold and dry planet it is today.

Beyond its primary research mission, MAVEN served an important secondary role as a communications relay for NASA's two Mars rovers, Curiosity and Perseverance. The spacecraft transmitted data from these surface rovers back to Earth, supporting their ongoing exploration and scientific discoveries.

The loss of MAVEN leaves NASA with fewer active orbiters around Mars. However, the space agency still maintains two other spacecraft in Mars orbit: the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, launched in 2005, and Mars Odyssey, launched in 2001. Both continue to operate and study the planet.

Mission operators expressed the emotional impact of losing the long-lived spacecraft. The loss was compared to losing a loved one, reflecting the deep connections that develop between teams and the spacecraft they operate over many years of continuous work.

Despite the end of active operations, MAVEN's contributions to science will continue to benefit researchers. The extensive data collected by the orbiter throughout its years of operation will inform scientific understanding of Mars and planetary atmospheres for years to come. NASA has not announced immediate plans for a replacement mission with similar atmospheric research objectives focused on studying the Martian upper atmosphere and its interaction with solar wind.

The conflict erupted after CBS News fired the show's executive producer, executive editor, and two correspondents, Cecilia Vega and Sharyn Alfonsi, without explaining the reasons for their terminations. During a Monday morning staff meeting with newly appointed executive editor Nick Bilton and other executives, Pelley strongly objected to the changes and directly criticized Weiss's leadership. According to sources, Pelley said that Weiss was "murdering 60 Minutes" and had been "brought in to kill it."

Following the meeting, Weiss and other executives met with Pelley on Tuesday to address his conduct. That evening, Bilton sent Pelley a termination message stating he had been "terminated for cause effective immediately." In the message, Bilton cited Pelley's behavior during the staff meeting, saying Pelley had "hijacked" the meeting "to disparage me, my qualifications, and my intentions with remarkable incivility and contempt."

Pelley subsequently issued a public statement accusing CBS News executives of silencing employees and instructing him to "inject falsehoods and bias" into his reporting. He emphasized that 60 Minutes had maintained its position as the top program in its category because audiences value "integrity, quality, and humanity" in its stories.

The termination has left 60 Minutes with significant staffing challenges. The program now has only three full-time correspondents heading into its 59th season: Lesley Stahl, Bill Whitaker, and L Jon Wertheim. This follows Anderson Cooper's earlier announcement that he would be leaving the show.

The firings have prompted concern among staff members about editorial independence and the future direction of the news division. Former 60 Minutes correspondent Steve Kroft characterized the changes as "disastrous" and called them "journalistic interference," noting that the program remains the highest rated show in its time slot, which he said makes the management decisions senseless from a business perspective.

Pelley's colleagues have acknowledged his contributions to the network. He joined CBS News in 1989 and served as chief White House correspondent and anchor of the CBS Evening News from 2011 to 2017 before joining 60 Minutes as a correspondent in 1999. His departure reflects broader tensions within media organizations as they balance traditional journalism practices with pressure from various stakeholders.

The conflict erupted after CBS News fired the show's executive producer, executive editor, and two correspondents, Cecilia Vega and Sharyn Alfonsi, without explaining the reasons for their terminations. During a Monday morning staff meeting with newly appointed executive editor Nick Bilton and other executives, Pelley strongly objected to the changes and directly criticized Weiss's leadership. According to sources, Pelley said that Weiss was "murdering 60 Minutes" and had been "brought in to kill it."

Following the meeting, Weiss and other executives met with Pelley on Tuesday to address his conduct. That evening, Bilton sent Pelley a termination message stating he had been "terminated for cause effective immediately." In the message, Bilton cited Pelley's behavior during the staff meeting, saying Pelley had "hijacked" the meeting "to disparage me, my qualifications, and my intentions with remarkable incivility and contempt."

Pelley subsequently issued a public statement accusing CBS News executives of silencing employees and instructing him to "inject falsehoods and bias" into his reporting. He emphasized that 60 Minutes had maintained its position as the top program in its category because audiences value "integrity, quality, and humanity" in its stories.

The termination has left 60 Minutes with significant staffing challenges. The program now has only three full-time correspondents heading into its 59th season: Lesley Stahl, Bill Whitaker, and L Jon Wertheim. This follows Anderson Cooper's earlier announcement that he would be leaving the show.

The firings have prompted concern among staff members about editorial independence and the future direction of the news division. Former 60 Minutes correspondent Steve Kroft characterized the changes as "disastrous" and called them "journalistic interference," noting that the program remains the highest rated show in its time slot, which he said makes the management decisions senseless from a business perspective.

Pelley's colleagues have acknowledged his contributions to the network. He joined CBS News in 1989 and served as chief White House correspondent and anchor of the CBS Evening News from 2011 to 2017 before joining 60 Minutes as a correspondent in 1999. His departure reflects broader tensions within media organizations as they balance traditional journalism practices with pressure from various stakeholders.

War powers resolutions require congressional authorization for sustained military action, but presidents have historically resisted such constraints on their role as commander in chief.

The vote reflects growing unease among some Republicans about the scope and duration of military engagement with Iran. The four Republicans who crossed party lines to support the resolution represent a shift in GOP sentiment on the conflict, though the vast majority of the party remains aligned with the administration's approach. Democrats have repeatedly sought to limit Trump's authority to conduct military operations against Iran without explicit congressional approval.

The measure comes after months of escalating tensions and military action between the United States and Iran. Congressional Democrats have argued that the president exceeded his constitutional authority by ordering sustained military operations without a formal declaration of war or explicit authorization from Congress. The administration has maintained that existing authorizations and the president's constitutional powers justify the military response.

The House vote sets up a potential showdown over war powers and executive authority, even as the practical impact of the resolution remains limited. Without Senate passage and the ability to override a presidential veto, the measure serves primarily as a statement of congressional opposition to the current military posture toward Iran.
