The United States conducted new strikes on military targets in southern Iran on Wednesday, marking the second attack in three days while a ceasefire between the two countries remains officially in place. Both nations are simultaneously engaged in peace negotiations aimed at ending the three-month conflict.

A US official confirmed the strikes targeted attack drones and a drone ground control station, describing the military action as a self-defense measure. The attacks occurred near the Strait of Hormuz, a waterway critical to global energy supplies that handles roughly one-fifth of the world's oil transport.

Oil prices rose immediately following news of the strikes. The Trump administration has denied reaching any agreement with Iran regarding passage through the strategic waterway.

The fresh military action threatens the fragile ceasefire that both countries have maintained during diplomatic talks. The strikes represent a pattern of continued US military operations that risk undermining peace efforts, even as negotiators from both sides work toward a comprehensive agreement.

The military site targeted in the strikes underscores their strategic importance. The narrow Strait of Hormuz serves as a chokepoint for global energy supplies, making the region's security a matter of international concern beyond US-Iran relations alone.

Iran had imposed a near-total internet shutdown lasting 88 days during the conflict. The government recently began allowing citizens to reconnect with the outside world, though access remains limited and uncertain. Many Iranians remain unsure how long the restored connectivity will persist.

The pattern of military strikes followed by continued diplomatic negotiations illustrates the unstable nature of the current ceasefire. While military commanders conduct operations they view as necessary for national security, diplomatic teams continue working toward a permanent resolution.

Neither the US government nor Iran has publicly stated how the latest strikes will affect ongoing peace talks. The renewed military action raises significant questions about whether the ceasefire can remain stable long enough for negotiators to reach an agreement that would end the war.

The strikes come after a three-month period of conflict between the two countries. Previous US military operations occurred just days earlier, making Wednesday's attack the latest in a series of operations that complicate diplomatic efforts. Both governments have characterized the ceasefire as fragile, acknowledging the difficulty of maintaining peace while military operations continue.

The timing and location of these strikes demonstrate how security concerns continue to drive military decisions even as political leaders pursue negotiations. The international community, particularly nations dependent on Middle Eastern oil supplies, watches closely as the situation develops.

Oil prices climbed sharply after the United States launched military strikes against Iran, marking the second attack in three days despite an ongoing ceasefire and peace negotiations between the two countries.

Brent crude rose 1.95% to $71.72 per barrel, while West Texas Intermediate increased 2.12% to $66.67 per barrel by Thursday afternoon, continuing gains from overnight trading when both benchmarks jumped 4%. The rally reflects investor concerns about potential disruptions to global energy supplies.

The timing creates significant uncertainty for markets and policymakers. Oil had fallen below $100 a barrel earlier in the week on optimism that a peace deal might be reached to end the three-month conflict. That sentiment reversed once the strikes were announced, demonstrating how quickly investor expectations can shift in response to geopolitical developments.

The strikes occurred despite a six-week-old ceasefire and ongoing diplomatic efforts between Washington and Tehran. According to market analysts, the situation reveals a dangerous complacency among some investors who may underestimate escalation risks.

"Oil markets are starting to price in higher risk as Iran remains a major producer, and more importantly, sits at the heart of the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 20% of global oil supply transits," explained Daniela Hathorn, a senior market analyst at capital.com. Even limited disruptions to shipping lanes could trigger immediate supply shocks that ripple through global economies.

Market participants are assigning roughly a 70% probability to a potential US strike on Iran. Beyond energy markets, analysts warn that broader economic consequences could follow, including shipping delays, global supply chain disruption, and increased inflation that might influence central bank interest rate decisions.

Stock markets declined in response to the developments. In the US, major indexes opened lower, with the Dow down 0.25%, the S&P 500 down 0.35%, and the Nasdaq down 0.49%. European indexes extended losses, with the FTSE 100 down 0.6%, Germany's DAX down 0.8%, France's CAC 40 down 0.79%, and Spain's IBEX down 0.85%.

Safe haven assets also moved higher. Gold prices increased 0.23% to $4,991.58 per ounce, as investors sought protection against potential escalation.

The situation has prompted warnings from analysts about the risks of investor complacency. "This muted response suggests that investors are either sceptical of imminent escalation or confident that any conflict would be short-lived," Hathorn noted. "This complacency, however, raises the risk of a sharp repricing event. If tensions move from rhetoric to action, oil could spike rapidly, bond yields could rise on inflation fears, and equities could experience a volatility shock."

All eyes remain on the White House for any signals about whether diplomatic efforts will continue or if escalation appears likely.

Federal authorities have charged a Google engineer with insider trading after the individual made $1.2 million through bets placed on the prediction market platform Polymarket.

According to the criminal complaint, the engineer wagered over $2.7 million on bets related to Google's 2025 Year in Search campaign. The charges suggest the engineer used confidential information obtained through their position at the technology company to place the wagers.

Polymarket is a prediction market platform where users can bet on the outcomes of future events, ranging from political elections to corporate announcements. The platform has grown in popularity as an alternative way to forecast events through market-based predictions.

The case marks a notable intersection between traditional insider trading enforcement and newer prediction market platforms. While insider trading prosecutions typically involve traditional stock markets, this case demonstrates that authorities are applying the same legal framework to trading on prediction markets when nonpublic information is involved.

The complaint indicates the engineer risked a substantial sum on wagers tied specifically to outcomes related to Google's annual Year in Search campaign, which highlights trending searches and cultural moments from the year. The timing and specificity of the bets apparently drew the attention of investigators.

The $1.2 million profit represents the gains allegedly made from the trades, while the $2.7 million figure reflects the total amount wagered across the related bets. The engineer would have had access to internal Google information about the campaign before it became public.

Federal insider trading laws prohibit trading securities or other instruments based on material nonpublic information. Prosecutors have increasingly applied these statutes beyond traditional stock trading to encompass various financial instruments and markets.

The case is part of ongoing efforts by financial regulators and law enforcement to monitor activity on prediction markets, which have operated in a regulatory gray area. As these platforms have gained users and trading volume, they have attracted greater scrutiny from authorities concerned about potential market manipulation and the misuse of confidential information.

CNN first reported the investigation on Wednesday night. The probe focuses on whether Carroll committed perjury while speaking at a deposition as part of her two civil lawsuits against Trump.

Carroll sued Trump twice in civil court. The first lawsuit alleged defamation after Trump denied her allegations of sexual assault. A second lawsuit made similar claims. Both cases resulted in verdicts against Trump, with juries awarding Carroll substantial damages.

The investigation represents a significant shift in the legal battles between Trump and Carroll. Previously, Trump faced civil liability in courts over his denials of Carroll's allegations. The criminal probe now places Carroll herself under scrutiny by federal prosecutors.

Perjury charges require prosecutors to prove that a witness knowingly made false statements under oath during official proceedings. Depositions in civil lawsuits carry the same legal weight as courtroom testimony, and witnesses face potential criminal penalties for lying.

The Justice Department has not publicly commented on the investigation. Federal prosecutors in criminal cases typically decline to confirm or deny ongoing investigations until charges are filed.

The development adds another layer to the years-long legal conflict between Trump and Carroll. Her initial allegations dated back decades but became public when she published a memoir detailing the alleged assault. Trump's denials led to the defamation lawsuits that ultimately resulted in the depositions now under investigation.

The criminal investigation proceeds separately from the civil cases, which have concluded with judgments against Trump. Federal investigators will examine deposition transcripts and other evidence to determine whether Carroll's statements meet the legal threshold for perjury charges.
