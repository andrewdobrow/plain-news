President Trump's appointment of Bill Pulte as acting director of national intelligence has ignited significant backlash from Senate Republicans and created a crisis over the renewal of critical surveillance authorities expiring next week.

Pulte, who currently heads the Federal Housing Finance Agency and is a major Republican donor from a home construction fortune, has no intelligence experience. His selection came days after Tulsi Gabbard left the role. Senior Democrats immediately warned that the appointment could derail a fragile bipartisan agreement to renew Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, which permits U.S. intelligence agencies to collect communications of foreign targets operating outside the country without a warrant.

The standoff carries unusual leverage. Republicans need at least eight Democratic votes in the Senate to prevent Section 702 from expiring on June 12. Democratic leaders have made clear that a bipartisan deal could collapse unless Trump reverses the appointment.

Senator Mark Warner, vice-chair of the Senate intelligence committee, criticized Pulte's lack of qualifications. "What qualifications does Mr Pulte bring to the office? He has shown that he is willing to do anything that President Trump wants, legal or otherwise," Warner said during a hearing. In an NPR interview, Warner stated the appointment had upended renewal efforts. "I do not have the confidence I had yesterday," he said, warning that Pulte has "no intelligence background" and "a record of misusing private information."

Senate Majority Leader John Thune offered a notably cool response to his own president's pick, telling reporters that "we don't need a weaponized" national intelligence director and warning that Pulte would face "a lengthy road ahead" if nominated permanently. Thune privately asked Mark Warner whether Democrats could reverse course on the surveillance renewal if Pulte remained in the role.

Former Senate Majority Leader Mitch McConnell, who was the only Republican voting against Tulsi Gabbard's confirmation, said on Wednesday that anyone in this role "must have the extensive national security experience required by statute, and no nominee who falls short of this requirement will earn my vote."

Senior Trump administration officials struggled to defend the appointment publicly. Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent did not endorse the decision during congressional hearings.

Critics worry Pulte could weaponize the intelligence community against political opponents. He has previously leveled unproven fraud allegations at Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook while at the FHFA, allegations Trump tried to use to remove her from the Fed's board. Cook has denied wrongdoing, and the matter is now before the Supreme Court.

The current Section 702 renewal bill, circulated by Senators Tom Cotton and Chuck Grassley, would extend the program through June 2029 and include new penalties for intelligence abuses and additional FBI search requirements.

Israel and Lebanon have agreed to implement a renewed cease-fire arrangement brokered by the United States. The Trump administration announced the agreement following negotiations in Washington, with the deal requiring a complete cessation of fire from Hezbollah, an Iran-aligned armed group operating in southern Lebanon.

Under the terms of the agreement, Hezbollah must stop all attacks and evacuate its fighters from the area south of the Litani River in southern Lebanon. Both countries have rejected "any attempt, by any state or non-state actor, to hold Lebanon's future hostage," according to a joint statement released by the U.S. State Department.

While the agreement was signed between the governments of Lebanon and Israel, the actual fighting has been between Hezbollah and Israel. The Lebanese army has not been a direct party to the conflict. However, the Lebanese government has been negotiating with Israel in an effort to reassert government control over the country and disarm the armed group.

The two countries also agreed to create "pilot zones" in which the Lebanese armed forces will take exclusive control of territory to the exclusion of all non-state actors. The joint statement indicated that ongoing meetings in Washington would continue to flesh out the cease-fire details and plan its phased implementation.

However, the agreement faces immediate credibility questions. Despite the announced accord, Israel carried out multiple drone strikes in southern Lebanon on Thursday morning, raising doubts about commitment to the arrangement. Additionally, a previous cease-fire scheduled to take hold on April 17 failed, with both sides continuing hostilities and accusing each other of violations.

A Hezbollah official told media outlets that the group would not accept a partial cease-fire and had not been party to the talks. The group signaled privately, however, that it is ready for a complete cease-fire, meaning it would refrain from attacks if Israel also stopped strikes. Hezbollah has stated it will not return to the pre-war status quo, under which Israel conducted frequent strikes despite the existence of a previous cease-fire.

The cease-fire agreement resembles a 2024 arrangement in which Hezbollah agreed to pull back from south of the Litani River so Lebanese armed forces could deploy there. That earlier accord failed to achieve Hezbollah's full disarmament, and Israel carried out more than 10,000 strikes in violation of the cease-fire during the 15 months that followed.

The negotiations come as the U.S. seeks to resolve broader regional conflicts, including the war with Iran. President Trump has indicated he wants to separate talks on the Lebanon conflict from negotiations with Iran, though Tehran insists the conflicts are linked. The fate of Hezbollah is understood to be connected to outcomes of negotiations with Iran and potentially a broader political settlement in the region.

A new study has found that the National Guard's presence in Washington, D.C. has had no measurable effect on violent crime in the city. Despite this finding, the federal government is moving forward with plans to expand the deployment significantly.

Nearly 2,400 National Guard troops are currently stationed in the capital, according to CNN, at a cost of approximately $1 million per day. The troops were originally set to leave at the end of November, but the deployment has been extended through at least February by Pete Hegseth, who leads the U.S. Department of Defense.

The Guard has been operating in Washington since last August as part of a federal task force aimed at reducing crime. The decision to continue and expand their presence comes despite research showing their deployment has not reduced violent crime rates.

The continued military presence in the nation's capital has become increasingly controversial. Brian Schwalb, the District of Columbia's attorney general, has characterized the deployment as an "involuntary military occupation" and has sued the Trump administration, arguing it represents an illegal use of military forces for domestic law enforcement.

The Washington deployment is part of a broader federal strategy to address crime in major cities. Following the initial dispatch to Washington, the administration has sent National Guard troops to Chicago and has threatened deployments to other Democratic-led cities including San Francisco, Portland, and New York.

Legal challenges to these deployments have already emerged. A federal judge in California ruled in September that Trump's deployment of National Guard troops to Los Angeles following protests was illegal. However, that ruling does not directly apply to Washington, where the president has greater authority over the National Guard compared to deployments in states.

The expansion of the Guard's role extends beyond Washington. The Pentagon has ordered National Guard units in all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and U.S. territories to form "quick reaction forces" trained in riot control tactics. These forces are being trained to use batons, body shields, Tasers, and pepper spray, according to an internal Pentagon directive.

Most states are required to train at least 500 National Guard members for these quick reaction forces, bringing the total to approximately 23,500 troops nationwide. State guard units are required to report monthly on their progress in developing these capabilities.

The decision to maintain and expand the Guard's presence in Washington despite the study's findings demonstrating no crime reduction represents a significant federal intervention in urban policing. The deployment marks one of the more extensive domestic uses of military personnel for law enforcement purposes in recent memory and has drawn sharp criticism from Democratic officials and local organizers across affected cities.

Nvidia has unveiled the RTX Spark chip, a new "superchip" designed to bring artificial intelligence capabilities directly to laptops and desktop computers. The move marks a significant expansion for the semiconductor company beyond its dominant position in data center hardware, pitching it against established PC chipmakers including Intel, Apple, Qualcomm and AMD.

The RTX Spark, which combines a microprocessor and graphics chip developed with Taiwan's MediaTek, is scheduled to launch this year. Computer makers including Dell, Lenovo, Asus and HP plan to use the chip paired with Microsoft's Windows software. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang announced the partnership at Computex in Taiwan, describing the chip as a way to "reinvent the PC" for the AI era after three years of collaboration with Microsoft.

The chip is designed to run AI agents locally on personal computers rather than relying on cloud computing. This approach would allow AI agents to navigate computers autonomously, potentially replacing traditional mouse and keyboard interactions. Despite its power, the company stated that computers using the chip can remain thin and light.

Huang framed the development as a reimagining of the PC for the first time in 40 years. The move opens a new business line for Nvidia, though analysts cautioned that building presence in the consumer PC industry will require time to develop.

Nvidia is also introducing the Vera CPU, designed for AI agents. Early adopters of the Vera platform include OpenAI, Anthropic and SpaceX. These products demonstrate the company's growing focus on expanding beyond graphics cards into integrated chips that power entire computers.

Industry observers view the RTX Spark as potentially transformative. Neil Shah, co-founder of Counterpoint Research, compared the moment to the iPhone, ChatGPT and DeepSeek launches, predicting that AI-powered personal computers will eventually become commonplace. Susannah Streeter, chief investment strategist at Wealth Club, called it a bold attempt to extend Nvidia's dominance into consumers' daily lives, though she noted investors likely see it as a longer-term growth opportunity rather than an immediate earnings driver.

The company's pivot to personal AI computing comes as other chipmakers pursue similar strategies. Intel announced plans to ship an AI chip later this year using cheaper memory and cooling technology than its California rivals. Intel's new graphics processing unit, codenamed Crescent Island, is being positioned as purpose-built for the upcoming generation of AI agents.

Addressing concerns about AI's impact on employment, Huang dismissed suggestions that the technology would reduce jobs, arguing instead that it would increase demand for software engineers by making workers more productive.
