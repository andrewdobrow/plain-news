Speaker Mike Johnson told President Trump that the proposed $1.776 billion anti-weaponization fund has become a political liability for Republicans trying to pass legislation in Congress, and indicated the administration is moving away from the proposal.

"I told him that it was a difficult prospect right now, given our vote tallies," Johnson said Tuesday, referring to Republicans' slim House majority. With narrow margins, Johnson explained, the ongoing controversy over the settlement fund threatens to divide his caucus and undermine efforts to advance other priorities.

The fund would compensate people who claim they were wrongfully prosecuted or targeted by the federal government. Since its announcement last month as part of a settlement in Trump's lawsuit against the IRS, the program has faced intense legal and political opposition.

A federal judge temporarily blocked the administration from transferring money from the fund after a lawsuit was filed to dissolve it. The court action combined with mounting Republican concern has pushed administration officials to back away from the proposal. The Justice Department said it would comply with the court order halting the fund's operations.

Republican criticism of the fund has been substantial. Vice President Mike Pence called the possibility of compensating January 6 rioters "deeply offensive," noting the concern is broadly shared among Republicans and Americans. Several states, including California, Illinois, New York and Connecticut, have proposed or announced plans to tax any distributions from the fund.

Senate Majority Leader John Thune suggested the best approach would be for the administration to voluntarily shut down the fund, saying "I do think the best way to handle it is if the administration decides to shut it down themselves."

Democrats have also mounted aggressive opposition to the program. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer called it "corruption in broad daylight" and a "$1.8 billion Maga slush fund." Democrats have vowed to force Republicans to vote on measures blocking fund payments and preserving records related to the settlement.

Despite the apparent retreat by the Trump administration and Johnson's declaration that the fund is off the table, some Republicans are pushing for formal congressional action. Representative Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania said he plans to force a House vote on legislation to block the fund, suggesting lawmakers want an explicit legislative decision on the matter regardless of the administration's current position.

Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche was scheduled to testify before a House Appropriations Committee panel about Justice Department oversight, with the anti-weaponization fund expected to be a central topic of discussion. The fund's uncertain status reflects broader tensions between executive branch actions and congressional oversight authority, particularly given Republicans' narrow majority in the House.

Two people were shot dead during protests in Kenya against a United States plan to build an Ebola quarantine and treatment facility in the country. The demonstrations took place near the site of the proposed center, reflecting widespread public opposition to the project.

The Trump administration confirmed it was establishing a quarantine and treatment facility in Kenya for Americans exposed to or infected with Ebola. According to a White House official, the facility would provide high-quality care for Americans who need to quickly leave the Democratic Republic of the Congo without the risks of a lengthy transport back to the US. The center would also treat Americans who contract the virus, including critical care services, though cases would be evaluated for further transport to maximize patient outcomes.

The proposal has generated significant anger among Kenyan residents living near the proposed site at Laikipia Air Base in Nanyuki, about 120 miles from Nairobi. Local residents have organized demonstrations to oppose the facility, citing concerns that it could expose them to the virus in a country with no known Ebola cases.

Charles Mathenge, a taxi driver living near the proposed site, expressed the community's concerns. "Everybody should be quarantined in their home country. We shouldn't allow foreigners to bring us diseases," he said. "Kenya is our country, and we should be careful with it."

The plan has also drawn criticism from American public health experts. Jennifer Nuzzo, an epidemiologist and director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University School of Public Health, raised ethical concerns about the approach. "There are profound ethical concerns with this approach," Nuzzo said. She warned that without adequate plans for safe quarantine of exposed individuals, the facilities could amplify virus spread and discourage people from disclosing exposure.

Jeremy Konyndyk, president of Refugees International and a former leader in the 2014-2015 Ebola response, expressed concern that the policy would discourage American health workers from volunteering. He noted that in 2014, the US avoided travel bans because such restrictions would ultimately be counterproductive to ending the outbreak. "It disincentivizes people from going," Konyndyk said of the current plan.

US officials did not clarify whether Americans would be allowed to return to the US if they chose not to go to Kenya, or whether further transport from the Kenya facility would be to the US or Europe. The US has banned green card holders and other recent travelers from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Uganda, and South Sudan from entering the country.

The shooting deaths represent a serious escalation in public resistance to the proposed medical infrastructure. The violence is likely to intensify the debate over the facility and complicate US public health planning in the region.

President Trump has appointed Bill Pulte, head of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, to serve as acting director of national intelligence, replacing Tulsi Gabbard who recently announced her departure from the role.

Pulte, an heir to a home construction company fortune with no background in national security or intelligence, will assume the position immediately as acting director while continuing to lead the FHFA, which oversees the federal mortgage lenders Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Trump announced the decision on his Truth Social platform, stating that "William has deep experience managing the most sensitive matters in America."

The appointment has drawn immediate scrutiny from multiple quarters. Pulte's tenure at the housing agency has been marked by aggressive actions against targets he has publicly accused of wrongdoing. Shortly after arriving at the FHFA, he removed substantial portions of the boards overseeing Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac and appointed himself as chair. Most notably, he has made extraordinary allegations against Trump's political opponents, including Federal Reserve governor Lisa Cook, whom he has accused of mortgage fraud. Cook has denied any wrongdoing.

Senate Republican leaders have expressed considerable unease with the selection. Senate Majority Leader John Thune told reporters that the country does not need "a weaponized" director of national intelligence and warned that Pulte would face "a lengthy road ahead of him" if nominated for permanent confirmation. Thune emphasized the need for "professionals" in intelligence leadership rather than individuals who might pursue political opponents.

A former high-ranking official described Pulte's handling of the Cook case as "very bizarre." The growing questions surrounding his fraud referrals at the FHFA have raised concerns about whether similar patterns could extend to his intelligence role.

Critics have also pointed to Pulte's family background, with some describing him as a "nepo baby" given his ties to the PulteGroup building firm. However, the New York Times reported that Pulte no longer holds any official connection to the company after being pushed off the board in 2020. The Pulte Family Charitable Foundation, which has assets of approximately 500 million dollars, has taken steps to distance itself from him, stating in 2023 that "Bill Pulte is not a representative of, nor has he served in any capacity" for the foundation.

The appointment comes days after Gabbard's exit from the intelligence director position. According to sources briefed on internal discussions, Trump had been asking cabinet members as early as April whether he should replace Gabbard, who appeared marginalized during her tenure as Trump pursued aggressive foreign policy actions.

For Pulte to serve permanently in the role, the Senate must confirm his nomination, a process that will likely involve hearings and extensive questioning about his qualifications and potential conflicts of interest.

1:10 PM ET

Ebola outbreak in Congo hampered by conflict and medical shortages

An Ebola outbreak in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo is spreading faster than the response can contain it, according to the head of the World Health Organization. The situation has been worsened by armed conflict in the region and critical shortages of medical supplies.

As of Tuesday, Congo's health ministry confirmed 101 Ebola cases, though the number of suspected cases is approaching 1,000. At least 220 suspected deaths have been reported. The outbreak was confirmed on May 15 in Ituri province, which borders South Sudan and Uganda. It has since spread to rebel-held areas of North Kivu and South Kivu provinces.

WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus called for an immediate ceasefire in eastern Congo to help control the outbreak. He described the situation as a "catastrophic collision of disease and conflict" and said he would travel to the DRC this week. Tedros emphasized that stopping transmission depends on humanitarian access, but ongoing armed clashes are displacing populations and making it impossible to track cases and their contacts.

The conflict is directly hampering medical response efforts. Health facilities have been attacked by people demanding the release of Ebola victims' bodies for traditional burials, which involve family members washing and touching the deceased. Since Ebola victims' bodies remain highly contagious, these practices have spread the disease in previous outbreaks. During attacks on a hospital in Mongbwalu on Saturday and Sunday, 25 Ebola patients fled after unidentified individuals burned isolation tents.

Medical staff are also facing severe equipment shortages. A doctor in Butembo reported having only two body bags remaining and said the facility lacks adequate soap, chlorine, and personal protective gear including boots, body suits, masks, and gloves. The doctor noted that foreign aid cuts to international and local aid organizations have contributed to these shortages.

Save the Children reported that a quarter of confirmed deaths have been children. The organization called for infection prevention measures to be urgently scaled up, noting that years of conflict have weakened community systems and left many young people malnourished and vulnerable to the virus.

Uganda has reported seven confirmed Ebola cases and one death. In response, Uganda closed its border with the DRC for four weeks, with exceptions only for Ebola response teams, humanitarian operations, security operations, and food and cargo transport. Any authorized traveler from the Congo must undergo mandatory self-isolation for 21 days.

Aid groups are rushing staff and equipment to eastern Congo, but community distrust has led to attacks on health workers, further hampering containment efforts. The UN refugee agency reported that transit and reception sites in Uganda's West Nile region are operating at more than double capacity.

1:42 PM ET

The Cleveland Browns traded star edge rusher Myles Garrett to the Los Angeles Rams on Monday in exchange for linebacker Jared Verse and three draft picks. The deal marks a significant shift for both franchises as they reshape their rosters.

Garrett, 30, is a two-time NFL Defensive Player of the Year and seven-time Pro Bowl selection. Since the Browns selected him first overall in 2017, he has become one of the league's most dominant defensive players. He holds the record for most sacks (125.5), pressures (413), and tackles for loss (149) among all players since his draft year. In 2025, he set a single-season sacks record with 23.

The star edge rusher had requested a trade from the Browns in 2024 after the team finished with a 3-14 record, stating he wanted to play for a championship contender. However, the Browns signed him to a four-year extension worth 160 million dollars the following month, making him the highest-paid non-quarterback in league history at that time. Despite his individual success, the Browns finished the 2025 season with a 5-12 record and missed the playoffs for the second consecutive year.

The trade became possible after the Browns and Garrett agreed to modify his contract in March, deferring option payments across the 2026-28 seasons. Browns general manager Andrew Berry explained the decision, saying the team faced "a legitimate crossroads: do we hold on to a truly generational player who has become the identity of our team, or do we make the difficult decision that we think is best for the organization over the long run?"

The Rams received a 2027 first-round selection, a 2028 second-round pick, and a 2029 third-round selection as part of the trade. Verse, 25, was a first-round draft pick by the Rams in 2024 and earned Defensive Rookie of the Year honors. He was selected to the Pro Bowl this past season and has recorded 12 career sacks along with 22 tackles for loss.

For Los Angeles, the trade represents an aggressive move to compete after losing to the Seattle Seahawks in last season's NFC Championship Game. The Rams have a history of making bold roster moves, most notably trading for quarterback Matthew Stafford in 2021, who led them to a Super Bowl victory in his first season. Stafford won his first NFL MVP award last season.

Garrett's arrival in Los Angeles marks the first time the reigning AP NFL MVP and Defensive Player of the Year will be teammates. The Rams will host Super Bowl LXI at SoFi Stadium next February, giving them the opportunity to pursue another championship on their home field.

An Ebola outbreak in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo is spreading faster than the response can contain it, according to the head of the World Health Organization. The situation has been worsened by armed conflict in the region and critical shortages of medical supplies.

As of Tuesday, Congo's health ministry confirmed 101 Ebola cases, though the number of suspected cases is approaching 1,000. At least 220 suspected deaths have been reported. The outbreak was confirmed on May 15 in Ituri province, which borders South Sudan and Uganda. It has since spread to rebel-held areas of North Kivu and South Kivu provinces.

WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus called for an immediate ceasefire in eastern Congo to help control the outbreak. He described the situation as a "catastrophic collision of disease and conflict" and said he would travel to the DRC this week. Tedros emphasized that stopping transmission depends on humanitarian access, but ongoing armed clashes are displacing populations and making it impossible to track cases and their contacts.

The conflict is directly hampering medical response efforts. Health facilities have been attacked by people demanding the release of Ebola victims' bodies for traditional burials, which involve family members washing and touching the deceased. Since Ebola victims' bodies remain highly contagious, these practices have spread the disease in previous outbreaks. During attacks on a hospital in Mongbwalu on Saturday and Sunday, 25 Ebola patients fled after unidentified individuals burned isolation tents.

Medical staff are also facing severe equipment shortages. A doctor in Butembo reported having only two body bags remaining and said the facility lacks adequate soap, chlorine, and personal protective gear including boots, body suits, masks, and gloves. The doctor noted that foreign aid cuts to international and local aid organizations have contributed to these shortages.

Save the Children reported that a quarter of confirmed deaths have been children. The organization called for infection prevention measures to be urgently scaled up, noting that years of conflict have weakened community systems and left many young people malnourished and vulnerable to the virus.

Uganda has reported seven confirmed Ebola cases and one death. In response, Uganda closed its border with the DRC for four weeks, with exceptions only for Ebola response teams, humanitarian operations, security operations, and food and cargo transport. Any authorized traveler from the Congo must undergo mandatory self-isolation for 21 days.

Aid groups are rushing staff and equipment to eastern Congo, but community distrust has led to attacks on health workers, further hampering containment efforts. The UN refugee agency reported that transit and reception sites in Uganda's West Nile region are operating at more than double capacity.
