A federal appeals court has ruled that the Trump administration's policy to remove transgender service members currently serving in the military violates the Constitution. The divided decision by a three-judge panel of the US Court of Appeals for Washington DC dealt a significant blow to the administration's approach to military personnel policies.

The court's majority opinion found the ban to be "arbitrary, and based on animus," according to the ruling. The decision distinguishes between expelling troops already on active duty and blocking new transgender recruits from enlisting. While the court blocked the removal of current service members, it allowed the armed services to continue preventing transgender individuals from joining the military.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth responded to the ruling by signaling his intention to appeal the decision to the Supreme Court. In a statement, Hegseth indicated he is prepared to take the case all the way to the nation's highest court, saying "see you at SCOTUS," referring to the Supreme Court of the United States.

The ruling affects transgender troops who have been serving openly in the military and now face potential expulsion under Hegseth's policy. The court's decision creates an immediate legal barrier to the Defense Department enforcing the ban against active duty personnel.

Legal experts expect the government to challenge the appeals court decision, and the case is likely to ultimately reach the Supreme Court. The outcome could have significant consequences for the Trump administration's broader agenda, as the appeals court characterized the ban as part of an "anti-diversity" policy.

The divided nature of the panel suggests contentious disagreements among the judges about the legal issues involved. Courts have increasingly scrutinized military policies enacted during the Trump administration's second term, particularly when those policies involve discrimination claims.

The Supreme Court previously addressed transgender military service during Trump's first term, but legal analysts note that this case involves different constitutional questions. The current dispute centers on whether the government can remove service members already serving in uniform, rather than on policies affecting new enlistments.

The ruling creates uncertainty for affected service members, leaving them in legal limbo as the case moves through the appeals process. Supporters of transgender military service have celebrated the decision as a victory for equal treatment under the law. Administration officials have continued to argue that the policy is necessary for military readiness and unit cohesion.

At least 18 people were killed in a major overnight missile and drone attack on Kyiv, marking the most deadly night raid on the Ukrainian capital since a failed diplomatic attempt in August. The strikes also targeted other cities including Dnipro and Kharkiv, with rescue teams searching for survivors trapped beneath rubble across multiple locations.

Seventeen people died when a five-story residential building in Kyiv's eastern Darnytskyi district was struck in the middle of the night. Another person was killed in the central Shevchenkivskyi district near the railway station. An additional 38 people were wounded across the capital, while 10 people remained unaccounted for from the main strike, though not all may have been in the building at the time of impact.

Four children were among the dead, with one victim as young as two years old. Emergency responders deployed 200 rescuers and 59 rescue vehicles to the main site, where search and cleanup efforts continued into the night. Officials reported impacts at more than 20 locations around Kyiv.

The overnight assault was one of the largest aerial attacks mounted by Russia since the war began. Ukraine's air force said the country was targeted with 629 missiles and drones, including 11 ballistic missiles, 20 cruise missiles, and 598 Shahed drones. While most were intercepted or jammed, three high-speed ballistic missiles and two cruise missiles reached their targets.

The strikes damaged numerous civilian infrastructure sites. The EU delegation to Ukraine and the British Council both sustained severe damage near the railway station. EU Ambassador Katarina Mathernova reported that the delegation's offices were "severely damaged by the shock wave," while the British Council building had its front windows shattered. EU Commission President Ursula von der Leyen confirmed staff were safe.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said the attack demonstrated Russia's unwillingness to pursue genuine negotiations. "Russia chooses ballistics instead of the negotiating table," he stated on social media, calling for international sanctions and an international response to the strikes.

The attack came after a period of relatively reduced bombardment during August, when diplomatic efforts focused on negotiating a ceasefire. However, the Kremlin indicated it remained "still interested" in diplomacy while continuing its military campaign.

Kyiv Mayor Vitali Klitschko declared Friday a day of mourning, describing it as "a terrible night" for the capital with extensive building damage. A railway siding in eastern Kyiv was also bombed, with intercity carriages burned out and journey delays expected.

UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer condemned the strikes, saying "Putin is killing children and civilians, and sabotaging hopes of peace."

The fundraising demonstrates the escalating financial demands of competing in the AI sector. Alphabet stated that it is "experiencing strong demand for its AI solutions and services from enterprises and consumers, at levels that are exceeding the company's available supply." The company plans to use the capital to expand its foundational infrastructure and support future growth opportunities.

Berkshire Hathaway's investment is particularly significant given the conglomerate's historical preference for traditional businesses over technology companies. The decision reflects growing institutional confidence in AI as a transformative technology. Under Warren Buffett's leadership, Berkshire has a track record of providing substantial funding to companies during critical moments, including its $5 billion investment in Goldman Sachs during the 2008 financial crisis.

The capital raise comes as technology giants including Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta have each committed tens of billions of dollars to AI development. This funding competition has created pressure on corporate balance sheets, even for the most profitable companies. By raising capital through stock sales rather than debt, Alphabet can maintain its competitive position without increasing financial leverage, though the move will dilute existing shareholders.

Industry analysts note that this massive fundraising signals important shifts in how AI development is being financed. Ipek Ozkardeskaya, senior analyst at Swissquote, explained that AI funding is becoming increasingly institutionalized. "The financing is becoming increasingly institutionalized," she said, noting that the AI story is evolving from purely a technology narrative toward a financing and credit narrative. As traditional financial institutions deepen their involvement, AI-related investments are spreading across pension funds, insurers, asset managers, and corporate balance sheets.

Jim Reid, market strategist at Deutsche Bank, told clients that Alphabet's announcement reminds investors of the "unprecedented scale of the AI spending boom" and that "funding of the AI capex boom is becoming an increasingly key topic for markets."

The announcement comes as Anthropic, which produces the Claude chatbot, filed confidentially for an initial public offering. Anthropic's valuation has reached approximately $965 billion after raising $65 billion in funding, surpassing OpenAI as the world's most valuable startup. Both companies are preparing to access public markets to fund their expanding AI operations, indicating that major AI-focused companies view equity offerings as essential to sustaining their growth trajectories.
