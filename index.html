The United States military conducted strikes against Iranian targets at the Strait of Hormuz on Thursday, prompting Iran to respond with missiles and drones. The exchange represents the most serious test of a month-long ceasefire between the two nations.

The US military said it targeted sites responsible for attacking three American destroyers: the USS Truxtun, USS Rafael Peralta, and USS Mason. According to US Central Command, Iranian forces launched multiple missiles, drones, and small boats at the vessels, though no US assets were struck. The military said it eliminated inbound threats and targeted Iranian military facilities including missile and drone launch sites and command centers.

Iran's military offered a different account, accusing the US of violating the ceasefire by targeting two ships at the Strait of Hormuz and striking civilian areas. The Iranian military spokesperson claimed the US carried out airstrikes on coastal cities including Bandar Khamir and Sirik, as well as the island of Qeshm, which has about 150,000 residents. Iran's armed forces responded by attacking US military vessels, the spokesperson said, claiming to have inflicted significant damage.

President Trump characterized the incident minimally, calling it a "love tap" during an interview with ABC News. "They trifled with us today. We blew them away," Trump told reporters. He insisted the ceasefire remained intact despite the strikes.

On the negotiations front, Trump said a deal "might not happen, but it could happen any day," adding that he believed Iran wanted an agreement more than his administration did. Reports prior to Thursday's exchanges suggested the US and Iran may have been close to a temporary agreement, with a one-page memorandum being shared between Washington and Tehran through Pakistan.

However, senior Iranian officials have recently rejected concessions. Some analysts suggest Iran may prefer to drag out negotiations closer to November's midterm elections in the US, when the Trump administration could face greater pressure to settle the conflict. Regional diplomats warn that Iran risks overplaying its hand, as the current moment offers an opportunity to end the war and claim victory before fighting potentially resumes at a larger scale.

The escalation immediately affected energy markets. Brent crude oil rose to approximately $101 per barrel following news of the attacks. The conflict has already created concerns about fuel costs for American consumers, with rising gas prices becoming a factor in political calculations.

The ceasefire, which had largely held for the previous month, now faces serious questions about its viability. Both sides have accused the other of violations, and despite diplomatic efforts to negotiate an end to the three-month conflict, military operations continue.

Australia alleges that 3M concealed information about t...

4:52 AM ET

The Australian government filed a lawsuit against manufacturing giant 3M seeking $1.4 billion in damages, marking the largest lawsuit ever brought by the Australian government.

The case centers on PFAS, a group of synthetic chemicals commonly called forever chemicals because they persist in the environment and human body. Australia alleges that 3M concealed information about the harmful effects of PFAS used at military installations across the country.

PFAS chemicals have been widely used in firefighting foams at military bases for decades. The substances do not break down naturally and can accumulate in soil, water, and living organisms over time.

The Australian government's legal action represents an effort to hold the company accountable for environmental contamination at defense sites. The chemicals have raised concerns about potential health impacts on service members, base workers, and surrounding communities.

3M manufactured aqueous film-forming foams containing PFAS that were deployed at numerous Australian military facilities. The government contends the company knew about risks associated with these chemicals but failed to disclose that information to users and regulators.

The $1.4 billion figure reflects Australia's assessment of cleanup costs, environmental damage, and other impacts stemming from PFAS contamination at the affected sites. The lawsuit seeks to recover funds the government says it will need to address the contamination.

Similar legal battles over PFAS contamination have unfolded in other countries, particularly in the United States, where 3M and other manufacturers have faced thousands of lawsuits. The chemical industry has come under increasing scrutiny as research continues to emerge about the persistence and potential health effects of these compounds.

The case will proceed through Australian courts, where the government will need to prove its allegations that 3M withheld critical safety information about the products used at military bases.

Federal authorities have charged a Google engineer with insider trading after the employee earned $1.2 million through wagers on the prediction market platform Polymarket.

The complaint alleges the engineer placed bets totaling over $2.7 million on wagers connected to Google's 2025 Year in Search campaign. The charges suggest the employee used non-public information about the company's annual search trends summary to profit on the betting platform.

Polymarket operates as a prediction market where users can wager on the outcomes of future events, ranging from political elections to corporate announcements. The platform has grown in popularity as a tool for forecasting, but it has also raised regulatory concerns about potential misuse of insider information.

The case represents one of the first major insider trading prosecutions involving prediction markets rather than traditional stock trading. Federal prosecutors appear to be signaling that insider trading laws apply equally to cryptocurrency-based betting platforms as they do to conventional securities markets.

Google has not yet commented publicly on the charges against its employee. The engineer's name was not provided in the available information about the complaint.

Insider trading occurs when someone trades or bets using material non-public information that could affect the value of a security or, in this case, the outcome of a prediction market. Federal law prohibits such activity because it undermines fair markets and gives unfair advantages to those with access to confidential information.

The charges come as regulators have increased scrutiny of prediction markets and their potential for abuse. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission has previously raised questions about whether certain prediction markets should be regulated as derivatives exchanges.

The size of the alleged profit, at $1.2 million, represents a substantial return on information that should have remained confidential within Google. Prosecutors will need to prove the engineer had access to non-public information about the Year in Search campaign and used that information to place the bets.
