2:29 AM ET

The United States and Iran have reached a tentative agreement on a 60-day ceasefire extension, though the deal requires final approval from President Trump. Vice President Vance said the two countries are very close to finalizing the arrangement, describing the negotiations as nearing completion.

According to a U.S. official who spoke with Axios, the proposed deal would include several key components. During the 60-day period, the Strait of Hormuz would be reopened for trade, Iran would be permitted to freely sell oil, and both nations would hold talks on limiting Iran's nuclear program. Iran would also agree to clear mines it has deployed in the strait and refrain from imposing tolls on ships passing through.

In return, the U.S. would lift its blockade on Iranian ports that has been in effect since April 13. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the deal could achieve "a completely open" Strait of Hormuz "without tolls" if successful, though Iranian media has reported that the strait would remain under Iranian control.

The agreement would reportedly unfreeze some Iranian assets held in banks outside Iran. Hostilities would be halted on all fronts, including between Israel and Lebanon.

A senior Iranian source told Reuters that Tehran had not agreed to hand over its highly enriched uranium stockpile. The source stated that Iran's nuclear issue was not part of the preliminary agreement with the U.S., contradicting earlier reporting that Tehran had expressed willingness to give up the stockpile.

The draft plan has been put on the table by both sides, though multiple details remain unconfirmed. Neither government has released comprehensive details about all terms included in the proposed ceasefire. The 60-day timeframe would serve as an initial cooling-off period rather than a permanent end to hostilities.

U.S. officials indicated they are moving closer to a deal despite ongoing military engagement between the nations. The negotiations have progressed even as fighting continues, with officials acknowledging the apparent contradiction between ongoing combat and the diplomatic push toward a ceasefire.

Trump has not yet signed off on the tentative deal. His approval remains the final step before the ceasefire can take effect. Officials did not provide a timeline for when Trump might make a decision on the proposed agreement.

The deal marks a significant development in efforts to halt the conflict between the two nations. It remains possible that some aspects of the agreement could change before final authorization is given.

3:29 AM ET

A Blue Origin New Glenn rocket exploded during a static fire test at Cape Canaveral in Florida on Thursday night, destroying the launch pad in a massive fireball. The explosion occurred at 9pm ET during what was scheduled as a routine engine-firing test before the rocket's planned satellite launch.

The blast was visible for miles along Florida's Atlantic coast. An orange sky was reported in Fort Pierce, 185 kilometers south of the launch site, and shockwaves were felt across the space coast region. Residents in nearby Cape Canaveral and Cocoa Beach reported their homes shaking, while people in South Carolina, hundreds of miles away, reported seeing a glow in the sky. Flames continued burning at the launch pad for more than two hours after the explosion.

Blue Origin founder Jeff Bezos confirmed that all employees were safe and uninjured. He acknowledged the incident on social media, saying "It's a very rough day" and added that while it was too early to determine the root cause, the company was already investigating. "We'll rebuild whatever needs rebuilding and get back to flying," Bezos wrote.

The explosion carries significant consequences for NASA's lunar program. NASA had announced just two days earlier that Blue Origin had won a contract to launch the first of three planned uncrewed missions this year to begin construction of a $20 billion lunar base. The New Glenn rocket is the vehicle NASA intends to use for these missions, including launching landers that will eventually transport astronauts to the moon's surface.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said the agency would conduct a full evaluation of the timeline for the Artemis program following the explosion. "Spaceflight is unforgiving, and developing new heavy-lift launch capability is extraordinarily difficult," he stated. NASA indicated it would work with Blue Origin to support a thorough investigation and assess the impact on near-term missions.

This represents the latest in a series of setbacks for Blue Origin as it competes with SpaceX for NASA contracts. Last month, a payload from the New Glenn's third flight ended up in the wrong orbit, leading the Federal Aviation Administration to temporarily ground the rocket. Thursday's test was the first static fire test since the FAA cleared the rocket to return to flight the previous week.

The explosion also affects Blue Origin's competition with SpaceX for the Artemis program. Both companies have built new facilities at Cape Canaveral to support crewed and cargo missions. NASA plans to test both Blue Origin's Blue Moon lander and SpaceX's Starship Human Landing System during the Artemis III mission in 2027 to determine which will carry astronauts to the lunar surface in the subsequent Artemis IV mission scheduled for 2028.

SpaceX founder Elon Musk commented briefly on the incident, saying "Most unfortunate" and noting that "Rockets are hard."
