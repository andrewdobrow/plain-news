US proposes rule allowing government to cancel research grants at any time

Yesterday, 6:58 PM ET

The US government has proposed new funding rules that would allow officials to terminate research grants at any time, according to a policy draft. The changes would also make peer review optional for grant decisions and introduce screening by political staff to identify forbidden research topics.

The proposed rules represent a substantial shift in how federal research funding operates. Under current practice, grants undergo peer review by independent scientists who evaluate proposals based on scientific merit. The new framework would give political appointees authority to screen applications before approval.

The policy would also eliminate the requirement for peer review in the grant approval process. Peer review has traditionally served as a cornerstone of scientific funding, with researchers in relevant fields assessing the quality and potential impact of proposed studies.

Under the proposed system, political staff would review grant applications to determine whether they address topics the government has designated as forbidden. The draft does not specify which topics would fall under this classification.

The termination provision would give officials discretion to cancel grants after they have been awarded and research has begun. Current grant agreements typically include specific conditions under which funding can be withdrawn, often related to compliance failures or misconduct.

Researchers rely on federal grants to fund laboratories, pay staff, and conduct studies across fields including medicine, climate science, physics, and engineering. Multi-year grants allow scientists to plan long-term projects and maintain research teams.

The proposal comes as the administration examines federal spending across agencies. Research institutions and universities receive billions in federal grants annually to support both basic science and applied research projects.

The changes would affect grants distributed by federal agencies including the National Institutes of Health, the National Science Foundation, and the Department of Energy. These agencies currently award funding through competitive processes that emphasize scientific merit and potential societal benefit.

The proposed rules are subject to a public comment period before any final decision on implementation.

A Russian drone struck an apartment building in Galati, Romania on Friday, injuring two people in the first such attack on a NATO member state, according to Romanian and alliance officials. The incident represents a significant escalation in the spillover from Russia's war in Ukraine.

The drone hit a residential building in Galati, located on the Danube River west of Romania's borders with Moldova and Ukraine. The impact sparked a fire and forced the evacuation of several residents. Romanian military scrambled two F-16 fighter jets and a helicopter authorized to engage targets in response.

NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte responded swiftly to the incident. "Russia's reckless behaviour is a danger to us all," Rutte wrote on social media after speaking with Romanian President Nicuşor Dan. "I affirmed that Nato stands ready to defend every inch of allied territory."

Dan convened an emergency meeting of Romania's supreme council of national defence and instructed the foreign ministry to prepare measures regarding the country's relationship with Moscow. Romania's foreign minister, Oana Țoiu, said the government would "officially communicate the consequences that this lack of responsibility on the part of the Russian Federation will have for the diplomatic relations between our countries."

Romanian officials described the incident as a "grave and irresponsible escalation" and a serious breach of international law. Romania's defence ministry said the drone was tracked by radar in Romanian airspace before it crashed. The country requested that NATO accelerate the transfer of anti-drone capabilities and signaled it would invoke Article 4 of the NATO treaty, which triggers formal discussions within the alliance about threats to member security.

Russia denied direct responsibility for the strike. Vladimir Putin claimed without evidence that the drone might have been a stray Ukrainian weapon and suggested the wreckage be handed over to Russia for independent investigation. However, NATO confirmed the drone was "of Russian origin" despite Moscow's denials.

General Gheorghe Maxim, a commander for Romania's armed forces, characterized the strike as not "an attack from Russia against Romania," but emphasized that "Romanians should understand that Russia is a threat to the security of the countries in the area."

The incident drew condemnation from European leaders. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said Russia's "war of aggression has crossed yet another line" and pledged solidarity with Romania. France's foreign minister also denounced what he called an "irresponsible act by Russia."

Romania's response reflects broader European concern about the war's potential to expand beyond Ukraine's borders. The strike came amid Russia's intensified campaign of systematic strikes against Ukrainian targets and growing anxiety among NATO members about Russia's hybrid warfare tactics across Eastern Europe.

4:34 AM ET

A federal judge has ordered the removal of Donald Trump's name from the Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts in Washington, ruling that the prestigious venue cannot be renamed without Congressional approval.

U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper issued a 94-page decision on Friday requiring the Trump administration to remove all physical signage bearing Trump's name and eliminate any references to a "Trump Kennedy Center" from official materials within 14 days.

"The Kennedy Center's organic statute makes crystal clear that the Center is to be named for President Kennedy, and it cannot bear any other formal name or public memorial based on the Board's unilateral say-so," Cooper wrote. "Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name, and only Congress can change it."

The judge determined that the Kennedy Center's board of trustees exceeded its authority by unilaterally attempting to rename the institution after Trump. The ruling also temporarily blocked the center from closing this summer for proposed two-year renovations that Trump had announced.

Cooper criticized the board's decision-making process, stating that board members were "derelict in discharging" their responsibilities by basing the closure decision on insufficient information and failing to consider the full consequences for programming and memorial functions. He called the decision to halt operations "ill-informed and seemingly preordained."

The lawsuit was brought by Joyce Beatty, an Ohio Democratic representative who serves on the Kennedy Center's board by virtue of her Congressional position. "Today's ruling rightly affirms that this administration's efforts to rename and close the Center have no basis in law," Beatty said. "The Kennedy Center is an institution that belongs to the American people, not to Donald Trump."

Trump responded to the ruling by posting on Truth Social that he instructed the Department of Commerce to arrange a complete transfer of the Kennedy Center to Congress. "Unless I am free to do what I do better than anyone else, bring this Institution back, physically, financially, and artistically, I have no interest in continuing what could only be a hopeless journey," Trump wrote.

The dispute stems from Trump's appointment of himself as chair of the Kennedy Center's board in February after taking office. He replaced board members with his own appointees, and in December the board voted to add his name to the institution. The move sparked significant controversy, with numerous performing groups and artists canceling bookings in protest, including a run of the Tony Award-winning musical Hamilton. The National Symphony Orchestra's executive director also departed to lead another arts organization.

The Kennedy Center, which receives federal funding, has long been one of America's leading arts venues and enjoyed bipartisan support before becoming embroiled in the Trump administration's broader effort to reshape the nation's cultural institutions.

Energy markets have already experienced volatility as traders assess the potential duration of the supply constraints and the likelihood of additional disruptions to oil flows from the region.

Yesterday, 10:18 PM ET

The US military carried out another strike on a boat in the eastern Pacific Ocean on Friday, killing three men in what authorities described as a counter-narcotics operation.

US Southern Command announced the strike, stating that intelligence confirmed the vessel was traveling along known drug-trafficking routes and engaged in narco-trafficking operations. The command said the boat was operated by designated terrorist organizations, though it provided no evidence to support these claims.

A 16-second video posted by Southern Command shows a single strike being launched at the boat, which subsequently burst into flames. No US military forces were harmed in the operation.

The Friday strike marks the third attack this week and brings the total number of people killed in US military strikes on suspected drug-smuggling boats since September to at least 148. An earlier strike earlier in the week killed 11 people, making it one of the deadliest attacks this year.

The operations are part of the Trump administration's expanded military campaign in the region to intercept alleged drug traffickers. US Southern Command, which oversees military operations in Latin America and the Caribbean, is now led by General Francis Donovan.

However, the legality of these strikes has drawn increasing scrutiny from lawmakers and legal experts. Some have raised concerns that the Pentagon is conducting extrajudicial killings and exercising what they characterize as "abuse of power with life-or-death consequences."

According to a statement from the American Civil Liberties Union, "Under both U.S. and international law, it is flagrantly illegal to use the military to kill civilians suspected only of crimes. Civilians, including those suspected of smuggling drugs, are not lawful targets."

The ACLU statement continued: "Just because the Trump administration says these strikes are firmly grounded in law doesn't make it true."

General Donovan took over leadership of US Southern Command following the abrupt retirement of Admiral Alvin Holsey, who reportedly left over disagreements concerning the strike policy.

The eastern Pacific has long served as a major transit route for cocaine and other narcotics moving from South American production areas toward Central America and Mexico. Drug trafficking organizations have increasingly turned to small boats and semi-submersible vessels to move cargo through the region.

The US military has expanded its role in counter-narcotics operations in recent years, working with the Coast Guard and partner nations to intercept suspected trafficking vessels. These operations have sometimes involved the use of force when boats fail to stop or are deemed to pose a threat.

Southern Command did not provide additional operational details about Friday's engagement, including when precisely the strike occurred.
