Microsoft Supports Anthropic's Legal Challenge Against Pentagon

Microsoft has filed a court brief backing Anthropic's legal challenge against a Pentagon designation that has effectively blocked the AI company from government work. The filing, submitted to federal court in San Francisco this week, argues that a temporary restraining order is necessary to prevent disruption to suppliers whose products depend on Anthropic's technology.

Google, Amazon, Apple and OpenAI have also signed onto a brief supporting Anthropic. In a statement, Microsoft said: "The Department of War needs reliable access to the country's best technology. And everyone wants to ensure AI is not used for mass domestic surveillance or to start a war without human control. The government, the entire tech sector, and the American public need a path to achieve all these goals together."

Anthropic launched two lawsuits Monday, one in federal court in California and one in the DC circuit court of appeals, challenging the Pentagon's decision to label the company a supply-chain risk. This designation has never before been applied to a US company. The dispute emerged after contract negotiations collapsed last month over a $200 million deal to deploy Anthropic's AI on classified military systems. Anthropic insisted its technology should not be used for mass surveillance of US citizens or to power autonomous lethal weapons. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth then designated the company a supply-chain risk, and Anthropic says its government contracts have already begun to be cancelled.

Pentagon chief technology officer Emil Michael told CNBC on Thursday that "there's no chance" the agency will renegotiate with Anthropic after the designation.

In its legal complaint, Anthropic explained the reasoning behind its restrictions. "Anthropic currently does not have confidence, for example, that Claude would function reliably or safely if used to support lethal autonomous warfare," the filing stated. "These usage restrictions are therefore rooted in Anthropic's unique understanding of Claude's risks and limitations."

The company also argued that its First Amendment rights were under attack, contending that the Pentagon used the supply-chain risk designation, typically reserved for firms with ties to foreign adversaries like China, as ideological punishment for its public stance on AI safety.

Microsoft is one of the Pentagon's most deeply embedded tech partners. The company holds a share of the military's $9 billion Joint Warfighting Cloud Capability contract alongside Amazon, Google and Oracle, plus separate software and enterprise services deals worth several billion dollars more. Under the Trump administration in September, Microsoft struck another multibillion-dollar deal to help advance cloud services and AI in the federal government.

Meanwhile, House Democrats have pressed the Pentagon for information about whether artificial intelligence, including the Maven Smart System, was used to identify targets in recent military strikes in Iran that have resulted in civilian casualties.

Zelensky proposes direct talks with Putin to end war

1:20 AM ET

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky has written an open letter directly to Russian President Vladimir Putin, calling for face-to-face negotiations to end the ongoing conflict. The letter marks the first time Zelensky has publicly addressed Putin in writing since Russia's full-scale invasion began in 2022, and represents a significant diplomatic gesture toward direct engagement between the two leaders.

In the letter, Zelensky argues that only direct talks between Ukraine and Russia can resolve the war. He contends that while Ukrainian resilience remains strong, most Russians have grown weary of the conflict and are ready for peace. The proposal suggests holding talks in a neutral third country.

The timing of Zelensky's initiative coincides with shifting diplomatic priorities in Washington. With the United States focusing its efforts on Iran rather than Ukraine, Kyiv appears to be taking a more independent approach to seeking resolution directly with Moscow, rather than relying primarily on Western mediation.

The Kremlin has not yet publicly responded to Zelensky's proposal. Previous high-level negotiations between Ukrainian and Russian leaders have achieved limited results, with both sides maintaining fundamentally incompatible positions on territorial control and security guarantees.

Any potential meeting would represent the most significant direct diplomatic contact between the two leaders since the war began. However, analysts say a breakthrough remains unlikely given current circumstances. Both armies show signs of exhaustion while sustaining heavy casualties, and the conflict continues to devastate Ukraine's energy infrastructure through ongoing strikes.

The prospects for productive talks face substantial obstacles. The Kremlin continues to demand that Ukraine withdraw its troops from the Donbas region as a precondition for negotiations. Ukraine has flatly rejected any suggestion that it unilaterally withdraw from its own territory, a position strengthened by recent battlefield dynamics in which Russian advances have largely stalled.

Germany's defence minister, Boris Pistorius, expressed skepticism about Putin's apparent interest in peace discussions. He suggested the Russian leader may be using peace rhetoric as a strategic deception, noting that if Putin genuinely wanted to end the conflict, he could do so himself rather than setting conditions. Pistorius characterized such statements as part of Putin's broader hybrid war strategy aimed at diverting attention from Russian weaknesses.

A US-brokered ceasefire is set to expire, and Moscow has rejected extending the truce. Meanwhile, European Union leaders have sought to secure a role in any future substantive peace negotiations, insisting that Ukraine must remain involved in all discussions and decisions about its own future.

Success in any talks would depend on whether both leaders prove willing to make significant concessions on core issues including territorial control, security arrangements, and Ukraine's international alignment. Given the heavy investments both sides have made in military operations and their opposing positions, the path forward remains highly uncertain.

Yesterday, 10:35 PM ET

The House of Representatives approved legislation providing aid to Ukraine and imposing sanctions on key segments of the Russian economy in a 226-195 vote on Thursday, marking the second major foreign policy break with Donald Trump in a week.

The vote represents significant congressional pushback against Trump's approach to the war in Ukraine. Republican leaders, aligned with Trump's position, had warned members that the bill would undermine negotiations they believed could achieve comparable but stronger results. Despite these objections, enough Republican lawmakers joined Democrats to pass the measure.

The passage comes just one day after the House voted for the first time to approve a war powers resolution aimed at halting U.S. military action against Iran. Together, these votes signal congressional impatience with Trump's foreign policy direction and suggest limits to his influence over the Republican caucus on international matters.

The legislation addresses Ukraine's ongoing defense needs as the conflict continues. The sanctions component targets Russian economic interests in response to the invasion. Supporters of the bill have argued that aiding Ukraine serves American national security interests by opposing Russian territorial expansion and supporting a democratic ally against authoritarian aggression.

The debate reflects divisions within the Republican Party over U.S. foreign policy commitments. Some GOP members advocate for reducing American involvement abroad, aligning with Trump's position, while others maintain that supporting Ukraine against Russia remains strategically important. The successful passage of Thursday's bill demonstrates that a working coalition exists in the House to advance Ukraine assistance regardless of Trump's opposition.

The vote also arrives as Europe takes parallel steps to support Ukraine. The European Union recently completed a 90 billion euro loan agreement for Ukraine after Hungary lifted its veto, and the bloc finalized its 20th sanctions package against Russia. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen stated that while "Russia doubles down on its aggression, we are doubling down on our support to the brave Ukrainian nation."

The U.S. House legislation now moves to the Senate for consideration, where its fate remains uncertain. Democratic lawmakers have expressed strong support for the measure, framing it as necessary to counter Russian aggression. The breadth and durability of the pro-Ukraine coalition in Congress will be tested as additional votes approach on related foreign policy matters.

The repeated congressional breaks with Trump on Ukraine and Iran policy suggest that despite his influence within the Republican Party, significant numbers of GOP representatives are willing to vote against his positions on major international issues. This pattern may shape the dynamics of foreign policy debates for the remainder of the congressional session.

1:47 AM ET

S&P Dow Jones Indices has declined to modify its rules to permit SpaceX early admission to its benchmark index, preventing the rocket company from joining the S&P 500 before its highly anticipated initial public offering. The decision upholds existing eligibility standards that typically require companies to be publicly traded for a specified period before consideration for index inclusion.

SpaceX is preparing to launch what would be the largest initial public offering in history, seeking $75 billion in its public debut. The company has committed to its planned $135 per share IPO price and will not adjust this valuation. JPMorgan Chase chief executive Jamie Dimon is personally involved in pitching the offering to investors, with banks preparing to collect substantial fees from the historic deal.

Index funds had begun preparing for SpaceX's potential inclusion by allocating space in their portfolios for the company's shares. The S&P's decision to maintain standard rules means SpaceX must now meet conventional eligibility criteria before it can be added to the index. This development affects both the company and passive funds that track S&P indices.

Retail investors had anticipated a significant buying surge if index funds were required to purchase SpaceX shares following early index entry. That scenario has now been eliminated. Following the announcement, other space-related stocks experienced declines in overnight trading as the expected positive spillover effect from index-driven purchases failed to occur.

The company has established a dedicated website to reach retail investors ahead of next week's public offering. If successful, the IPO would value SpaceX at more than $200 billion. Industry observers expect the listing to increase Elon Musk's net worth past $1 trillion. The offering represents a significant test for public markets, which have seen relatively few mega-deals of comparable size in recent years.

The index provider's refusal to create an exception surprised some market participants who had expected SpaceX might receive special treatment given the unprecedented scale of its upcoming offering. However, S&P maintained that existing rules would apply without modification.

The decision underscores how even the largest and most prominent companies must follow standard procedures for index inclusion rather than receive preferential treatment. SpaceX will need to establish a track record as a publicly traded company before becoming eligible for S&P 500 membership.

Industry analysts note that the ruling provides clarity to market participants regarding index methodology and demonstrates that major index providers will not adjust their established criteria, regardless of deal size or company prominence. The SpaceX IPO proceeds as scheduled next week without the added boost that early S&P 500 inclusion would have provided.

The Los Angeles Police Department is continuing its investigation into the fatal stabbing. The department did not provide additional details about the circumstances that led to the stabbing or the nature of the relationship between Handy and Gledhill beyond their connection through Gledhill's mother.

Handy's death marks the end of a career spanning nearly five decades in entertainment. House committee passes $1.15 trillion defense bill after 14-hour session

The House of Representatives voted 312-112 on Wednesday to approve a sweeping defense policy bill that authorizes $900 billion in military programs for the coming year. The 3,000-page legislation, known as the National Defense Authorization Act, includes significant provisions for troop compensation, weapons procurement reforms, and continued support for Ukraine. The bill prov...

1:11 AM ET

The House of Representatives voted 312-112 on Wednesday to approve a sweeping defense policy bill that authorizes $900 billion in military programs for the coming year. The 3,000-page legislation, known as the National Defense Authorization Act, includes significant provisions for troop compensation, weapons procurement reforms, and continued support for Ukraine.

The bill provides a 3.8 percent pay raise for many military members and funds improvements to military housing and facilities. It also overhauls how the Department of Defense purchases weapons, emphasizing speed after years of delays from the defense industry.

The legislation maintains a strong U.S. military presence in Europe by requiring the Pentagon to keep at least 76,000 troops and major equipment stationed there unless NATO allies are consulted. The bill also sets a minimum requirement of 28,500 troops in South Korea. Additionally, it authorizes $400 million for each of the next two years to manufacture weapons for Ukraine, demonstrating congressional commitment to supporting the country against Russian aggression.

The bill includes a controversial provision regarding boat strikes in the Caribbean targeting suspected drug smugglers. It demands that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth provide Congress with full, unedited video of a September strike near Venezuela that killed survivors of an earlier strike. The legislation withholds part of Hegseth's travel budget until the Pentagon releases this footage and allows Congress to review the orders for the strikes.

The measure makes significant cuts aligned with the Trump administration's priorities. It reduces climate-related spending by $1.6 billion, reversing years of military investment in addressing climate change as a national security threat. The bill also cuts $40 million in funding for diversity, equity, and inclusion programs.

Congress is formally ending the war in Iraq by repealing the 2003 authorization for the invasion. Supporters say the repeal prevents future abuses and reinforces that Iraq is now a strategic partner. The legislation also permanently removes U.S. sanctions on Syria imposed in 2019, allowing international companies to invest in reconstruction efforts under the new Syrian government.

The bill strikes bipartisan compromises on several issues. While cutting climate and diversity efforts, it boosts congressional oversight of the Pentagon and includes provisions to keep U.S. troops stationed abroad. However, the legislation faced criticism from some senators regarding military flight restrictions over Washington following a deadly midair collision earlier this year between an Army helicopter and a commercial jetliner near Ronald Reagan National Airport.

Democrats criticized Republican House Speaker Mike Johnson for stripping a provision to expand in vitro fertilization coverage for active duty military personnel.

The White House has signaled support for the legislation, which now heads to the Senate for consideration before lawmakers depart for the holiday break.

The British Heart Foundation announced plans to close 150 of its charity shops across the United Kingdom. The organization cited what it called an exceptionally challenging trading environment as the reason for the widespread closures.

The decision represents a significant contraction for one of Britain's largest charity retail operations. The foundation's stores have been a fixture on British high streets for decades, selling donated goods to fund cardiovascular research and support programs.

Trump figures prominently in New York congressional debate

Four Democratic candidates competing to replace Rep. Jerry Nadler frequently referenced the president during their debate.

Yesterday, 9:39 PM ET

President Trump's name came up repeatedly during Thursday's debate among Democratic candidates seeking to represent New York's 12th Congressional District. The four contenders—lawyer George Conway, Jack Schlossberg, and state Assemblymembers Alex Bores and Micah Lasher—frequently invoked the president as they discussed their positions.

The candidates are competing for the Democratic nomination to replace Rep. Jerry Nadler in the seat. The debate aired on a local network as the four Democrats laid out their platforms.
