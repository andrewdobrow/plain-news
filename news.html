The Trump administration has threatened local and state election officials with arrest and is withholding millions of dollars in federal police and anti-terrorism funding from jurisdictions that refuse to implement new voting rules, according to legal challenges filed across the country.

The White House is conditioning federal grants for public safety and counterterrorism programs on adoption of specific voting procedures. By linking law enforcement funding to election policy compliance, the administration is forcing officials to choose between maintaining police operations and preserving their current election administration practices.

Harris County, Texas Attorney Abbie Kamin characterized the effort as an attempt to defund public safety and counterterrorism programs. The controversial voting rules the Trump administration is demanding include transitioning to paper ballots and implementing proof of citizenship requirements.

Two lawsuits have been filed by state and local governments accusing the administration of unconstitutionally imposing election policies that Congress has not approved. Officials argue the administration lacks authority to unilaterally attach voting requirements as conditions for law enforcement funding, marking an escalation in federal-state tensions over election administration.

Private voting rights attorneys and Democratic attorneys general are mobilizing to defend election officials and prepare additional legal challenges against the federal government. These legal strategies represent a significant conflict between the federal government and local jurisdictions over the proper scope of federal power.

The funding restrictions represent a dramatic shift in how federal money is being deployed. Rather than simply proposing new election policies through Congress, the Trump administration is using federal dollars as leverage to advance voting procedures at the state and local level. Affected jurisdictions have resisted what they view as an inappropriate use of federal spending power to circumvent the legislative process.

The disputes are emerging as a significant flashpoint, with both parties preparing for potential legal battles over election procedures in multiple states. Legal experts involved in the cases argue that conditioning federal law enforcement funding on election policy compliance exceeds the administration's constitutional authority and represents an improper use of federal spending power.

Officials in affected jurisdictions say the move essentially blackmails them into choosing between public safety funding and their current election practices. Local governments across the country that have refused to comply with the administration's voting requirements are facing the prospect of losing substantial federal police and anti-terrorism grants.

The conflict highlights growing tensions between the Trump administration's election policy ambitions and its constitutional authority to implement those policies. By withholding federal funding rather than pursuing congressional legislation, the administration is attempting an alternative pathway to achieve voting changes that have faced resistance through traditional legislative channels.

5:01 AM ET

Rising electricity costs during the summer heat have triggered protests and power disconnections in cities across the United States. The price surge comes as households run air conditioners and fans continuously to cope with high temperatures. In Tulsa, Oklahoma, utility companies have begun cutting power to residents unable to pay their bills, a practice that has drawn criticism from consumer advocates.

The electricity price increases affect households nationwide as demand peaks during the hottest months of the year. Air conditioning units and cooling systems consume significantly more power during summer, driving up both usage and costs per kilowatt-hour. The combination of increased consumption and higher rates has left many families struggling to afford basic cooling.

In Tulsa, disconnections have become common as residents fall behind on payments. The practice of cutting power during extreme heat raises safety concerns, particularly for vulnerable populations including elderly residents and young children. Some cities have implemented temporary moratoriums on disconnections during heat waves, but these protections vary widely by jurisdiction.

Consumer groups have organized protests in several cities, demanding rate relief and protections against disconnections during dangerous heat conditions. The protests have focused on utility company policies and the lack of affordable options for low-income households. Advocates argue that access to electricity for cooling should be treated as a public health necessity during extreme weather.

The situation highlights the growing challenge of energy affordability as climate patterns shift and cooling becomes essential for longer periods each year. Summer heat waves place enormous strain on electricity systems. When temperatures soar, power generation capacity often declines at the same time demand spikes. Gas plants can struggle to operate efficiently in extreme heat, and renewable energy sources face their own constraints. Wind power generation decreases in high-pressure weather systems, while solar panels lose efficiency when temperatures rise too much.

These supply-side pressures combine with skyrocketing consumer demand to push electricity prices to record levels. The result is a crisis that extends beyond individual cities, affecting communities throughout regions experiencing prolonged heat.

While some states have assistance programs to help low-income residents pay utility bills, funding often falls short of demand. The disconnect between rising costs and household incomes has created hardship for millions of Americans. As climate change intensifies heat waves and extends cooling seasons, electricity affordability has become an increasingly urgent concern for vulnerable populations and policymakers alike.

The challenge facing households and utility systems underscores the need for solutions that balance energy demands with affordability and public health protections during extreme weather events.

5:04 AM ET

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh will deliver his first address at the central bank's annual Jackson Hole conference this week as anxiety grips the $30 trillion U.S. government bond market and inflation concerns resurface.

The timing presents a significant challenge for Warsh early in his tenure. Bond traders are watching closely for signals about the Fed's commitment to fighting inflation, particularly ahead of policy meetings scheduled for September and December. However, Warsh has indicated he may not use the traditional approach of signaling the Fed's next moves on interest rates.

Instead, Warsh has suggested his speech could focus on "bigger questions" such as productivity and demographics concerns, rather than the conventional policy hints expected at the Jackson Hole gathering. This departure from tradition comes as selling pressure intensifies in Treasury markets despite Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's recent announcement that the government would significantly increase its purchases of Treasury bonds to stabilize prices and lower yields.

Bessent's intervention effort has yielded limited results. While Treasury yields initially fell after his announcement, they quickly recovered. By Friday, the 10-year Treasury yield had returned to near its pre-announcement levels, with 30-year bond yields trading near their highest points in two decades.

The Jackson Hole conference, held in Grand Teton National Park, ranks as one of the financial calendar's most significant events, drawing the world's most powerful central bankers and economists. The annual gathering traditionally serves as a platform for major policy announcements and shifts in central bank thinking.

The current moment carries heightened significance given the economic pressures facing the U.S. economy. Bond market volatility and renewed inflation worries have created challenging conditions for Warsh as he seeks to establish his leadership of the Federal Reserve. The anxiety in financial markets reflects broader questions about whether the Fed will need to take more aggressive action to combat inflation while maintaining economic stability.

Warsh's remarks will be scrutinized not only for immediate policy implications but also for insights into his broader approach to monetary policy. His peers at the conference are expected to emphasize the challenges posed by global inflation dynamics, adding to the pressure surrounding his debut speech at the gathering.

The address comes at a moment when Warsh's initial honeymoon period as Fed chair appears to be ending. He must now navigate the real economic pressures and market anxieties that have emerged in recent weeks. How he addresses these concerns, and whether he maintains the traditional signaling approach or pursues his suggested focus on longer-term economic questions, could significantly influence financial market sentiment in the coming months.

According to her team, Parton died after "bravely facing a brief battle with cancer" at the Vanderbilt-Ingram Cancer Center in Nashville, surrounded by loved ones. In a recent interview, she had mentioned dealing with health issues that went unaddressed while caring for her husband of nearly 60 years, Carl Dean, who died last year. Despite her health challenges, she remained determined, telling People magazine: "I still have so many things I want to achieve, so I'm gonna keep on working as long as I can to see everything come to life."

Parton rose from poverty in Tennessee to become a rhinestone-studded cultural icon. She wrote approximately 3,000 songs, including massive hits like "Jolene," "I Will Always Love You," and "9 to 5." Beyond her music career, she became an accomplished businesswoman and philanthropist with ventures spanning from her Dollywood theme park to cosmetics, pet apparel, and truck stops.

Her charitable impact was substantial. The Dollywood Foundation distributes about 25 million books annually across the US, Canada, Australia, the UK and Ireland. She also donated one million dollars for COVID-19 vaccine research, which became foundational to the development of the Moderna vaccine.

The news prompted an outpouring of tributes from across music, film, and politics. Actor Jane Fonda wrote on Instagram that Parton "will always be with us because she was so present, so powerful, so generous and playful that she is woven into all our lives and hearts forever." Country star Reba McEntire shared: "Dolly, thank you for all the wonderful things you did for us here on earth. Your love, your music and you gave so much with your huge huge heart."

Tributes came from artists including Beyonce, Paul McCartney, Eminem, Mick Jagger, Tom Jones, Julia Roberts, Billy Ray Cyrus, Katy Perry, Elton John, and Christina Aguilera. Cyrus, whose daughter Miley is Parton's godchild, said he loved her "with my whole heart." Musician Pitbull performed "Powerful Women," a 2024 collaboration with Parton, and described her as "a good human being with a good heart" who showed him love when few others believed in him.

Kesha paid tribute by covering "Old Flames (Can't Hold a Candle to You)," a 1980 Parton hit that was co-written by Kesha's mother, Pebe Sebert, highlighting the multi-generational impact of Parton's work.

The White House lowered its flag to half-mast in her honor. Her funeral will be small and private, per her wishes.
