Federal Reserve to Announce First Rate Decision Under New Chairman Kevin Warsh

12:20 PM ET

The Federal Reserve will announce its latest interest rate decision Wednesday afternoon, marking the first policy meeting led by new Chairman Kevin Warsh. Traders and investors are closely monitoring the decision as Warsh makes his public debut in the role, with the announcement expected to provide fresh signals about the central bank's economic outlook and monetary policy direction.

Warsh faces significant attention from both markets and political observers during his inaugural meeting as Fed chair. The new chairman is expected to face persistent scrutiny from the president who nominated him, adding a political dimension to what is traditionally an independent monetary policy process. His leadership comes at a critical moment for the institution, as former Fed Chair Jerome Powell recently warned about threats to the central bank's independence.

Powell, who stepped down last month and remains on the Fed's board, cautioned that political interference in monetary policy could permanently damage public trust in the Federal Reserve. In a speech accepting the 2026 John F Kennedy Profile in Courage award, Powell emphasized that legal protections shielding the Fed from politics "have served the public well" across administrations of both parties. He argued that if any administration removes Fed officials over policy disagreements, future administrations will follow that precedent, ultimately undermining the institution's credibility.

Powell's comments come as the Supreme Court considers the constitutionality of the Trump administration's attempt to remove Fed Governor Lisa Cook. The administration cited alleged misconduct related to mortgage transactions, marking the first time in the Fed's history that a sitting president has attempted to remove a governor. A federal district judge blocked the firing, and both conservative and liberal justices signaled skepticism about the administration's position during oral arguments in January.

The Fed's decision comes as markets show mixed signals. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose to a record high Wednesday, while the S&P 500 and Nasdaq slipped as traders awaited the rate announcement. Oil prices ticked higher amid ongoing developments in global energy markets. The varied performance across different market segments reflects investor uncertainty about the Fed's next moves.

Warsh has indicated a particular focus on the Federal Reserve's balance sheet, a technical but consequential aspect of monetary policy that affects liquidity in financial markets. How the Fed manages its holdings of government bonds and other securities can influence longer-term interest rates and overall financial conditions.

The rate decision will directly affect borrowing costs for American consumers and businesses. Changes in the Fed's benchmark interest rate ripple through the economy, influencing mortgage rates, credit card interest, business loan costs, and savings account yields. The decision also carries implications for the dollar's value in foreign exchange markets and can affect international capital flows. Warsh's approach in his first meeting will set the tone for his leadership during a period of complex economic challenges and institutional pressure.

Justice Department challenges reparations program in Chicago suburb

10:34 AM ET

The Trump administration has taken legal action against a reparations program in Evanston, Illinois, a Chicago suburb that became the first municipality in the United States to distribute reparations payments to Black residents. The Department of Justice joined a lawsuit challenging the program, which provides $25,000 payments to eligible Black residents who experienced housing discrimination.

Evanston launched its reparations initiative to address historical housing discrimination against Black residents. The program aims to compensate individuals who lived in the city during periods when discriminatory housing policies were in effect or who are direct descendants of those residents. The city designed the payments as a remedy for documented patterns of segregation and unequal treatment in housing access.

Federal officials argue that the race-based payments themselves constitute illegal discrimination. The Justice Department contends that using racial criteria to determine eligibility for the program violates federal civil rights laws, even when the stated purpose is to address past discrimination. This position represents a significant shift in how the federal government approaches remedial programs designed to address historical injustices.

The legal challenge puts the administration at odds with local efforts to provide restitution for documented harm. Evanston officials structured their program around specific historical evidence of discriminatory practices that prevented Black families from building wealth through homeownership. The program drew national attention as other cities and states explored similar initiatives to address the lasting economic effects of slavery, segregation, and discriminatory policies.

The outcome of this case could affect reparations discussions across the country. Multiple municipalities and state legislatures have been studying or implementing various forms of reparations programs. A federal ruling against Evanston's program could halt or reshape these efforts, while a decision in the city's favor might provide a legal framework for other jurisdictions to follow.

The case raises fundamental questions about how governments can address historical discrimination and whether race-conscious remedies are permissible under current civil rights law. The Evanston reparations program has become a focal point in the broader national debate over how to remedy the effects of past racial injustices. Supporters argue that targeted payments are necessary to address documented harms, while critics contend that such programs themselves violate civil rights principles by using race as a determining factor.

The Trump administration's challenge signals its approach to civil rights issues and remedial programs. The decision to intervene in the Evanston case demonstrates the Justice Department's willingness to contest race-based initiatives, even those designed to compensate for acknowledged historical discrimination. As the legal proceedings continue, the case will likely influence how other jurisdictions approach reparations efforts and shape federal policy on remedying past discrimination.

Walter Parazaider, a co-founding member of the band Chicago and one of its core instrumentalists for five decades, died Wednesday. Family members announced his death, stating he had been suffering from Alzheimer's disease for six years. He was 81.

Parazaider played reed instruments including flute and saxophone for Chicago from the band's formation in 1967 until his retirement in 2017. His contributions spanned the entirety of the group's commercial peak and evolution from a jazz-rock fusion ensemble into one of the most successful American rock bands of the 1970s and 1980s. Chicago's string of hits, including 25 top 40 singles and multiple platinum albums, made the band one of the highest-selling groups in music history.

The multi-instrumentalist's distinctive horn arrangements became a signature element of Chicago's sound. His work helped define the brass rock genre that the band pioneered, blending jazz sophistication with rock accessibility. Parazaider performed on albums that sold more than 100 million copies worldwide during his tenure with the group.

Born in Chicago, Parazaider met several future bandmates while attending DePaul University in the mid-1960s. The group originally performed as The Big Thing and Chicago Transit Authority before shortening their name to Chicago. Parazaider remained with the band through lineup changes, shifting musical trends, and the death of original guitarist Terry Kath in 1978. He continued touring and recording until 2017, when he retired after 50 years as a performing member.

Parazaider is survived by his family members, who requested privacy following the announcement. The band has not yet issued a public statement regarding his death or plans for any memorial tributes.

The United States and Iran have reached a memorandum of understanding that brings an immediate end to military operations across all fronts, including Lebanon. Both nations plan to sign the agreement on Friday, committing themselves to halt warfare and refrain from initiating armed conflict or threatening force against each other going forward.

President Trump discussed the preliminary agreement at a press conference during the Group of Seven summit in France. He emphasized that the document is not final and warned that the US would resume bombing Iran if the Islamic Republic violates its terms. Trump noted that the deal addresses reopening the Strait of Hormuz, a critical shipping passage that had been closed during the conflict. According to Trump, oil reserves could have depleted within four weeks had the strait remained closed, a situation he described as potentially catastrophic.

Trump defended the agreement's allowance for Iran to keep its ballistic missile arsenal, saying the US would work with Gulf allies on related security concerns. He estimated that military operations inflicted up to two trillion dollars in damage on Iran. When asked about the deal's likelihood of success, Trump joked that he would claim credit if it succeeds but blame Vice President Vance if it fails.

The memorandum represents a significant shift in US policy following sustained military conflict between the two nations and their respective allies. The fighting began on February 28 after Trump ordered Operation Epic Fury and notified Congress accordingly. On April 7, Trump ordered a two-week ceasefire, which has since been extended. Trump stated in a letter to congressional leaders that there had been no exchange of fire between US forces and Iran since April 7 and that hostilities have terminated.

Trump dismissed concerns about the War Powers Act of 1973, which requires presidential notification to Congress before military operations and congressional approval within 60 days to continue them. He told reporters he had no intention of seeking congressional approval, suggesting the law had never been invoked before and questioning its constitutionality.

Democratic lawmakers challenged Trump's interpretation. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer called it an "illegal war," while Senator Jeanne Shaheen noted that tens of thousands of US service members remained in harm's way and that the Strait of Hormuz remained closed with prices skyrocketing. The American Civil Liberties Union expressed "profound concern" about what it called an illegal war, stating that the War Powers Resolution contains no pause button.

Senate Republicans blocked a war powers resolution brought by Democrats aimed at ending the conflict. Despite Trump's previous claims the war would end "very soon," his letter acknowledged that the threat posed by Iran to the US and its armed forces remained significant, and the Pentagon would continue adjusting its force posture across the region as necessary.

12:06 PM ET

The Federal Reserve's interest rate decisions have immediate consequences for American consumers' financial lives. When the Fed adjusts its benchmark rate, the effects cascade through the economy, influencing the cost of mortgages, auto loans, credit card balances, and the returns on savings accounts.

Higher interest rates generally increase borrowing costs for consumers seeking loans while providing better returns for savers. Lower rates make borrowing cheaper but reduce the interest earned on deposits. The direction and magnitude of rate changes affect household budgets and financial planning decisions across the country.
