A few minutes ago

The Social Security retirement trust fund is now expected to be depleted by 2032, one year earlier than previous estimates, according to new projections. Without congressional action, the program that supports roughly 68 million Americans could face automatic benefit cuts averaging 22 percent when the fund runs out.

The accelerated timeline represents a significant shift in the financial outlook for the program. Social Security has long faced funding challenges as the ratio of workers paying into the system to retirees drawing benefits continues to shrink. The trust fund has been drawing down its reserves to cover the gap between incoming payroll tax revenue and outgoing benefit payments.

The projected depletion does not mean Social Security would stop paying benefits entirely in 2032. Even after the trust fund is exhausted, the program would continue to collect payroll taxes from current workers. However, those incoming revenues would only be sufficient to pay about 78 percent of scheduled benefits, resulting in across-the-board cuts unless Congress intervenes.

The program faces structural challenges that have been building for decades. The retirement of the baby boom generation has increased the number of beneficiaries, while birth rates have declined and life expectancy has increased. These demographic trends mean fewer workers are supporting each retiree, straining the system's finances. The COVID-19 pandemic and its economic effects also reduced payroll tax collections, further pressuring the trust fund.

Congress has several options to address the shortfall, including raising the payroll tax rate, increasing the cap on earnings subject to Social Security taxes, raising the retirement age, or reducing benefits for higher earners. Each approach involves difficult tradeoffs. Previous efforts to reform Social Security have proven politically difficult, as the program remains widely popular and any changes affect millions of Americans. With the depletion date now less than six years away, pressure is mounting for lawmakers to act before automatic cuts take effect.

Serena Williams returned to professional tennis after 1,375 days away from the sport, winning her doubles match at Queen's Club in London. The 44-year-old and partner Victoria Mboko defeated third seeds Nicole Melichar-Martinez and Erin Routliffe 7-6 (2), 6-2 to advance to the second round.

Williams last competed professionally in September 2022. Her comeback at Queen's marks her first appearance on a competitive tennis court in nearly four years. The unseeded pair claimed the opening set in a tiebreak before dominating the second set.

Williams chose to make her return in doubles rather than singles, suggesting a gradual approach to competitive play. She has committed to playing doubles in Berlin after Queen's, though her plans beyond that remain uncertain. When asked about returning to singles competition, Williams said she could not commit either way but added "right now, no."

The 23-time Grand Slam singles champion explained her motivation for returning. "I don't need to win. I've won more than most people have in their whole lives, so for me it's not important," Williams said. "I don't have anything to prove, I don't have anything to lose and everything here is just a gain."

Williams emphasized that her return is about family and personal fulfillment rather than competitive necessity. "It's really about my kids getting to see me play," she said, referencing her daughters Olympia, aged eight, and Adira, aged two. "An athlete is the best thing that you can be in the highest place, and having an opportunity to still be able to possibly do that one last time is kind of cool and exciting."

She selected Mboko, the world number 9 in singles, as her doubles partner after being impressed by the 19-year-old's performance in Montreal. Williams said Mboko "reminded me a lot of myself" and praised her consistency and drive.

For Mboko, the partnership proved meaningful. She was only 10 years old when Williams won her last Grand Slam title. "Growing up, Serena has always been my idol," Mboko said. "It was really hard not to like her because she won every tournament she played. Seeing someone on TV versus actually talking to them in person is very different. I think she's really nice and very personable."

Queen's Club on grass serves as the traditional warm-up period before Wimbledon. Williams has won seven Wimbledon singles titles on grass. Fellow competitor Emma Raducanu, who faced Williams in the American's penultimate tournament in 2022, welcomed her return. "It's really great to see her back," Raducanu said. "She's the greatest female tennis player of all time. It's just amazing to have her part of the tour again."

James Blood Ulmer, the US guitarist celebrated for his avant-garde fusion of jazz, blues and funk, has died at 86. He passed away on June 3, according to a statement from his family, who described him as fearless in both his music and spirit.

Born Willie James Ulmer in South Carolina in 1940, Ulmer began his music career in funk bands, moving between Pittsburgh, Columbus and Detroit while backing musicians including Jewel Bryner and Hank Marr. In the early 1970s, he relocated to New York and made a dramatic artistic shift. "I ain't never thought nobody could make no money playing free music," he later reflected. "So I always played structured blues, rhythm playing, dance music, or something like that. And I abandoned it! When I came to New York, it was like ... I just went totally another way."

In New York, Ulmer played with jazz luminaries including Art Blakey, Joe Henderson and Rashied Ali. More significantly, he was mentored by saxophonist Ornette Coleman, who introduced him to harmolodic theory, an approach that avoided traditional keys and harmonics in favor of greater freedom in sound. This philosophy shaped Ulmer's entire career, characterized by instinctive, unbounded playing even as he developed his songwriting abilities.

Coleman co-produced Ulmer's debut album, Tales of Captain Black. His next release, Are You Glad to Be in America?, came out on the UK's Rough Trade label. The title track became a signature song with its spirited social commentary, and Ulmer subsequently supported punk and rock bands including Public Image Ltd and Captain Beefheart. He demanded focus from audiences at these performances, telling them to "shut the fuck up" and give him five minutes to win them over or leave.

Collaborations with saxophonist Arthur Blythe on albums including Lenox Avenue Breakdown and Illusions led to Ulmer's own record deal with Columbia. Between 1981 and 1983, he released three progressive albums: Free Lancing, Black Rock and Odyssey. Rolling Stone hailed him after Free Lancing as "the most original electric guitarist to emerge since the late Jimi Hendrix," praising his pairing of virtuosic bluesy guitar with tight funk arrangements and soulful vocals.

Ulmer formed the Music Revelation Ensemble with saxophonist David Murray, bassist Amin Ali and drummer Ronald Shannon Jackson, debuting with No Wave in 1980 and eventually releasing seven albums. He also formed the group Phalanx and continued recording through subsequent decades, shifting focus toward blues while maintaining his distinctive approach. His 2001 album Memphis Blood: The Sun Sessions earned his only Grammy nomination for best traditional blues album.

Ulmer retired in 2024 after performing at the Detroit jazz festival, ending a career that fundamentally reshaped how guitarists approached fusion between avant-garde and popular music forms.

Supreme Court to rule this week on mail-in ballot deadlines

A few minutes ago

The Supreme Court has revived a lawsuit challenging state laws that allow mail-in ballots to be counted after Election Day, potentially affecting how 16 states and several U.S. territories handle late-arriving votes.

The case centers on an Illinois law that permits ballots postmarked by Election Day to be counted if received up to two weeks afterward. Republican congressman Mike Bost filed the original lawsuit, arguing that this practice unconstitutionally extends the election period. The Supreme Court ruled 7-2 to allow his case to proceed, with Chief Justice John Roberts writing the majority opinion.

Roberts stated that political candidates have a legitimate interest in challenging voting rules that govern their elections. "Candidates have a concrete and particularized interest in the rules that govern the counting of votes in their elections, regardless whether those rules harm their electoral prospects or increase the cost of their campaigns," he wrote. He further noted that this interest extends to "the integrity of the election and the democratic process."

Two liberal justices dissented. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, joined by Justice Sonia Sotomayor, argued that candidates should face the same legal standards as other plaintiffs before bringing lawsuits. Jackson warned that creating special rules for candidates could destabilize both election law and electoral processes nationwide.

The case previously failed in lower courts, which determined that Bost had not suffered sufficient injury to sue. His legal team appealed, arguing that the costs of maintaining campaign operations beyond Election Day constitute financial harm.

The practical implications are significant. Sixteen states, along with Guam, Puerto Rico, the Virgin Islands, and Washington D.C., currently accept ballots that arrive after Election Day if properly postmarked. Four states that mail ballots to all voters—Washington, Oregon, California, and Nevada—also count these late arrivals.

The case has become a focal point in Republican efforts to challenge mail-in voting procedures. The lawsuit aligns with broader conservative criticism of mail-in ballot policies, which has been a central component of recent political discourse. President Trump issued an executive order in March directing the attorney general to take action against states that count ballots received after Election Day.

Civil rights organizations including the Brennan Center, American Civil Liberties Union, and League of Women Voters have already filed lawsuits challenging the executive order.

Oral arguments in October focused narrowly on whether a federal candidate has standing to challenge the law, rather than on the constitutionality of mail-in ballot grace periods themselves. The justices questioned whether candidates should be treated as direct targets of election regulations and whether the likelihood of mail-in ballots affecting an election outcome should determine standing.

The Supreme Court's decision allows the Illinois case to proceed to the merits phase, where justices will eventually address whether the practice itself violates the Constitution.

More significantly, he was mentored by saxophonist Ornette Coleman, who introduced him to harmolodic theory, an approach that avoided traditional keys and harmonics in favor of greater freedom in sound. This philosophy shaped Ulmer's entire career, characterized by instinctive, unbounded playing even as he developed his songwriting abilities.

Coleman co-produced Ulmer's debut album, Tales of Captain Black. His next release, Are You Glad to Be in America?, came out on the UK's Rough Trade label. The title track became a signature song with its spirited social commentary, and Ulmer subsequently supported punk and rock bands including Public Image Ltd and Captain Beefheart. Rolling Stone hailed him after Free Lancing as "the most original electric guitarist to emerge since the late Jimi Hendrix," praising his pairing of virtuosic bluesy guitar with tight funk arrangements and soulful vocals.

Ulmer formed the Music Revelation Ensemble with saxophonist David Murray, bassist Amin Ali and drummer Ronald Shannon Jackson, debuting with No Wave in 1980 and eventually releasing seven albums. He also formed the group Phalanx and continued recording through subsequent decades, shifting focus toward blues while maintaining his distinctive approach. His 2001 album Memphis Blood: The Sun Sessions earned his only Grammy nomination for best traditional blues album.

Ulmer retired in 2024 after performing at the Detroit jazz festival, ending a career that fundamentally reshaped how guitarists approached fusion between avant-garde and popular music forms.
