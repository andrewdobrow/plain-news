President Trump has proposed that the U.S. government acquire small ownership stakes in major artificial intelligence companies, allowing the American public to benefit financially from what he expects will become trillion-dollar enterprises.

"There's something very interesting about it, where it almost becomes a partnership with the American public," Trump said to reporters aboard Air Force One. "It's like you make them [partners] in this revolution. It would be a beautiful thing. ... It would make 'em rich."

Trump indicated he has raised the concept during recent meetings with AI company leaders, describing their reactions as surprised. The idea would represent a significant shift from traditional government-industry relationships, establishing direct financial connections between federal authorities and private technology firms developing artificial intelligence.

The proposal aligns with existing advocacy from influential figures in the tech sector. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman has previously championed similar arrangements. This week, Senator Bernie Sanders revived the discussion by proposing that the public receive a direct ownership stake in major AI companies through a one-time 50 percent tax paid in stock.

The concept raises several unresolved questions about practical implementation. Details remain unclear regarding how ownership stakes would be structured, what percentage of companies the government would acquire, and the mechanisms for distributing any resulting profits to taxpayers. Trump has not specified whether he would pursue the idea through executive action or request congressional approval.

The timing of Trump's suggestion comes as artificial intelligence companies have attracted enormous investment and as policymakers grapple with regulating the rapidly advancing technology. The notion of federal ownership in private AI firms introduces potential complications, particularly regarding the government's multiple roles as investor, regulator, and national security overseer of the industry.

Federal ownership stakes could create significant conflicts of interest. The government would need to balance its financial interests in company growth and profitability against its regulatory responsibilities and obligations to protect national security. These competing interests might affect policy decisions related to AI development, safety standards, and international competitiveness.

The proposal also raises governance questions about how the government would exercise its ownership stake and whether federal representatives would serve on company boards. Such involvement could influence strategic decisions at some of the world's most important technology companies.

Industry observers note that the concept differs from traditional government support mechanisms like grants or tax incentives. It would tie public finances directly to the success of specific private companies, creating unprecedented arrangements in the technology sector.

As AI technology continues advancing rapidly and companies in the space command increasing valuations, the conversation about how the public should benefit from this growth appears likely to intensify. Trump's public endorsement of the idea suggests it may receive serious consideration within his administration going forward.

7:50 AM ET

A flesh-eating parasite that had not been confirmed in the United States for nearly 60 years has been found in a calf in south Texas, about 50 miles from the Mexico border. The discovery of the New World screwworm fly has triggered an emergency response from federal and state agricultural authorities and prompted neighboring countries to restrict cattle imports.

Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins announced the detection late on Wednesday, marking the first confirmed case in Texas since 1966. The parasite, whose larvae feed on the blood and tissue of warm-blooded animals, was eradicated from the United States in the 1970s after causing tens of millions of dollars in losses. Federal and Texas officials had warned livestock owners for more than a year about the parasite's progression through Mexico, where it advanced more than 1,100 miles northward.

The discovery has raised significant concerns for the cattle industry at a time when beef prices are at record highs. While the flies pose no direct threat to food safety, officials worry that an established population could cause shortages if the infestation spreads among cattle and poultry operations. A second infected calf was subsequently identified, and Texas declared a state of disaster over the outbreak.

Canada has responded by banning cattle imports from Texas in response to the outbreak. The movement restrictions reflect concerns about the parasite's potential to establish itself in North America if containment efforts fail.

Rollins stated that "there is no threat of mass infestation" and expressed confidence that the detection would not lead to widespread establishment of the pest in the country. However, Texas Agriculture Commissioner Sid Miller criticized the USDA's response strategy, noting that despite the release of billions of sterile flies, the screwworm had continued advancing toward Texas. Miller called for increased deployment of the screwworm adult suppression system, which uses attractants, bait, and targeted insecticides to reduce adult fly populations before sterile fly releases complete the eradication effort.

The USDA is leading containment efforts with support from the Army Corps of Engineers. In March, the agency announced construction of a new sterile fly production facility at Moore Airbase in Edinburg, Texas, to boost the response. The sterile fly approach has proven effective in previous eradication campaigns, as female screwworm flies mate only once in their lifetime, and eggs from sterile males do not hatch.

This detection comes after an isolated case confirmed in a Maryland resident who had traveled to El Salvador in August 2025, though that individual recovered with no further transmission. The last significant outbreak occurred in the Florida Keys in September 2016, mostly affecting wild deer, and was contained early the following year without spreading further.

4:50 AM ET

A machine learning system has identified more than 800 previously undocumented objects hidden within 35 years of images captured by the Hubble Space Telescope, revealing that one of astronomy's most celebrated archives still contains undiscovered phenomena waiting to be found.

The artificial intelligence system processed the vast collection of Hubble observations accumulated since the telescope's launch in 1990. These newly identified objects were present in images that astronomers had already examined, indicating that traditional human analysis had overlooked them despite previous reviews.

The Hubble archive contains millions of observations gathered across more than three decades of continuous operation. While professional astronomers have studied numerous images extensively, the sheer volume of data makes it impractical for researchers to manually review everything comprehensively. Machine learning systems can analyze images at speeds far exceeding human capabilities and can identify patterns or unusual features that might be missed during conventional examination.

The discovery demonstrates the lasting scientific importance of long-term space missions and highlights how artificial intelligence can extract previously hidden information from existing datasets. Throughout its operational lifetime, Hubble has captured imagery of distant galaxies, nebulae, star clusters, and various other celestial objects and phenomena. Some of the newly discovered objects may have remained unidentified in the archive for years or even decades before the AI system brought them to light.

The findings suggest that other major astronomical collections may similarly harbor undiscovered objects. Space agencies and research organizations maintain extensive archives of images from multiple telescopes and space missions. Implementing comparable AI techniques across these diverse datasets could uncover additional objects and phenomena that have remained hidden despite supposedly thorough earlier analysis.

This work illustrates a broader trend in modern astronomy where machine learning augments human expertise rather than replacing it. The combination of human scientific knowledge and artificial intelligence's pattern recognition abilities creates powerful tools for exploring massive volumes of scientific data. As telescopes continue operating and generating new observations, and as archives from past missions remain scientifically valuable, AI systems will increasingly help researchers identify discoveries that might otherwise escape notice.

The application of these techniques could accelerate the pace of astronomical discovery and help maximize the scientific return from expensive space missions. It also suggests that many important findings may still be waiting within data that scientists have already collected, offering research value that extends far beyond the original mission objectives.

Anthony Head, the acclaimed British actor best known for playing Rupert Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, has died at age 72. He passed away peacefully from complications related to pneumonia, surrounded by his family, according to a statement from his daughters Emily and Daisy Head.

"It has been, and forever will be, an honour and a privilege to be his daughters, and to have witnessed first-hand the impact both he and his work have had on so many," they said.

Head's portrayal of Giles, the school librarian and mentor to Sarah Michelle Gellar's title character, became iconic in television history. The role established him as a respected figure in both British and American entertainment. After the show ended in 2003, Head continued working steadily across multiple projects and genres.

Beyond Buffy, Head's career demonstrated remarkable versatility. He appeared in the BBC sketch comedy series Little Britain, where he played a prime minister constantly fawned over by a flirtatious assistant. He also took roles in the fantasy series Merlin and more recently featured in the Apple TV+ series Ted Lasso, where he played former football club owner Rupert Mannion and developed praised on-screen chemistry with co-creator Jason Sudeikis.

Head's earlier work included film roles in The Iron Lady and The Inbetweeners Movie. He also gained significant public recognition in the 1980s through a long-running series of Nescafe Gold Blend television advertisements that followed the slow-burn romance between two characters.

Before achieving international fame, Head built a strong stage career. He starred in the 1978 West End revival of Godspell alongside Su Pollard and later performed in productions including the original West End production of the Abba musical Chess in 1988, where he played Freddie Trumper. He also took the lead role as Dr Frank-N-Furter in the 1990 West End production of The Rocky Horror Show.

Born in Camden, London, Head came from an artistic family. His father founded Verity Films as a documentary filmmaker, while his mother was an actor who appeared in the 1960s BBC crime drama Maigret. His brother also pursued an acting career. In the early 1980s, Head sang backing vocals for the British pop group Red Box and appeared on their 1986 debut album.

During his five years in Los Angeles filming Buffy, Head maintained a long-distance relationship with his partner Sarah and their two young daughters in England. He described traveling home every three to four weeks whenever the production schedule allowed.

His daughters emphasized that Head considered himself fortunate to work with exceptional talent across his lengthy career. "We know his legacy will live on in the shows he was a part of and in the audiences that love them," they said.
