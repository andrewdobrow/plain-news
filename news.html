4:14 AM ET

Major stock indexes fell sharply on Saturday as chip stocks tumbled and traders adjusted expectations for Federal Reserve interest rate policy. The Nasdaq Composite declined 4.2%, marking its steepest single-day loss in more than a year. The S&P 500 and Dow Jones Industrial Average also posted significant declines.

The selloff centered on the technology and semiconductor sectors, with chip stocks leading the losses. Investors pulled back from artificial intelligence related equities that had driven much of the market's recent gains. The broad retreat reflected a shift in sentiment toward high-growth technology companies that had dominated market performance.

Traders cited growing expectations that the Federal Reserve may raise interest rates as a factor in the downturn. Rising rate hike odds prompted investors to reassess valuations in sectors that had benefited from lower borrowing costs. Technology stocks, particularly those with high valuations relative to earnings, proved especially sensitive to the changing rate outlook.

The decline represented a reversal for markets that had been propelled higher by enthusiasm over artificial intelligence and related technologies. The sharp one-day move suggested increased volatility as investors weighed economic data against monetary policy expectations. Trading volume was elevated as market participants repositioned portfolios in response to the changing landscape.

The losses wiped out recent gains across major indexes, with the technology-heavy Nasdaq bearing the brunt of the selling pressure. The speed and magnitude of the decline highlighted the concentration of market gains in a relatively narrow group of stocks. Broader market participation had already shown signs of weakening before Saturday's selloff, with fewer stocks participating in recent rallies.

This pullback underscores the risks inherent in concentrated market rallies driven by specific sectors or investment themes. When sentiment shifts quickly, as it did Saturday, the unwinding of positions can accelerate losses. The AI trade, which had lifted markets for months, proved vulnerable to reassessment as investors confronted the possibility of higher borrowing costs ahead.

The market's reaction reflects ongoing uncertainty about the path of monetary policy and economic growth. While some investors had grown comfortable with elevated technology valuations based on AI potential, the prospect of tighter financial conditions forced a reckoning. The scale of Saturday's decline suggests many market participants had positioned themselves for continued strength in high-growth stocks.

Looking ahead, investors will likely remain focused on Federal Reserve communications and economic data that could influence rate decisions. The heightened volatility demonstrated in Saturday's trading could persist as markets digest new information about inflation, employment, and growth. The episode serves as a reminder that even sectors that have led market gains can experience sharp reversals when investment sentiment shifts.

A few minutes ago

Ukraine launched a large-scale drone assault on St Petersburg early Wednesday, targeting energy infrastructure and military installations in Russia's second-largest city. The strike occurred just hours before the opening of St Petersburg's flagship economic forum, dealing a significant blow to Vladimir Putin ahead of his planned keynote speech on Friday.

Multiple long-range drones penetrated Russian air defenses to hit an oil storage facility, causing explosions and sending thick black smoke across the city. Governor Alexander Beglov confirmed that the Kirovsky and Krasnoselsky districts were targeted. Ukrainian forces also struck the nearby Kronstadt naval base and shipyard in Leningrad oblast, home to Russia's Baltic fleet. Video footage showed a drone hitting the Boikiy, a Russian guided-missile corvette undergoing repairs in dry dock, which subsequently caught fire.

The timing of the attack proved particularly damaging to the Kremlin's prestige. The economic forum, described as Russia's answer to Davos, opened under a pall of smoke from the burning oil terminal located about 10 miles away. The temporary closure of St Petersburg's airport prevented some of the approximately 20,000 expected visitors from 130 countries from arriving for the three-day summit.

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy claimed the operation targeted "important facilities on Russian territory," including the Petersburg oil terminal and the Kronstadt base. He noted that the oil trans-shipment facility, one of the largest on Russia's Baltic Sea coast, sat roughly 1,100 kilometers from Ukraine's border. Zelenskyy described the strike as part of Ukraine's "long-range sanctions" campaign designed to bring peace closer.

"I thank our warriors for their precision," Zelenskyy said. "Ukraine's plan for long-range sanctions is being implemented exactly as needed to bring peace closer."

Speaking with NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte in Kyiv, Zelenskyy characterized the strikes as justified retaliation following a Russian attack that killed 23 people across Ukraine the previous day.

The attack reflects Ukraine's expanding long-range strike capabilities and increasing willingness to target major Russian economic and military centers far from the front lines. In recent months, Ukrainian drones have successfully disrupted Russian ports, oil storage facilities, military factories, and airbases. They have also destroyed fuel tankers and trucks on supply routes to Crimea, creating shortages across the peninsula.

Russia's economy envoy, Kirill Dmitriev, described the forum as a gathering of "sovereign countries," noting that it represented an alternative to Western-dominated international meetings. The Kremlin had invited leaders from Uzbekistan and Tanzania along with officials from Cuba, Belarus, and Saudi Arabia. Putin announced that Russia would bolster its air defenses in response to the Ukrainian drone campaign.

The Forest Service says it's closing offices to cut costs. But the math doesn't add up

The U.S. Forest Service is moving forward with plans to close research facilities as part of a cost-cutting initiative, but an analysis reveals a significant inconsistency in the agency's approach. The Forest Service says it must shut down research hubs to operate within budget constraints, yet the facilities targeted for closure cost far less to maintain than some locations the agency plans to keep open.

Forest Service data shows that some research offices slated for shutdown cost less than one dollar annually to rent. Meanwhile, the agency intends to continue operating a facility that costs one million dollars per year. This gap between the stated financial rationale and the actual spending decisions has prompted questions about what is actually driving the closure plan.

Research facilities operated by the Forest Service provide critical scientific data on forest health, wildfire behavior, watershed management, and ecosystem changes. These research hubs employ scientists who investigate issues ranging from climate adaptation to timber production. Closing these offices could diminish the agency's capacity to conduct field research and generate data that informs land management decisions across the nation's forests.

The Forest Service has not publicly identified which specific offices will close, nor has it provided detailed justifications for why lower-cost facilities are being eliminated while higher-cost operations continue. The affected facilities are distributed throughout the country.

Federal agencies across the government face mounting pressure to reduce spending. However, the underlying financial logic of this particular round of cuts suggests that factors beyond straightforward cost reduction may be influencing which facilities are selected for closure. The decision to eliminate inexpensive research stations while retaining much more expensive facilities raises questions about the true basis for these selections.

Without additional explanation from the Forest Service, the reasoning behind targeting specific facilities for closure while preserving more expensive ones remains unclear. Scientists, stakeholders, and observers who depend on the agency's research output have expressed concern about understanding the rationale for these decisions.

The closures occur amid broader federal budget pressures, but the mathematics presented by the Forest Service contradicts its cost-cutting justification. Until the agency provides clearer information about how these closure decisions align with its stated financial objectives, the disparity between stated goals and actual resource allocation decisions will likely continue to draw scrutiny from those who rely on the Forest Service's scientific work to inform policy and management practices.

Jalen Brunson, the Knicks' All-NBA guard, delivered the decisive moment by sinking a go-ahead free throw with 9.5 seconds remaining following Wembanyama's costly error. The result marks a historic achievement for New York. The Knicks became only the third team to win the first two games of an NBA Finals on the road, joining the 1993 Chicago Bulls and 1995 Houston Rockets. After stealing Game 1 with a furious fourth-quarter comeback, the Knicks demonstrated remarkable resilience by closing out another thriller away from home.

The Spurs mounted a late rally to pull within striking distance, but Wembanyama's turnover and missed final attempt proved decisive. The young center, who has been a cornerstone of San Antonio's surprising Finals run, acknowledged the difficult stretch after the game.

New York's offensive firepower has been on full display throughout the postseason. The Knicks' dynamic ball movement and off-the-ball action helped them dismantle three opponents on the way to the Finals. Role players have been making shots consistently, and Brunson has risen to the occasion when the game is on the line, creating what many observers describe as Team of Destiny energy.

The series now shifts to Madison Square Garden for Game 3, where the Knicks will look to put themselves in position to clinch the championship. No team has ever come back from a 3-0 deficit in NBA playoff history, meaning the Spurs face an increasingly difficult path to winning the Finals.

San Antonio's youth and resilience have been impressive throughout the postseason, particularly in a bruising Game 7 victory over Oklahoma City in the previous round. However, the exhaustion from that series combined with the Knicks' fresher roster may continue to take its toll.

3:33 AM ET

Anthony Head, the British actor best known for playing Rupert Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, has died at age 72. He passed away peacefully from complications related to pneumonia, surrounded by his family, according to a statement from his daughters Emily and Daisy Head.

"It has been, and forever will be, an honour and a privilege to be his daughters, and to have witnessed first-hand the impact both he and his work have had on so many," they said.

Head became widely recognized for his role as the school librarian and mentor figure to Sarah Michelle Gellar's vampire slayer in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, which aired from 1997 to 2003. The character of Giles served as a guide throughout the series' seven-season run and became a fan favorite. His portrayal established him as a beloved figure in television history.

Born in Camden, London, Head came from an artistic family. His father was a documentary filmmaker who founded Verity Films, while his mother was an actor. Head's early life immersed him in performance, with his mother handmade his dressing-up outfits as Christmas presents.

Beyond Buffy, Head maintained a prolific career across British and American entertainment. He began his professional career on stage, appearing in the 1978 West End revival of Godspell. He later performed in productions including the original West End staging of the Abba musical Chess in 1988 and The Rocky Horror Show in 1990. In the early 1980s, he sang backing vocals for the British pop group Red Box and appeared on their 1986 debut album.

Head became widely recognized in the United Kingdom for his role in Nescafé Gold Blend television advertisements, which featured a slow-burn romantic storyline between a British couple. His film roles included appearances in The Iron Lady and The Inbetweeners Movie.

After Buffy ended, Head took a recurring role in the BBC sketch comedy show Little Britain, where he played a British prime minister. More recently, he appeared in the Apple TV+ series Ted Lasso as former football club owner Rupert Mannion, where he worked regularly with co-creator Jason Sudeikis and earned praise for their on-screen chemistry.

During Buffy's production, Head lived apart from his family, spending five years in Los Angeles while his partner Sarah and two young daughters remained in England. He told the Guardian that he would return home every three or four weeks whenever possible.

"We know how dearly he will be missed by friends, colleagues and fans," his daughters said. "We know his legacy will live on in the shows he was a part of and in the audiences that love them."

Sarah Michelle Gellar paid tribute to Head on Instagram, writing that she was lucky to have known him.

A few minutes ago

The Supreme Court is working through several consequential cases in the coming weeks before its summer break. These decisions will address critical questions about birthright citizenship, immigration enforcement, and the president's authority to remove federal officials from their positions.

The compressed timeline reflects the court's typical end-of-term schedule, when justices release rulings in the most contentious cases argued during the session. The birthright citizenship case requires the court to interpret constitutional language about who automatically becomes a U.S. citizen at birth. The immigration cases involve challenges to enforcement policies and legal protections for certain groups of migrants. The presidential power case examines how much authority the president holds to fire federal officials, particularly those positioned to maintain some independence from direct executive control.

The outcomes could reshape how the federal government operates. The presidential power decision may redefine the balance between the White House and federal agencies, with implications for how future administrations manage the federal workforce. The immigration rulings will likely influence policy for years to come, depending on how broadly or narrowly the court decides the cases.

These cases arrive as the Supreme Court faces mounting scrutiny over its role in politically divisive disputes. The justices have increasingly found themselves at the center of heated debates over immigration enforcement, executive authority, and the scope of constitutional protections. The court's recent decisions have drawn particular attention, including a landmark ruling in April that struck down a critical provision of the Voting Rights Act and made it nearly impossible to win Voting Rights Act claims by requiring plaintiffs to prove intentional discrimination.

That decision has already had consequences. In a subsequent case, the court allowed Alabama to use a redrawn congressional map that eliminates one of the state's two majority-Black districts for upcoming elections. The court's conservative justices said a lower court panel had failed to properly apply the new standards established by the earlier Voting Rights Act decision. The three liberal justices issued a sharp dissent, accusing the majority of causing chaos and abandoning the rule of law.

The timing matters considerably. The court typically concludes its term in late June or early July, meaning justices have a compressed schedule to finalize opinions in major cases. The decisions in the citizenship, immigration, and presidential power cases are expected to draw significant public and political attention given their potential impact on federal policy and the structure of executive power.

How the court rules in these matters will likely influence legal and policy battles for years ahead. The decisions could reshape immigration policy, define citizenship rights, and determine how much power presidents hold over the federal government's structure and operations.

Anthony Head, British Actor Known for Buffy and Ted Lasso, Dies at 72

8:00 AM ET

Anthony Head, the British actor best known for playing Rupert Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, has died at age 72. He passed away peacefully from complications related to pneumonia, surrounded by his family, according to a statement from his daughters Emily and Daisy Head.

"It has been, and forever will be, an honour and a privilege to be his daughters, and to have witnessed first-hand the impact both he and his work have had on so many," they said.

Head became widely recognized for his role as the school librarian and mentor figure to Sarah Michelle Gellar's vampire slayer in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, which aired from 1997 to 2003. The character of Giles served as a guide throughout the series' seven-season run and became a fan favorite. His portrayal established him as a beloved figure in television history.

Born in Camden, London, Head came from an artistic family. His father was a documentary filmmaker who founded Verity Films, while his mother was an actor. Head's early life immersed him in performance, with his mother handmade his dressing-up outfits as Christmas presents.

Beyond Buffy, Head maintained a prolific career across British and American entertainment. He began his professional career on stage, appearing in the 1978 West End revival of Godspell. He later performed in productions including the original West End staging of the Abba musical Chess in 1988 and The Rocky Horror Show in 1990. In the early 1980s, he sang backing vocals for the British pop group Red Box and appeared on their 1986 debut album.

Head became widely recognized in the United Kingdom for his role in Nescafé Gold Blend television advertisements, which featured a slow-burn romantic storyline between a British couple. His film roles included appearances in The Iron Lady and The Inbetweeners Movie.

After Buffy ended, Head took a recurring role in the BBC sketch comedy show Little Britain, where he played a British prime minister. More recently, he appeared in the Apple TV+ series Ted Lasso as former football club owner Rupert Mannion, where he worked regularly with co-creator Jason Sudeikis and earned praise for their on-screen chemistry.

During Buffy's production, Head lived apart from his family, spending five years in Los Angeles while his partner Sarah and two young daughters remained in England. He told the Guardian that he would return home every three or four weeks whenever possible.

"We know how dearly he will be missed by friends, colleagues and fans," his daughters said. "We know his legacy will live on in the shows he was a part of and in the audiences that love them."

Sarah Michelle Gellar paid tribute to Head on Instagram, writing that she was lucky to have known him.
