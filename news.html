Senate passes $70 billion immigration enforcement funding through 2028

6:52 AM ET

The Senate voted early Friday morning to fund President Trump's immigration enforcement agencies through the end of his second term, allocating $70 billion to Immigration and Customs Enforcement and Border Patrol operations over the next three years.

The legislation passed on a party-line vote of 52-47 following an 18-hour marathon session known as a "vote-a-rama," during which senators proposed unlimited amendments. Republican Senator Lisa Murkowski of Alaska was the only member of her party to vote against the bill, while Democratic Senator Michael Bennet of Colorado did not vote.

The passage came after weeks of uncertainty and fierce internal debate over controversial provisions. A central point of contention was a proposed $1.8 billion "anti-weaponization" fund that would allow the administration to issue financial settlements to people connected to the January 6 insurrection. Senate Democrats attempted to strip this provision from the bill through an amendment introduced by Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer. The amendment failed by a single vote, 49-50, with three vulnerable Republican senators breaking ranks to join all Democrats in support.

The debate also involved Republican resistance to initially proposed spending for White House renovations, which Senate leadership ultimately dropped from the bill.

Democratic Senator Amy Klobuchar of Minnesota expressed her frustration with the outcome. "Your tax dollars should not be going to Jan. 6th rioters who went after officers," she wrote on X. "And why give MORE money to ICE when they're already bigger than the FBI?"

Democratic Senator Alex Padilla of California highlighted concerns about immigration enforcement practices, citing deaths of Americans and detention of children, stating that thousands of children have been jailed since the administration began and that ICE has been found to violate the Constitution in more than 10,000 rulings by judges appointed by both Democratic and Republican presidents.

Republican supporters characterized the funding as essential. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer said Republicans were prioritizing Trump's interests over working families. "The Republican agenda is now written in black and white: A slush fund for Trump, tax dodges for Trump, a ballroom for Trump, and a private militia for Trump. For hard-working Americans? Nothing," Schumer stated.

In contrast, Republican Senator Lindsey Graham expressed satisfaction with the outcome, saying he was "very proud of my Republican colleagues for sticking together and making sure that Border Patrol and ICE are fully funded."

The bill now moves to the House of Representatives for consideration, where it is expected to be taken up next week. The legislation represents a major priority for the Trump administration, securing resources for immigration enforcement operations throughout the remainder of his term.

A United States-led cease-fire agreement between Israel and Lebanon has failed to stop fighting, with Hezbollah continuing attacks despite the deal reached on April 17. The Iranian-backed group has rejected the terms, stating they do not consider themselves bound by negotiations conducted without their participation.

Since the cease-fire began, Hezbollah has launched drone attacks against Israeli soldiers in south Lebanon on a nearly daily basis. On Tuesday, the group conducted several drone strikes using fiber-optic-guided aircraft that have evaded Israeli air defenses. An Israeli soldier was killed and six others wounded in a Hezbollah drone attack on Sunday. The armed group claims its Tuesday attacks injured additional Israeli soldiers, though the Israeli military has not confirmed casualties beyond reporting that it fired interceptor missiles.

Israel has responded with continued airstrikes and new displacement orders. On Tuesday alone, Israeli forces ordered residents of 16 villages in south Lebanon to evacuate northward. Over the weekend, Israeli airstrikes killed 18 people and wounded 88 more in Lebanon, according to the Lebanese health ministry. Since fighting began on March 2, at least 2,534 people have been killed and 7,863 wounded by Israeli airstrikes in Lebanon.

The cease-fire agreement permits Israel to conduct strikes in self-defense, language identical to a previous 2024 cease-fire during which Israel struck Lebanon more than 15,000 times. Israel has established a "yellow line" in south Lebanon comprising at least 55 villages, where Israeli troops remain active and have continued demolishing homes.

Hezbollah leadership has explicitly rejected the agreement. Naim Qassem, the group's head, called direct negotiations between the Lebanese government and Israel a "grave sin" that would plunge the country into instability. He stated that the group will not disarm, a key Israeli demand. The Lebanese government responded that "real betrayal" comes from those who drag their country into war for foreign interests.

The collapse of cease-fire enforcement reflects the limited authority of the Lebanese government over Hezbollah. While Lebanese officials negotiate in Washington, they cannot control the armed group's military operations. This dynamic has prompted US Secretary of State Marco Rubio to suggest the United States could help Lebanon develop specialized army units to confront Hezbollah directly, a prospect that has alarmed Lebanese officials concerned about sparking civil conflict.

Israeli officials continue applying pressure, with Defense Minister Israel Katz accusing the Lebanese government of taking cover under Hezbollah rather than acting forcefully against the group. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu stated that Hezbollah retains about 10 percent of its original missiles and promised continued strikes on what he termed Hezbollah infrastructure both inside and north of the security zone.

The United States asked Israel to ensure any response to Hezbollah attacks remained "calculated and limited."

The company released a detailed investor prospectus this week, marking a significant shift for Elon Musk's private space and artificial intelligence company, which has previously limited ownership to institutional investors and employees.

The offering values SpaceX at $1.77 trillion, potentially positioning founder Musk to become the world's first trillionaire. The prospectus reveals financial details about SpaceX's operations and future ambitions while also disclosing numerous quirks and risks associated with the company and its interconnected businesses.

SpaceX's filings show extensive financial ties to other Musk enterprises. The company spent $506 million purchasing Tesla's Megapack battery products in 2025 and $191 million in 2024. More notably, SpaceX acquired $131 million worth of Cybertrucks at manufacturer's suggested retail prices last year, purchasing at least 1,300 vehicles. This represented a significant portion of Tesla's total Cybertruck sales of 20,237 units for the year.

The prospectus emphasizes SpaceX's broader mission beyond commercial spaceflight. The company frames its ultimate goal as establishing permanent colonies on the moon and Mars to ensure human survival and "extend the light of consciousness to the stars." Musk is eligible to receive an award of 1 billion shares if SpaceX achieves the establishment of a permanent human colony on Mars with at least 1 million inhabitants.

The filings also outline substantial risks to investors. SpaceX acquired the artificial intelligence company xAI in February, and the prospectus warns about risks posed by xAI's Grok chatbot. The document discloses that Grok's "spicy" and "unhinged" modes present heightened risks of generating explicit content, misinformation, nonconsensual imagery, and other harmful outputs. The Center for Countering Digital Hate estimated that Grok generated more than 3 million sexualized images in just 11 days earlier this year before the company increased restrictions.

SpaceX's financial filings reveal other notable expenses. The company spent $4 million in 2025 on Musk's personal security through his private security firm, with spending increasing from $2 million in 2023 and $3 million in 2024.

The prospectus also highlights significant financial challenges. SpaceX reported losses of $4.9 billion as a result of its massive spending, and the company discloses that it may never become profitable, presenting a substantial risk to investors despite its market-leading position in commercial spaceflight and satellite operations.

The IPO represents an unprecedented opportunity for retail investors to own shares in a company controlling critical infrastructure for both commercial and government space operations.

FIFA demands payment from fans mistakenly given free World Cup tickets

FIFA told fans who received free tickets to World Cup group stage matches in Toronto due to an error that they must pay within seven days or forfeit the seats.

5:10 AM ET

FIFA has demanded that fans who mistakenly received complimentary tickets to World Cup group stage matches in Toronto pay for them. The organization gave ticket holders a seven-day deadline to complete payment or forfeit their seats.

FIFA acknowledged responsibility for the ticketing error but still requires affected fans to purchase the tickets they initially obtained at no cost.
