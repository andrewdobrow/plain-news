The files being sought would reveal identifying information about patients who visited transgender clinics, along with details about the healthcare providers who treated them. The scope of the request encompasses both patient records and staff information from hospitals offering these services.

A court has issued a temporary order preventing the DOJ from accessing these records for now. The legal challenge raises questions about patient privacy protections and the extent of federal authority to demand medical information from state healthcare facilities.

This action follows other recent moves by the Trump administration targeting transgender people across multiple areas of policy. The Department of Education has launched an investigation into one of the nation's largest women's colleges after it admitted transgender women, claiming the college may be violating federal law by "allowing biological males into women's intimate spaces," including dormitories, bathrooms, and locker rooms.

The Education Department's office of civil rights asserted that Title IX's exemption for single-sex colleges applies only to biological sex, not gender identity. According to the agency's statement, an all-women's college that admits transgender women no longer qualifies for that exemption. The college in question updated its admissions policy in 2015 to include transgender women, noting that "concepts of female identity have evolved" since the college's founding in 1875.

The Trump administration has pursued a series of policies limiting transgender rights. These include efforts to end transgender participation in sports through lawsuits against states that permit such participation, attempts to ban access to gender-affirming medical care, a military ban on transgender service members, and requirements that passports match biological sex at birth.

A conservative education group filed a complaint against the college with the Education Department, urging an investigation. The group states its mission is to fight what it characterizes as "indoctrination in classrooms and on campus."

The college responded to the investigation announcement by affirming its commitment to institutional values and compliance with civil rights laws, while declining to comment further on the pending government investigation.

These actions represent an escalating effort by the administration to restrict transgender participation in education, healthcare, military service, and public spaces. The temporary court block on the California records request offers at least temporary protection for patient privacy, though the legal challenge is likely to continue.

A South Korean court has sentenced ousted President Yoon Suk Yeol to 30 years in prison for authorizing drone flights over Pyongyang in October 2024. The Seoul Central District Court found Yoon guilty of abuse of power and aiding the enemy, concluding that he had conspired in the drone operation from the beginning.

Prosecutors argued that Yoon ordered the flights to fabricate what they called "wartime conditions" that would justify his declaration of martial law. The operation, they contended, was designed to heighten tensions with North Korea and create a pretext for domestic political action. Special prosecutors stated in April that the drone incursions undermined state security.

Yoon has denied all wrongdoing. His legal team argued that he neither ordered nor approved the operation, which they characterized as unrelated to his martial law declaration. Instead, they said the flights were a response to months of North Korean balloon launches that dropped rubbish across the border.

In October 2024, North Korea accused Seoul of flying drones over Pyongyang to drop propaganda leaflets on three separate occasions. South Korea's defense ministry initially issued a vague denial before saying it could neither confirm nor deny the allegations. While the incursions raised tensions between the two countries, they did not result in military clashes.

Prosecutors also argued that the operation led to the leak of classified information about South Korean military capabilities after the drones crashed. This security breach further complicated an already tense situation on the peninsula.

This latest conviction adds to mounting legal troubles for Yoon, who already faces severe penalties from previous cases. In February, he received a life sentence for leading an insurrection related to his martial law declaration, which he claimed was declared "solely for the sake of the nation." He has appealed that conviction.

Yoon was removed from office last year after South Korea's Constitutional Court upheld his impeachment. A snap election followed, won by liberal President Lee Jae Myung. Lee himself faced diplomatic complications when an investigation revealed that government officials had sent drones into North Korea in January. Lee expressed regret over the operation, which North Korea's leader Kim Jong-un's sister initially praised as "wise behaviour."

Drone flights remain a significant source of tension between the two Koreas, which technically remain at war. The fragile military situation on the peninsula means that airspace violations can quickly escalate into serious confrontations.

Yoon, who is currently in custody, has the right to appeal Friday's ruling. Legal experts note that his accumulated sentences could result in lengthy imprisonment.

Wall Street analysts are watching closely to see how the trading debut unfolds and what impact it may have on the broader market. However, investment research group Morningstar has raised concerns about the company's valuation. The firm calculated that SpaceX is worth only $63 per share, well below the anticipated IPO price of $135. Michael Field, Morningstar's chief equity strategist, suggests investors should wait for "a more attractive entry point down the line." He stated: "We believe the business has real strengths, particularly in Starlink, but with so many unknown and untested technologies underpinning much of the valuation price, particularly within the AI business, we think the valuation is extremely speculative."

Senator Elizabeth Warren also expressed concerns about the IPO, calling on the Securities and Exchange Commission to delay it due to worries about the company's valuation and corporate governance.

The company reported a net loss of $4.9 billion in 2025. At the proposed valuation, the IPO values SpaceX at roughly 92 times its trailing sales, a substantial multiple that reflects investor expectations that Musk can achieve ambitious goals including orbital datacenters in space, a base on the moon, and cities on other planets.

SpaceX has claimed Starlink has a total addressable market of $1.6 trillion, though Morningstar estimates the segment's realistic global opportunity at approximately $129 billion.

The Nasdaq index has adjusted its rules to allow SpaceX to join on a fast-track basis. The index provider MSCI has confirmed it will apply existing rules for early inclusion of large IPOs in its Global Standard Indexes. However, S&P Dow Jones Indices has declined to relax its strict entry rules, which could delay SpaceX's inclusion in the S&P 500 index by several months.

Yesterday, 11:02 PM ET

An underground particle detector in China has released its first major scientific findings on neutrinos, measuring two key parameters of these subatomic particles with unprecedented precision. The Jiangmen Underground Neutrino Observatory, known as JUNO, published its results in the journal Nature after completing its initial data collection phase.

The detector measured reactor neutrino oscillation, a phenomenon where neutrinos change between different types as they travel. JUNO sits deep underground to shield its instruments from cosmic radiation that could interfere with detecting these elusive particles, often called ghost particles because they rarely interact with ordinary matter. The facility monitors neutrinos produced by nearby nuclear reactors.

Researchers from the University of California, Irvine contributed to the project and announced that the measurements achieved precision levels beyond previous experiments. The findings focused on two specific neutrino parameters that help scientists understand the fundamental properties of these particles. Neutrinos are among the most abundant particles in the universe but remain poorly understood because of their weak interactions with matter.

The JUNO detector represents a significant investment in particle physics research. Its underground location in China provides ideal conditions for neutrino detection by minimizing background interference. The facility joins other major neutrino observatories around the world in trying to answer basic questions about particle physics and the universe's composition.

The publication of these first results marks a milestone for the project, demonstrating that the detector operates as designed and can produce meaningful scientific data. Future measurements from JUNO are expected to contribute to ongoing debates in particle physics about neutrino mass ordering and other fundamental questions that current theories have not fully resolved.

David Hockney, one of the most important figures in contemporary art across the 20th and 21st centuries, has died at age 88. His representatives confirmed he passed away peacefully at home on June 11, 2026, just one month before his 89th birthday.

Hockney became famous during the 1960s as a pop artist and gained particular recognition for his paintings of swimming pools that became synonymous with Los Angeles culture. His most celebrated works, including A Bigger Splash and Portrait of an Artist (Pool With Two Figures), captured scenes of leisure and emotion beneath California's bright skies. However, his six-decade career extended far beyond these iconic pool paintings. He explored perspective-shifting portrait work using photo-collage techniques, experimented with abstract landscape painting, and later investigated 3D technology as a medium for artistic creation.

Born in Bradford, West Yorkshire in 1937, Hockney grew up in what he described as a radical working-class family that nurtured his artistic talents. He studied at Bradford College and sold his first painting, a portrait of his father, for 10 pounds at the Yorkshire Artists Exhibition in 1957. After completing national service as a hospital orderly, he enrolled at London's Royal College of Art in 1959, where he quickly established himself as a rebellious talent willing to challenge conventions.

During the 1960s, Hockney became a recognizable figure in London and American social circles, known for his bleached blonde hair, thick-rimmed glasses, and bohemian lifestyle. Yet beneath this playboy reputation lay an artist driven by strong work ethic and artistic vision. His work from this period, such as We Two Boys Together Clinging and Cleaning Teeth, Early Evening, depicted gay life with unusual honesty for an era when homosexuality remained illegal in Britain.

After relocating to Los Angeles in the mid-1960s, Hockney's style matured into more restrained, emotionally complex work. Paintings like Man in Shower in Beverly Hills demonstrated his developing realist approach. His 1972 masterpiece, Portrait of an Artist (Pool With Two Figures), sold for $90.3 million in 2018, setting a world record for a living artist at that time.

Even after suffering a stroke in 2012 that temporarily affected his speech, Hockney continued creating art with characteristic determination.

UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer called Hockney "one of Britain's most celebrated artists" whose "vivid, instantly recognisable work influenced generations of artists." Alex Farquharson, director of London's Tate Britain, described Hockney as "an endlessly inventive artist, with a unique vision of the world" and noted that his passing "brings to a close an extraordinary body of work characterised by reinvention."

The Tate plans major exhibitions of his work next year, including a multimedia installation in the Turbine Hall at Tate Modern.

However, his six-decade career extended far beyond these iconic pool paintings. He explored perspective-shifting portrait work using photo-collage techniques, experimented with abstract landscape painting, and later investigated 3D technology as a medium for artistic creation.

Born in Bradford, West Yorkshire in 1937, Hockney grew up in what he described as a radical working-class family that nurtured his artistic talents. He studied at Bradford College and sold his first painting, a portrait of his father, for 10 pounds at the Yorkshire Artists Exhibition in 1957. After completing national service as a hospital orderly, he enrolled at London's Royal College of Art in 1959, where he quickly established himself as a rebellious talent willing to challenge conventions.

During the 1960s, Hockney became a recognizable figure in London and American social circles, known for his bleached blonde hair, thick-rimmed glasses, and bohemian lifestyle. Yet beneath this playboy reputation lay an artist driven by strong work ethic and artistic vision. His work from this period, such as We Two Boys Together Clinging and Cleaning Teeth, Early Evening, depicted gay life with unusual honesty for an era when homosexuality remained illegal in Britain.

After relocating to Los Angeles in the mid-1960s, Hockney's style matured into more restrained, emotionally complex work. Even after suffering a stroke in 2012 that temporarily affected his speech, Hockney continued creating art with characteristic determination.

The Uniform, currently showcased at the Monte-Carlo Television Festival, has been renewed for a second season. The show premiered its first season on DRTV in 2026 and is set within a competitive police academy in Denmark.

Following a deal with Fremantle, the series will now launch on BBC iPlayer and BBC Four in the United Kingdom. The show explores tensions and challenges within the police force training environment.
