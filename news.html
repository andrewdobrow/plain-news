Airlines Face $100 Billion Fuel Cost Increase as Iran Conflict Disrupts Energy Markets

3:27 PM ET

The global airline industry is bracing for an additional $100 billion in jet fuel expenses this year following the conflict in Iran and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a vital shipping channel for crude oil. The surge in fuel costs has forced carriers to slash profit forecasts and acknowledge that higher ticket prices are unavoidable.

Industry leaders gathered in Rio de Janeiro for the International Air Transport Association's 82nd Annual General Meeting to discuss the financial fallout. With jet fuel prices expected to be 70 percent higher across 2026, collective industry profits worldwide are projected to halve to $23 billion. IATA Director General Willie Walsh stated: "High oil prices will inevitably mean higher ticket prices. There's just no way to avoid that."

Jet fuel represents one of the largest variable costs for airlines, making the price surge particularly damaging to operating margins. While OPEC Plus announced plans to increase output by 188,000 barrels per day, this move offers limited relief given the massive quantities of oil prevented from reaching markets by the Strait of Hormuz closure.

For some carriers, the fuel price shock poses an existential threat. Walsh described the situation as "potentially existential" for many airlines, characterizing it as "a challenging and unpredictable time" with "wafer-thin margins." However, he noted that concerns about actual fuel shortages have diminished, and the industry remains profitable with traffic up 2 percent.

The timing of this crisis complicates an industry still stabilizing after pandemic-related disruptions. Walsh observed that "if you factor out the impact on the Middle East for the rest of the world it remains a pretty positive environment," with expectations for a strong northern summer season.

Fare increases will likely hit certain passenger categories hardest. Sean Doyle, chief executive of British Airways, suggested that long-haul and business passengers would absorb the bulk of price increases, while price-sensitive short-haul holiday flights would face smaller hikes. "If fuel goes up, fares have to go up," Doyle said.

Research from IATA indicates that roughly half of passengers are prepared to spend substantially more on fares if prices track oil costs. This willingness may help offset some impact during peak travel seasons.

Beyond fuel concerns, European airlines face additional headwinds from new border regulations. The EU's entry-exit system, scheduled for full implementation by September 7, will require biometric checks on most non-EU citizens. IATA warned that processing times could extend from 20 to 25 seconds to 90 seconds per passenger, creating significant congestion at European airports during peak travel periods. Rafael Schvartsman, IATA's vice president for Europe, cautioned that "the probability that people will be waiting in lines for a long time is very, very high," particularly affecting major Mediterranean destinations.

Alexander Zverev claimed his first Grand Slam title at the French Open, defeating Flavio Cobolli 6-1, 4-6, 6-4, 6-7 (5), 6-1 in a tense five-set final. The second-seeded German player finally secured the major championship that had eluded him throughout his career, despite previous success at every other level of professional tennis.

Zverev had previously won Masters 1000 events, claimed the ATP Finals twice, and earned an Olympic gold medal in Tokyo 2020, but he had lost in all three of his previous Grand Slam finals. The French Open final represented his fourth major final appearance and his first victory at this stage.

The match was marked by considerable tension and nerves on both sides of the net. Zverev dominated the opening set 6-1, but Cobolli fought back to win the second set 6-4 as the German's familiar problems with his forehand and second serve resurfaced. Zverev regained control in the third set with a 6-4 victory, completely outserving his opponent throughout the set. Cobolli crumbled under pressure in the set's final moments, spraying three wild unforced errors.

However, Zverev's nerves intensified as the finish line approached. In the fourth set, he appeared overwhelmed by the moment, though Cobolli also struggled. The set went to a tiebreaker where Cobolli, serving at 5-4 with just 39 percent first-serve success, mounted a brilliant comeback. The Italian saved the championship with a searing running forehand winner to force a fifth set.

In the decisive final set, Cobolli's energy reserves were exhausted. He immediately surrendered his opening service game and never recovered, allowing Zverev to pull away convincingly with a 6-1 victory.

After Cobolli missed an overhead on the second championship point, Zverev dropped to his back and began sobbing. He later addressed his team, saying: "We've been through injuries. We've been through heartbreak. We've been through losses. We've been losers at times in the important moments. But at the end of the day, we're grand slam champions now and that's what counts."

Zverev's path to the final was significantly aided by the absence of top competitors. Top seed Jannik Sinner and Novak Djokovic were eliminated in consecutive days, while Carlos Alcaraz was absent due to injury. Cobolli, the world number 14, reached his first Grand Slam final without playing in the semifinal after his Italian compatriot Matteo Arnaldi withdrew due to illness.

Speaking after his victory, Zverev reflected on his complex relationship with Roland Garros. "This court is so special to me in so many ways," he said. "I've had the best moments of my life on this court, I had the worst moment of my life on this court. I was laying in that corner over there with seven broken ligaments and two fractured bones. I lost a grand slam final here two years ago. But now, finally, it's a happy end."

Senate passes immigration enforcement funding bill

A few minutes ago

Senate Democrats have blocked a funding package for the Department of Homeland Security, pushing the agency toward a partial shutdown at midnight Friday. The vote fell short of the 60 votes needed to overcome a filibuster, with senators splitting largely along party lines at 52-47 in favor of continuing the department's funding.

The blockade reflects Democratic anger over the Trump administration's immigration enforcement operations and recent deaths in Minneapolis. Last month, federal agents shot and killed two US citizens, Renee Good and Alex Pretti, in the city. The controversy intensified when Tom Homan, the Trump administration's border czar, announced that ICE was largely withdrawing its operations from Minneapolis.

Democrats have demanded significant reforms to how ICE operates before agreeing to fund the agency. Their proposed changes include requiring agents to wear identification and body cameras, and prohibiting enforcement activities near schools, medical facilities, churches, polling stations, childcare facilities and courts. Democrats also want local and state law enforcement to have guaranteed authority to investigate potential crimes by federal agents, including excessive use of force.

Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer said the party was seeking common-sense changes. "From day one, I've said unless there were really strong, meaningful reforms to rein in ICE and stop the violence, there will not be Democratic votes to fund ICE," he said. "We've been pushing commonsense reforms, the very types of things that local police and sheriffs throughout America routinely follow. Today's strong vote was a shot across the bow to Republicans. Democrats will not support a blank check for chaos."

Republicans support body cameras but have resisted other proposals, arguing they would restrict agents' operations. Republican Senator James Lankford of Oklahoma criticized Democrats' approach, saying their reaction to the Minneapolis incident would harm broader homeland security efforts including the Coast Guard, Secret Service, and FEMA.

The shutdown's impact may be limited. ICE and Customs and Border Protection received 75 billion dollars in funding last year through what the Trump administration called a flagship spending bill. This means immigration enforcement agencies will continue operating even if the general DHS funding lapses. Instead, the shutdown would primarily affect agencies like the Transportation Safety Administration, FEMA, and the US Coast Guard.

Democratic Senator Maggie Hassan of New Hampshire noted that the department has substantial funding available regardless. "Even if the current funding bill stalls or is not passed that would fund the Department of Homeland Security, the department has an extraordinary amount of money at its disposal right now because it was funded during the big tax bill that my Republican colleagues passed last summer," she said.

The House will now decide whether to take further action on the legislation.
