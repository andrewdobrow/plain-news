4:24 PM ET

U.S. employers added 162,000 jobs in August, marking an uptick after a sluggish summer for the labor market. The unemployment rate remained steady at 4.1 percent, down from its peak of 4.5 percent in November, according to data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

The August hiring represents a rebound from inconsistent job growth in recent months. Job additions have fluctuated significantly, ranging from 214,000 in March to just 21,000 in July. The June and July figures were revised upward following initial releases, with June revised up by 11,000 and July revised up by 44,000 jobs.

Economists had predicted at least 50,000 new jobs would be added in August. The actual figure exceeded those expectations. However, other employment indicators painted a more mixed picture. The payroll firm ADP reported that private companies added just 38,000 jobs in August, marking the lowest monthly total since January. Meanwhile, the outplacement firm Challenger, Gray and Christmas reported that announced layoffs declined 41 percent compared to the same period last year.

Labor market analysts describe the current employment situation as stalled in a "slow hire, slow fire" state, with neither significant growth nor substantial contractions. A separate BLS report showed job openings and layoffs changed little in July. The number of workers quitting their jobs also remained flat, suggesting declining worker confidence about finding alternative employment.

The August employment data arrives amid persistent economic pressures facing households. U.S. inflation increased significantly, rising from 2.4 percent in February to 3.4 percent in July, with a peak of 4.2 percent in May. This marks the highest rate since 2023. Rising prices have compounded frustrations for many Americans even as job gains continue.

The inflation concerns have rippled through financial markets. Yields on U.S. Treasury bonds have risen, signaling investor concerns about long-term economic health. Higher bond yields could push up borrowing costs across mortgages, car loans, and student debt.

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh recently stated the central bank remained committed to lowering inflation to its 2 percent target. "We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed," Warsh said. "Otherwise, we have work to do." Economists expect at least one interest rate increase from the Federal Reserve before year's end.

The strong August employment figures complicate political pressures on the central bank. President Trump has publicly urged the Fed to cut rates and threatened to halt trade with countries holding trade deficits if the central bank does not comply. The robust jobs data provides economic justification for the Fed to maintain its current course or pursue rate increases rather than cuts, potentially undermining those pressure campaigns.

4:53 PM ET

A devastating flash flood triggered by glacial collapse has swept through the Himalayan border region shared by Nepal, Tibet and India, leaving at least 157 people dead and hundreds more unaccounted for. The disaster struck when a torrent of mud, water and debris burst from riverbanks, destroying homes, roads, bridges and power infrastructure across multiple countries.

The initial destruction occurred on Nepal's side of the border, though officials in Tibet reported significant casualties and expressed fears of "major casualties" in the region. The US Geological Survey initially classified the event as a 4.4-magnitude earthquake before determining the seismic energy actually came from a glacial collapse and debris flow.

In Nepal alone, 403 people have been reported missing, including 341 foreign nationals from countries such as the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, South Korea and Malaysia. Nepal's tourism board indicated that more than 100 of the missing were religious tourists from India and the Indian diaspora on a pilgrimage to Mount Kailash in Tibet. Specific numbers included 47 missing Americans, 34 Australians, 33 British citizens and 24 Canadians. In Tibet, Chinese state media reported at least three killed and 265 missing.

Rescue operations face significant obstacles. Helicopters cannot land in the hardest-hit areas of Rasuwa district until river waters recede, and heavy monsoon rains continue hampering efforts. The Gyirong region, which served as a key trade and transit point between China and Nepal, has been severely damaged. According to one health worker in the area, "The settlements next to the river have been completely swept away."

Officials warned that flooding could worsen because river sections remain blocked by ice, rocks and debris. Authorities issued alerts urging residents across affected districts in Nepal to avoid the Bhote Koshi, Trishuli and Narayani rivers. Flood alerts were also issued for Indian states downstream of the affected rivers.

Nepal's energy ministry reported significant electricity disruption, particularly from destroyed hydroelectric plants. Nepal's Prime Minister Balendra Shah mobilized police and military personnel for rescue efforts and urged people living near riverbanks to move to higher ground. India's Prime Minister Narendra Modi offered Nepal "all possible types of humanitarian assistance" after speaking with Shah.

China's President Xi Jinping called for "all-out" search efforts and better monitoring systems to prevent secondary disasters. UN Secretary-General António Guterres said UN teams were mobilizing supplies and personnel to support affected communities.

Scientists attribute the region's vulnerability to climate change. The Himalayan region is warming faster than the rest of the world, with Nepal's snow-capped mountains losing nearly one-third of their ice in just over 30 years. Glacier melt rates across the Hindu Kush region have doubled since 2000. Experts have called for enhanced surveillance and early warning systems across the Himalayan region to prevent future disasters.

A federal judge has extended an order blocking a Trump administration executive order that would restrict mail-in voting ahead of the midterm elections. The decision comes as states have already begun distributing ballots to voters.

The ruling represents a significant development in ongoing legal challenges to voting access restrictions. The judge's extended injunction prevents the executive order from taking effect while litigation continues. The blocked order had aimed to impose new rules that would limit how Americans can use mail-in ballots, a voting method that millions of people depend on for participating in elections.

North Carolina became the first state to send out mail-in ballots for the midterms on Friday, just hours after the Trump administration asked the Supreme Court to lift the judge's block and allow the administration to move forward with the restrictions. The timing highlights the urgent nature of the legal battle, as ballots are already in voters' hands and the administration simultaneously pursues appeals.

The Trump administration has argued that the mail voting restrictions are necessary for election security. However, voting rights advocates and legal experts have raised constitutional concerns about the administration's approach. Democracy Forward, an organization representing plaintiffs challenging the order, released a statement emphasizing the legal issues at stake.

"The court recognized the serious legal concerns raised by the Trump administration's attempt to create restrictions operating outside the constitutional safeguards that govern election procedures," an organization spokesperson said of a similar ruling blocking another Trump administration action.

The lawsuit involves multiple plaintiffs challenging the administration's efforts. These include a former federal prosecutor, voting rights watchdog groups, and other organizations concerned about election access and constitutional governance.

The extended injunction means that current mail voting procedures will remain in place for the midterm elections unless the Supreme Court or another higher court intervenes. The case reflects a broader pattern of election-related litigation moving through federal courts as the midterms approach.

Mail-in voting has become increasingly common since the pandemic, with millions of Americans using this method because they cannot vote in person due to work schedules, disabilities, or distance from polling places. Restrictions on mail voting would likely affect different populations unevenly, according to voting rights advocates.

The legal dispute over mail voting reflects deeper disagreements about how elections should be administered and who should have access to convenient voting methods. While supporters of restrictions emphasize security concerns, opponents argue that mail ballots are secure and that new limitations would create unnecessary barriers to participation.

The case will likely continue moving through the courts as the midterm elections approach, with the potential for further appeals and decisions from higher courts.

Microsoft has announced it will impose monthly time limits on cloud gaming for Xbox Game Pass subscribers, a significant change to the service's previously unlimited streaming model. The new restrictions come as the company grapples with rising operational costs associated with running its cloud gaming infrastructure.

The limitations will affect subscribers who use Xbox Cloud Gaming to stream games remotely rather than downloading them to local hardware. Microsoft has not publicly detailed the exact hour thresholds that will trigger the caps, but the move represents a fundamental shift in how the service operates. Cloud gaming allows users to play Xbox titles on devices like phones, tablets, and computers without owning a console, relying instead on Microsoft's remote servers to process the games.

The change arrives alongside other developments in Microsoft's gaming strategy. The company is expanding the Xbox app to TCL televisions and introducing a pay-as-you-go cloud gaming option, suggesting a broader restructuring of how it monetizes and delivers game streaming services. The television integration would allow users to stream games directly to their TVs without additional hardware, while pay-as-you-go could offer an alternative for users who want occasional cloud access without a full subscription.

Cloud gaming services require substantial infrastructure investment, with companies maintaining server farms equipped with gaming hardware to process streams for potentially millions of concurrent users. The computational and bandwidth costs scale with usage time, making unlimited streaming financially challenging. Microsoft's Xbox Cloud Gaming competes with services like Nvidia's GeForce Now and Sony's PlayStation Plus cloud offerings in a market where providers are still working to establish sustainable business models.

The imposed limits may affect how subscribers use the service, particularly those who relied on cloud streaming as their primary way to play games without investing in console hardware. Microsoft has positioned cloud gaming as central to its vision of making Xbox games accessible across devices, but the new restrictions suggest the company is balancing that accessibility against operational realities. The changes take effect as Microsoft continues to integrate its gaming division across platforms following its acquisition of Activision Blizzard.

The Supreme Court delivered a significant victory to Republican and Democratic party organizations Friday, ruling that political parties are entitled to discounted television advertising rates when they coordinate spending with candidates.

The decision addresses a long-standing dispute over how broadcast stations must price political advertising under federal communications law. Broadcasters are required to offer candidates the lowest unit rate for ad time during the weeks before an election, but questions have persisted about whether this rule applies to party committees that coordinate with campaigns. The court's ruling extends these discounted rates to party organizations, potentially saving national and state party committees millions of dollars in advertising costs.

The timing of the decision comes as both major parties prepare for the 2026 midterm elections in November, when control of Congress and numerous state offices will be decided. Political advertising spending for the 2026 cycle is expected to reach record levels, with television continuing to serve as a dominant medium even as digital platforms grow in importance. Party committees, which are permitted to raise larger contributions than individual candidates under federal law, play a crucial role in funding competitive races across the country.

The ruling could substantially reshape the financial landscape of campaign advertising by making party-funded television spots significantly less expensive during the critical final weeks leading up to elections. Television stations had opposed the extension, arguing that providing lowest unit rates to coordinated party spending would reduce their advertising revenue and create administrative difficulties. Campaign finance reform advocates have raised concerns that the decision could strengthen the influence of party organizations over individual candidates.

The Supreme Court has not yet released the complete opinion or provided details about the vote breakdown. Legal experts anticipate that the ruling will encourage parties to shift more resources toward traditional broadcast advertising in competitive markets, particularly in swing states and districts where television can reach large numbers of voters who might be persuadable.

The decision addresses a fundamental question about how federal communications law applies to party spending when it is coordinated with candidates. Broadcasters maintain strict pricing rules designed to ensure that candidates have access to affordable advertising, but the precise scope of these requirements has been contested when party organizations make related purchases.

Political parties have long sought equal treatment with candidates under advertising rate regulations. The court's decision validates their argument that coordinated party spending should qualify for the same favorable pricing that candidates receive. This ruling could accelerate a significant shift in how campaign funds are allocated and spent, with parties potentially redirecting resources from other activities to maximize their television advertising capacity during crucial election periods.

UN Adopts New World Map Showing Africa at True Size

The United Nations voted to adopt a new world map projection that accurately represents Africa's size, with only the United States opposing the resolution.

3:44 PM ET

The United Nations has voted to officially retire the Mercator projection in favor of the Equal Earth design, a more accurate map that displays Africa's true size. The Togo-sponsored resolution passed with support from 164 nations, with the United States as the sole dissenting vote.

The traditional Mercator projection, created in the 16th century, has long distorted continental sizes, making Africa appear far smaller than it actually is—comparable to Greenland despite being roughly 14 times larger. The new Equal Earth design, developed in 2018, provides a more geographically accurate representation for global educational and official purposes.
