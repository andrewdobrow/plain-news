12:00 PM ET

Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision Michael Barr warned that recent loosening of Wall Street bank regulations has increased risks to the financial system while failing to deliver promised benefits to lending. The remarks represent a pushback against the trend toward regulatory relief that has gained momentum in recent years.

Barr said the deregulation has boosted profits at large banks without producing corresponding increases in lending to businesses and consumers. His assessment challenges the core argument that lighter regulation would free up capital for productive use in the economy. Instead, according to Barr, the changes have primarily benefited bank shareholders while leaving the financial system more vulnerable.

The Fed official's comments come as regulators debate the appropriate level of oversight for major financial institutions. After the 2008 financial crisis, Congress and regulators imposed stricter capital requirements and stress testing on large banks to prevent future taxpayer-funded bailouts. Some of those rules have been rolled back in subsequent years, with supporters arguing they imposed unnecessary costs on banks and the broader economy.

Barr specifically pointed to regulatory relief measures that increased big bank profits without measurable improvements in credit availability. His position puts him at odds with those who advocate for further easing of banking rules. The debate over bank regulation often pits concerns about financial stability against arguments that excessive rules constrain economic growth.

The warning carries weight given Barr's role overseeing the Fed's regulatory framework for banks. His comments suggest resistance within the central bank to additional loosening of rules governing Wall Street institutions. The Fed shares responsibility with other agencies for setting and enforcing banking regulations, giving Barr's views significant influence over future policy direction.

4:32 PM ET

OpenAI introduced Lockdown Mode, a new security feature designed to protect sensitive data from prompt injection attacks targeting its ChatGPT platform. The company announced the feature as part of its ongoing efforts to enhance security for users handling confidential information through AI systems.

Prompt injection attacks occur when malicious actors craft specific inputs to trick AI models into revealing protected data or bypassing security controls. These attacks have become a growing concern as businesses increasingly integrate AI tools into workflows involving proprietary or sensitive information. Lockdown Mode aims to reduce the risk that such attacks successfully extract protected data from ChatGPT interactions.

The new security layer adds protections specifically designed to prevent unauthorized data sharing during AI conversations. OpenAI acknowledged that even with Lockdown Mode active, ChatGPT may still remain vulnerable to some forms of prompt injection. The company stated that the feature's primary goal is to reduce the likelihood that sensitive information gets shared during an attack, rather than eliminating the vulnerability entirely.

The launch comes as AI security concerns intensify across the technology industry. Organizations using AI assistants for tasks involving confidential business data, personal information, or proprietary research face mounting pressure to implement stronger safeguards. Prompt injection represents one of several attack vectors that security researchers have identified as AI adoption accelerates in enterprise environments.

OpenAI did not provide specific technical details about how Lockdown Mode operates or whether it will be available to all users or limited to enterprise customers. The company has been expanding its security offerings as competition grows among AI providers and as regulatory scrutiny increases around AI safety and data protection.

Kennedy Center Loses Lawsuit Against Musician Who Canceled Christmas Concert Over Trump Name Change

A federal judge dismissed a lawsuit filed by the Kennedy Center against jazz drummer Chuck Redd, who withdrew from a Christmas Eve performance after the venue added former President Donald Trump's name to its official title. Judge Tanya Jones Bosier ruled that Kennedy Center officials failed to establish a legally binding agreement with Redd. "There's no dispute that he did no...

8:37 PM ET

A federal judge dismissed a lawsuit filed by the Kennedy Center against jazz drummer Chuck Redd, who withdrew from a Christmas Eve performance after the venue added former President Donald Trump's name to its official title.

Judge Tanya Jones Bosier ruled that Kennedy Center officials failed to establish a legally binding agreement with Redd. "There's no dispute that he did not sign the 2025 agreement," the judge said, according to the Washington Post. "I could not find a valid breach-of-contract claim here."

The judge dismissed the case under Washington D.C.'s anti-SLAPP law, which protects people from lawsuits designed to silence their speech on matters of public interest. Kennedy Center President Richard Grenell had threatened legal action soon after Redd canceled, calling the withdrawal "this political stunt" and initially seeking one million dollars in damages.

The Kennedy Center filed suit in March and offered a settlement proposal: Redd would pay seven thousand five hundred dollars, perform at this year's Christmas Eve Jazz Jam concert, and refrain from making "political commentary" about his decision to withdraw from the annual free event.

Redd canceled his performance after the White House announced Trump's name would be added to the facility. The venue, formally known as the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, is designated by Congress as a living memorial to the 35th president.

A separate ruling came the week before the lawsuit dismissal. Another judge ordered the removal of Trump's name from the Kennedy Center's facade and website, determining that the performing arts venue cannot be renamed without an act of Congress.

Lisa J. Banks, one of Redd's lawyers, said in a statement that "the lawsuit against Mr. Redd was political retribution, pure and simple, by the Trump Kennedy Center, and the Court correctly saw it as such in dismissing the case with prejudice."

Redd told the Associated Press he was "very pleased" by the ruling. The case drew attention to tension within the arts community over the Kennedy Center's name change, with the musician's cancellation representing one of the first public protests by a performing artist against the decision.

The judge's dismissal suggests that artists may have more legal protection than previously thought when objecting to venue changes on personal or political grounds, particularly when no signed agreement exists. The ruling's grounding in anti-SLAPP law indicates the court viewed the Kennedy Center's lawsuit as an attempt to suppress Redd's political speech rather than a straightforward contract dispute.
