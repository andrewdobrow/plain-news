2:44 PM ET

A 3.6 magnitude earthquake shook the East Bay region on Sunday morning, with a smaller aftershock following the initial tremor, according to the United States Geological Survey.

The main quake was centered near Alamo in Contra Costa County, an area located east of the Berkeley Hills. The USGS recorded preliminary data placing the magnitude at 3.6, which typically produces noticeable shaking across a wide area but usually does not cause major structural damage to buildings and infrastructure.

Residents throughout the San Francisco Bay Area reported feeling the ground movement. The secondary tremor that followed was smaller in magnitude than the initial earthquake.

The East Bay region, which includes cities such as Oakland and Berkeley along with smaller communities in Contra Costa and Alameda counties, sits along several major fault lines. The San Andreas and Hayward faults run through the area, making it one of California's most seismically active regions. Thousands of small earthquakes occur in the Bay Area each year, though the vast majority are too weak for people to notice.

Sunday's earthquake was strong enough to be widely felt and reported by residents across the region. However, no immediate reports of injuries or property damage were released following the event.

California operates an extensive earthquake monitoring network to detect seismic activity and provide warnings to residents. The state's ShakeAlert system can deliver seconds to minutes of advance warning before powerful shaking reaches populated areas. This early warning capability helps people take protective actions before strong ground motion arrives. Sunday's earthquake did not reach the threshold required to trigger widespread public alerts through this system.

Earthquakes of the 3.6 magnitude level are relatively common occurrences in the Bay Area. The region's location along major fault lines means residents experience frequent seismic activity, though most earthquakes go unnoticed because they are too small. Larger quakes that cause significant damage or injuries happen far less frequently but remain a concern for Bay Area communities and emergency officials.

The USGS continues to monitor the area for additional seismic activity. Aftershocks following an initial earthquake are normal and can continue for hours, days, or even weeks after the main event. Most aftershocks are smaller than the original quake, as was the case with Sunday's temblor.

Residents in earthquake-prone areas like the East Bay are generally advised to prepare emergency kits, secure heavy furniture and appliances, and know how to protect themselves during shaking. Understanding local fault lines and earthquake risks helps communities prepare for future seismic events.

3:27 PM ET

Global airlines are confronting an additional $100 billion in jet fuel costs this year, a financial shock that will force carriers to raise ticket prices across the industry. The surge stems from disruptions to global oil supplies following the closure of the Strait of Hormuz in March amid conflict with Iran.

Jet fuel prices are expected to be 70 percent higher throughout 2026, leading the International Air Transport Association to project that worldwide airline industry profits will drop to $23 billion, down from previous forecasts of $46 billion. "High oil prices will inevitably mean higher ticket prices," said Willie Walsh, Iata's director general. "There's just no way to avoid that."

The financial pressure is reshaping profit forecasts across the sector, with industry leaders gathered in Rio de Janeiro to address the deteriorating outlook. Some carriers will struggle to survive the fuel price shock caused by the strait closure. Walsh described it as a "challenging and unpredictable time" with "wafer-thin margins," adding that "for a lot of airlines the increase in the fuel bill is potentially existential."

However, Walsh emphasized that concerns about fuel shortages have been resolved and the situation differs from previous crises. The industry remains profitable and is still forecasting growth, with traffic up 2 percent. He noted that outside the Middle East, conditions remain relatively positive.

Passengers should expect varying fare increases depending on route type. Sean Doyle, chief executive of British Airways, indicated that long-haul and business class travelers will likely face the bulk of increases first. "A brand like BA, which has got a lot of long haul, a lot of corporate, a lot of premium, we'd expect maybe to have more pass-through of prices than maybe a carrier who's solely competing for leisure short haul," Doyle explained.

Short-haul holiday flights, which compete heavily on price, are expected to see increases more gradually. According to Iata research, around half of passengers surveyed said they were prepared to spend substantially more on fares if prices tracked oil costs, which Walsh said "bodes well" for a strong summer season in the Northern Hemisphere.

Industry data shows more British and European travelers will fly within Europe than usual, with fewer venturing farther afield given ongoing uncertainty around Gulf hubs.

Beyond fuel costs, Iata raised concerns about Europe's new entry-exit system for border controls, which could create significant delays for summer travelers. The system, set to fully launch by September 7, will require fingerprinting and photographing of most non-EU citizens. Iata warned that processing times could stretch to 90 seconds per passenger compared to the current 20 to 25 seconds, creating potential bottlenecks at airports across the continent during peak travel season.

Alexander Zverev claimed his first Grand Slam championship at the French Open, defeating Flavio Cobolli 6-1, 4-6, 6-4, 6-7 (5), 6-1 in a tense five-set final. The second seed's victory came after three previous Grand Slam final losses, finally delivering the major title that had eluded him throughout his career.

Zverev dominated the opening set, but Cobolli mounted a comeback in the second as the German's familiar weaknesses emerged. Zverev's forehand troubles and second serve issues resurfaced, allowing the Italian to level the match. The German regained control in the third set, completely outserving his opponent. However, nerves overwhelmed Zverev in the fourth set as he approached the finish line. Cobolli forced a tiebreaker and won it with a running forehand winner to force a deciding set. In the fifth, Cobolli's energy completely depleted, and Zverev pulled away to secure the championship.

Throughout his career, Zverev had won at nearly every other level of professional tennis. He captured Masters 1000 events, triumphed twice at the ATP Finals, and earned Olympic gold in Tokyo in 2020. Yet the Grand Slam title remained elusive until now. This edition of the French Open was unusually chaotic, with top players eliminated early. Top seed Jannik Sinner and Novak Djokovic were bounced from the tournament in consecutive days, while Carlos Alcaraz was absent due to injury. With the strongest competitors gone, Zverev was clearly the most dominant player remaining in the draw.

Cobolli, ranked world number 14, reached his first Grand Slam final in unexpected fashion. He didn't need to strike a ball in the semifinal after his Italian compatriot Matteo Arnaldi withdrew due to illness. The underdog felt the moment intensely from the start, unable to compete in the opening set. He showed more composure in subsequent sets, using his drop shot effectively and approaching the net courageously, but ultimately couldn't maintain the intensity required to overcome Zverev.

After victory, Zverev addressed his team, saying: "We've been through injuries. We've been through heartbreak. We've been through losses. We've been losers at times in the important moments. But at the end of the day, we're grand slam champions now and that's what counts."

Zverev also reflected on his history at Roland Garros, speaking of the trauma of a serious injury sustained there. "This court is so special to me in so many ways," he said. "I had the worst moment of my life on this court. I was laying in that corner over there with seven broken ligaments and two fractured bones. I lost a grand slam final here two years ago. But now, finally, it's a happy end."

The victory marks a breakthrough moment for the German player and answers longstanding questions about his ability to perform when it matters most on tennis's biggest stages.

Pelley's termination came as part of a broader overhaul that also resulted in the firing of the show's executive producer, executive editor, and correspondents Sharyn Alfonsi and Cecilia Vega.

During a staff meeting on Monday morning with newly appointed executive producer Nick Bilton and CBS News managing editor Charles Forelle, Pelley directly attacked editor-in-chief Bari Weiss. "She's murdering 60 Minutes," Pelley said, according to sources present. "She does not love this place. She was brought in to kill it and is doing exactly that." The room responded with a standing ovation for Pelley, though Forelle accused him of being rude.

In a statement released after his firing, Pelley claimed that network executives instructed him "to inject falsehoods and bias" into his reporting. He specifically accused CBS News leadership of interfering with a broadcast segment examining an immigration officer's killing of Minneapolis protester Renee Good in January. According to Pelley, Weiss had sent an email requesting changes to the report shortly before it was set to air.

Alfonsi issued her own statement criticizing the network's direction, saying: "The wall between editorial independence and corporate interest at CBS is being methodically torn down." She noted that a December segment about a prison in El Salvador had been shelved by Weiss. "Journalists willing to challenge authority are being pushed aside in favor of those who will not," Alfonsi added.

Cecilia Vega similarly alleged political bias in the newsroom. "In recent months, my producing teams and I have experienced efforts to insert political bias into our stories," she said. "Reporting teams have held back on submitting story pitches about important news topics out of fear of the internal repercussions." CBS News contested Vega's characterization.

The controversy has sparked concern among remaining staff about the show's future. Of the seven full-time correspondents who appeared on a lobby poster at CBS headquarters, three have now been fired and Anderson Cooper previously announced his departure. Lesley Stahl, Bill Whitaker, and Jon Wertheim announced they would remain at the show, with Stahl stating: "We don't want to see 60 Minutes die. We have been grieving because this whole mess has wounded and damaged the broadcast."

The firings represent an unprecedented crisis for the long-running program, which had held the number-one position in news programming for 52 consecutive television seasons. CBS News declined to comment on the internal meeting and Pelley's specific accusations.

Alexander Zverev claimed his first Grand Slam title at the French Open, defeating Italy's Flavio Cobolli in a grueling five-set final. The second seed won 6-1, 4-6, 6-4, 6-7 (5), 6-1, securing victory when Cobolli missed an overhead on the second championship point. Zverev collapsed to his back in tears following the win.

The German player had reached three previous Grand Slam finals without winning, despite his success elsewhere in professional tennis. He had triumphed at Masters 1000 events, won the ATP Finals twice, and earned Olympic gold in Tokyo 2020—but a major championship had eluded him until now.
