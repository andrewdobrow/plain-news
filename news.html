South Korea's benchmark stock index experienced a sharp decline on Tuesday, forcing temporary trading halts as investors sold off semiconductor stocks amid concerns about artificial intelligence spending and Chinese competition.

The Kospi index fell more than 10 percent by the end of trading, representing one of the steepest single-day drops in recent months. The decline triggered circuit breakers that temporarily paused trading after the index had slumped 8 percent, a mechanism designed to prevent panic selling during severe market movements.

Semiconductor companies including Samsung and SK Hynix saw their shares drop by more than 10 percent as the selloff intensified. The losses extended well beyond South Korea, with chip stocks sliding across Asia and affecting American technology markets. Nasdaq futures slipped following the deepening selloff in Asia, suggesting continued pressure on US technology stocks.

Investors have become increasingly worried about whether artificial intelligence companies can justify the enormous amounts they are spending to expand data center infrastructure. Many AI companies have relied on significant borrowing to fund these expansions, raising questions about the sustainability of such spending levels. Additionally, concerns about potential competition from Chinese semiconductor manufacturers have added to market anxiety.

The semiconductor industry has seen massive capital investments focused on AI-related technology over the past year. However, investors are now questioning whether the financial returns will match these large outlays. Worries about circular funding patterns within the chip sector have contributed to the broader unease affecting markets.

The market turmoil extended beyond technology stocks. Oil prices also retreated as broader market concerns spread across different sectors. This volatility occurred during what analysts described as a critical period for global stock markets.

South Korea's position as one of the world's most important semiconductor manufacturing hubs made the country particularly vulnerable to shifts in investor sentiment about AI spending. South Korean chipmakers have significant exposure to the technology supply chain, meaning they face substantial risk when confidence in AI spending wavers.

The sharp moves in Asian markets have put investors on alert about potential continued pressure on technology stocks globally. The severity of selling pressure in South Korea's market underscored how deeply concerns about AI spending sustainability and Chinese competition are affecting investor confidence in the semiconductor sector.

The temporary trading halt illustrated the intensity of the selloff and highlighted how quickly market sentiment can shift when major investors reassess their positions in technology stocks. As markets continue to process these developments, analysts are monitoring whether the decline will stabilize or if additional losses may follow in coming trading sessions.

Japan maintains one of the world's most advanced earthquake early warning and response systems, designed to alert residents seconds before shaking begins and coordinate rapid emergency response. The country's building codes require structures to withstand significant seismic activity, though older buildings and infrastructure remain vulnerable to major quakes.

Rescue operations were underway at the Aeon Mall and other affected sites across Kumamoto. The full extent of casualties and structural damage was still being assessed as emergency crews worked to reach trapped individuals and extinguish fires. Japanese authorities typically conduct thorough building inspections following major earthquakes to identify structures unsafe for occupancy and coordinate evacuation and relief efforts for affected residents.

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani has unveiled two competing budget proposals to address the city's growing fiscal crisis, triggering significant pushback from political leaders across the city and state.

Mamdani's first proposal would raise income and corporate taxes on the city's wealthiest residents and corporations, a move that requires approval from Governor Kathy Hochul. His alternative proposal would increase property taxes by 9.5%, which could affect more than 3 million single-family homes, co-ops, and condos, along with over 100,000 commercial buildings.

"The first path is the most sustainable and fairest: raising taxes on the wealthiest and corporations, and ending the drain by fixing the imbalance between what the City provides the State and what we receive in return," Mamdani said. He warned that rejecting the income tax approach would force the city down "a more harmful path of property taxes and raiding our reserves."

The city faces a budget deficit expected to grow to 7 billion dollars over the next two years, up from 5.4 billion dollars. Hochul has pledged to contribute 1.5 billion dollars in state funding to help close the gap, but has ruled out approving income tax increases.

The property tax increase proposal has drawn swift criticism from city officials across the political spectrum. City Comptroller Mark Levine called it "a pretty extreme option," while City Council Speaker Julie Menin and Finance Committee Chair Linda Lee said raising property taxes during an affordability crisis is inappropriate. Queens Borough President Donovan Richards called a 9.5 percent property tax increase "a nonstarter," saying the city should not "balance our budget on the backs of working-class New Yorkers, especially seniors on fixed incomes."

Republican Council Member David Carr characterized raising property taxes as "among the worst ideas imaginable," describing it as one of the most regressive taxes the city levies.

Governor Hochul told reporters she does not believe a property tax increase is necessary.

Mamdani's budget proposals come as he inherited a larger deficit than expected from his predecessor, Eric Adams. The proposals align with campaign promises to make top earners and corporations contribute more to fund ambitious programs including free childcare, free buses, and rent freezes for one million rent-stabilized apartments.

City Council Speaker Menin acknowledged that budget negotiations typically involve significant back-and-forth between the mayor and city council, but expressed hope for more coordinated discussions going forward. She told media outlets she has been engaging "very constructively" with Mamdani about the budget proposals.

The competing proposals are expected to trigger prolonged negotiations between city and state officials before final budget decisions are made.
