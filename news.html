1:06 AM ET

Ukraine and Moldova have launched formal accession negotiations with the European Union, marking what officials describe as a historic moment for both countries. Senior EU officials and ministers from both nations met in Luxembourg on Monday to begin substantive talks on the first cluster of EU requirements, covering rule of law and democracy.

The start of negotiations comes after a delay caused by Hungary's previous opposition. Viktor Orban, Hungary's prime minister, had blocked progress on the membership process despite a symbolic decision to open talks in June 2024. The talks moved forward after Hungary's government changed in April, allowing EU member states to reach unanimous agreement last Friday to proceed with negotiations.

Ukraine and Moldova both applied for EU membership shortly after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, and the EU granted them candidate status that same year. The formal start of substantive talks means both nations will now begin the lengthy process of adopting thousands of European laws and aligning their institutions with EU standards.

The accession process typically requires candidates to meet strict criteria across numerous policy areas. Opening the first cluster on rule of law and democracy creates a pathway for talks on other areas, including the single market, environmental policy, and economic and social matters. Each member state must ultimately approve any new EU additions.

EU leaders Ursula von der Leyen and Antonio Costa said in a joint statement that they praised "the determination, courage and hard work shown by both countries in advancing reforms, even in the face of immense challenges."

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy responded positively, saying: "Ukraine is doing what is necessary, and it is important that the EU is also keeping its word. The opening of the first cluster is significant political and moral support for our state and our people."

However, EU officials have expressed concern about the pace of reforms. Ukraine has completed only about 15 percent of reforms contained in a ten-point plan agreed in December with EU enlargement commissioner Marta Kos. The plan includes strengthening anti-corruption agencies and reforming judicial appointment processes.

EU officials believe that with sufficient effort, Ukraine could complete technical talks in approximately four years, though they acknowledge that membership ultimately remains a political decision.

Heather Grabbe, a former European Commission adviser on enlargement, said the opening of cluster one was significant but would also serve as a reality check. She noted that Ukraine cannot bypass the lengthy process of adopting and implementing EU laws on its territory.

Ukraine continues to face significant challenges, fighting Russia's ongoing invasion while simultaneously working to meet EU requirements. The country's authorities have made high-profile anti-corruption arrests that EU officials view as positive signs of commitment to reform, though progress on several key areas remains incomplete.

This crash represents one of the deadliest military aviation incidents in recent years. Military officials have not yet released information regarding the cause of the crash or the identities of the crew members killed. The Air Force is expected to launch a formal investigation to determine the factors that contributed to the disaster.

The service regularly conducts training and testing operations to ensure the bombers remain mission-ready and operationally effective.

The crash occurred during a period when the military continues to rely heavily on the B-52 platform for strategic deterrence and long-range strike capabilities. 3:28 AM ET

The Bank of Japan has lifted its short-term policy rate to 1 percent, up from 0.75 percent, marking the highest borrowing costs the country has seen since 1995. The decision comes as policymakers attempt to control inflation driven by the Iran conflict and its impact on global energy markets.

The quarter-point rate increase reflects the central bank's concern that companies are passing rising oil costs along the supply chain at a relatively fast pace. Despite recent peace negotiations between Washington and Tehran, and despite Japan's core inflation falling to a four-year low of 1.4 percent in April, the Bank of Japan proceeded with the hike. Central bank governor Shinichi Uchida acknowledged that a U.S.-Iran peace memorandum was welcome, but emphasized uncertainty about how quickly oil supplies would recover.

"With underlying inflation approaching 2 percent, it's important to ensure we achieve our target stably," Uchida said at a Tokyo press conference. He noted that while the risk of sharp economic deterioration from the Middle East conflict had diminished, "price rises are broadening, and there is a risk that underlying inflation may deviate from our target."

The rate rise represents a significant shift in Japanese monetary policy. For decades, the Bank of Japan maintained near-zero or negative interest rates to stimulate its economy and combat deflation following the asset bubble that burst in the late 1980s. The central bank began raising rates in 2024, but this latest increase marks a substantial step away from the ultra-loose policies that had long defined Japan's approach.

The move attracted some market relief, as analysts had speculated the increase could have been larger. A 50-basis-point hike had been considered possible. Investment strategist Susannah Streeter noted that while the increase was widely anticipated, it still represents "a step-change in monetary policy for Japan."

The rate increase comes as the Bank of Japan joins other major central banks responding to war-related inflation. The European Central Bank raised its rates last week, while the U.S. Federal Reserve and Bank of England are expected to maintain their current rates at upcoming meetings.

The Japanese stock market reacted positively to the news. The Nikkei share index reached 70,000 points for the first time during Tuesday's trading session, having surged roughly one-third so far this year.

Higher Japanese interest rates carry global implications. Japan's traditionally low rates had made the yen attractive for carry trades, where investors borrow cheaply in one currency to invest in higher-yielding assets elsewhere. Rate increases could prompt shifts in these investment patterns and affect borrowing costs worldwide.

Yesterday, 7:21 PM ET

The San Andreas Fault has reached its highest stress level in a thousand years, according to a study from the University of Hawaii. The research marks a significant finding for earthquake preparedness efforts along one of the most seismically active fault lines in the United States.

The San Andreas Fault system extends roughly 800 miles through California, forming the boundary between the Pacific and North American tectonic plates. The fault has produced some of the most powerful earthquakes in California history, including the 1906 San Francisco earthquake that killed thousands and the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake that caused widespread damage across the Bay Area.

Stress accumulation along fault lines occurs as tectonic plates grind past each other, building pressure over time until it releases in the form of an earthquake. Scientists monitor these stress levels to better understand seismic risk and potential earthquake timing. The University of Hawaii study indicates that current stress levels exceed any measured over the past millennium, though the research does not predict when the next major earthquake might occur.

The finding carries significant implications for California, where more than 39 million residents live in proximity to the fault system. Major urban centers including Los Angeles, San Francisco, and San Diego sit near or on top of fault segments. Previous major earthquakes along the San Andreas Fault have caused catastrophic damage, and seismologists have long warned that the region remains overdue for another large seismic event.

Earthquake preparedness remains a priority for California state and local governments, which have invested in building code improvements, early warning systems, and public education campaigns. The elevated stress levels documented in the University of Hawaii study underscore the ongoing seismic threat facing California communities and the importance of continued investment in earthquake resilience infrastructure.

The Bank of Japan has lifted its short-term policy rate to 1 percent, up from 0.75 percent, marking the highest borrowing costs the country has seen since 1995. The decision comes as policymakers attempt to control inflation driven by the Iran conflict and its impact on global energy markets.

The quarter-point rate increase reflects the central bank's concern that companies are passing rising oil costs along the supply chain at a relatively fast pace. Despite recent peace negotiations between Washington and Tehran, and despite Japan's core inflation falling to a four-year low of 1.4 percent in April, the Bank of Japan proceeded with the hike. Central bank governor Shinichi Uchida acknowledged that a U.S.-Iran peace memorandum was welcome, but emphasized uncertainty about how quickly oil supplies would recover.

"With underlying inflation approaching 2 percent, it's important to ensure we achieve our target stably," Uchida said at a Tokyo press conference. He noted that while the risk of sharp economic deterioration from the Middle East conflict had diminished, "price rises are broadening, and there is a risk that underlying inflation may deviate from our target."

The rate rise represents a significant shift in Japanese monetary policy. For decades, the Bank of Japan maintained near-zero or negative interest rates to stimulate its economy and combat deflation following the asset bubble that burst in the late 1980s. The central bank began raising rates in 2024, but this latest increase marks a substantial step away from the ultra-loose policies that had long defined Japan's approach.

The move attracted some market relief, as analysts had speculated the increase could have been larger. A 50-basis-point hike had been considered possible. Investment strategist Susannah Streeter noted that while the increase was widely anticipated, it still represents "a step-change in monetary policy for Japan."

The rate increase comes as the Bank of Japan joins other major central banks responding to war-related inflation. The European Central Bank raised its rates last week, while the U.S. Federal Reserve and Bank of England are expected to maintain their current rates at upcoming meetings.

The Japanese stock market reacted positively to the news. The Nikkei share index reached 70,000 points for the first time during Tuesday's trading session, having surged roughly one-third so far this year.

Higher Japanese interest rates carry global implications. Japan's traditionally low rates had made the yen attractive for carry trades, where investors borrow cheaply in one currency to invest in higher-yielding assets elsewhere. Rate increases could prompt shifts in these investment patterns and affect borrowing costs worldwide.
