5:33 PM ET

The House of Representatives approved legislation providing $70 billion to fund immigration enforcement agencies through the remainder of President Trump's term, resolving a months-long funding dispute that had previously forced the Department of Homeland Security to shut down operations.

The Secure America Act passed in a 214-212 vote largely along party lines. All Democrats opposed the measure, as did independent Kevin Kiley who aligns with Republicans. The Senate had approved the bill the previous week.

The funding allocates $38 billion to Immigration and Customs Enforcement, $26 billion to Customs and Border Protection, and $5 billion to the Department of Homeland Security overall. The money extends through September 2029. The legislation now awaits President Trump's signature.

Republicans argued the bill was necessary to support border security and immigration law enforcement operations. The GOP caucus continued to back Trump's immigration agenda throughout the debate.

Democrats raised concerns about the legislation's lack of congressional oversight mechanisms. They argued that Congress had surrendered its oversight role by providing such extensive funding without adequate accountability measures in place.

The extended funding impasse had lasted for months as lawmakers debated both the scope of immigration enforcement spending and the level of congressional oversight required. The passage of this bill ended that standoff.

The approval of the measure highlights the deep political divide over immigration policy between the two parties. Republicans characterized the funding as essential to securing the border and enforcing immigration law. Democrats maintained their position that the bill failed to include sufficient safeguards to ensure accountability.

The bill's passage in the House came after similar approval in the Senate, setting the stage for Trump's expected signature. The legislation represents a significant financial commitment to immigration enforcement operations moving forward.

The vote reflects the current composition of Congress, where Republicans hold enough seats to pass legislation along party lines with minimal Democratic support. The narrow 214-212 margin demonstrates the contentious nature of the debate, with even one Republican-aligned independent joining Democrats in opposition.

This funding decision occurs as immigration enforcement has become a central focus of the Trump administration's policy agenda. The resources will support ongoing operations by ICE and Border Patrol through the remainder of Trump's presidential term.

The resolution of the funding impasse removes an obstacle that had created uncertainty for immigration enforcement agencies over an extended period. With the bill now moving toward the president for signature, these agencies can operate with clear funding authorization through 2029.

Social Security retirement benefits face 22 percent cut by 2032 without congressional action

If Congress does not intervene before the fund depletes, beneficiaries could experience an automatic reduction of roughly 22 percent in their...

4:31 PM ET

The trust fund that finances Social Security retirement benefits for approximately 68 million Americans is expected to run out of money by 2032, one year sooner than previously estimated, according to a report from the program's trustees. If Congress does not intervene before the fund depletes, beneficiaries could experience an automatic reduction of roughly 22 percent in their monthly benefit payments.

The accelerated timeline reflects mounting financial strain on Social Security as the baby boomer generation continues retiring and the ratio of working people paying into the system declines relative to retirees receiving benefits. The program depends on payroll taxes collected from current workers to fund payments to retirees. This demographic shift has created a growing gap between money coming in and money going out.

Social Security has served as a fundamental source of retirement income for millions of Americans since its creation in 1935. The program has consistently paid scheduled benefits throughout its history, but the projected shortfall signals an urgent challenge ahead. If the trust fund becomes depleted, benefits would not vanish entirely. Instead, payments would be limited to revenue from incoming payroll taxes, triggering the automatic cuts.

Congress has multiple options to address the funding gap. Lawmakers could raise the payroll tax rate, increase the income cap that payroll taxes apply to, reduce benefits for higher earners, raise the retirement age, or pursue a combination of these approaches. Addressing Social Security remains politically challenging, however, since the program touches virtually every American family and proposed changes typically encounter resistance from various groups.

The trustees' report creates pressure on lawmakers to act within the next six years to avert automatic benefit reductions. Earlier projections had suggested Congress had until 2033 to resolve the funding issue, but the accelerated timeline narrows the window for legislative action. The sooner depletion date increases the urgency for policymakers to reach a bipartisan agreement that would stabilize the program's finances while protecting benefits for both current and future beneficiaries.

The situation demands that Congress balance several competing interests. Younger workers worry about whether benefits will exist when they retire. Current retirees depend on stable payments for daily living expenses. Businesses object to higher payroll taxes, while higher earners may resist benefit reductions or tax increases. Finding common ground across these groups requires difficult political negotiations.

Experts emphasize that waiting longer makes any solution more painful. Early action allows for gradual adjustments spread over many years, while delayed action forces more dramatic changes concentrated in a shorter timeframe. The trustees' report serves as a warning that Congress cannot indefinitely postpone addressing Social Security's structural challenges. The clock is ticking toward a decision point that will shape retirement security for millions of Americans.

NASA has announced the crew of four astronauts selected for the Artemis III mission, marking a major milestone in the agency's plan to return humans to the moon. The crew will launch in 2027 for a two-week mission to low Earth orbit that will test critical systems before a planned lunar landing on Artemis IV in 2028.

The Artemis III crew consists of NASA astronaut and commander Randy Bresnik, European Space Agency pilot Luca Parmitano from Italy, and NASA mission specialists Frank Rubio and Andre Douglas. The all-male crew will practice docking their Orion capsule with lunar landers developed by private companies SpaceX and Blue Origin during their orbital mission.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman described the group as part of "Earth's first starfleet" as he unveiled the crew and detailed the next stages of the agency's moon program. The Artemis III mission represents a significant step forward in testing the systems and procedures needed for eventual human landings on the lunar surface.

The Artemis program has already achieved historic milestones. The recent Artemis II mission, which launched earlier this year, completed a record-breaking journey around the moon. That crewed flight surpassed the maximum distance achieved by Apollo 13, traveling more than 250,000 miles from Earth. The four astronauts on Artemis II were the first lunar travelers since Apollo 17 in 1972, and the mission included historic achievements including sending the first person of color, the first woman, and the first non-American on a lunar mission.

During their five-day journey around the moon, the Artemis II crew experienced remarkable moments. Commander Reid Wiseman and his crewmates captured stunning images of Earth and witnessed the lunar far side from closer than any humans since the Apollo program. Despite the spectacular views, the mission also dealt with practical challenges, including a malfunctioning toilet that caused problems throughout the flight.

The Artemis program uses NASA's Space Launch System rocket and Orion spacecraft, representing years of development following significant delays and cost overruns. The Space Launch System finally achieved its inaugural crewed flight with Artemis II after being rolled back to its hangar for repairs multiple times before launch.

Future Artemis missions will focus on the lunar south pole region, an area of scientific interest that differs from the Apollo landing sites near the moon's equator. The south pole contains permanently shadowed craters that may hold water ice, a resource that could support future exploration and establish a permanent lunar base.

The progression of Artemis missions demonstrates NASA's commitment to a multi-decade exploration program involving international partners and commercial companies. These missions will test capabilities needed for longer-duration stays on the moon and eventual missions beyond Earth orbit, laying the groundwork for human exploration of Mars.

By converting portions of a player's base salary into signing bonuses or adjusting payment schedules, teams can spread cap charges across multiple years while maintaining the overall value of the contract. The Bengals' decision to restructure Burrow's deal suggests they are looking to make additional roster moves or retain current players.

Burrow signed a five-year contract extension with Cincinnati in September 2023 worth $275 million, making him one of the highest-paid players in the league at the time. The $10 million in freed cap space could be used in several ways. The Bengals may look to sign free agents, extend current players whose contracts are expiring, or simply maintain roster flexibility throughout the season to address injuries or other needs that arise. Teams often restructure veteran contracts during the offseason to create room for draft picks or to retain key contributors.

The restructure does not reduce the total amount Burrow will earn over the life of his contract. Instead, it adjusts when the cap charges hit the team's books, allowing Cincinnati to better manage its financial commitments in the short term while potentially creating larger cap obligations in future years. As teams across the league navigate an expanding salary cap, such roster management techniques have become standard practice for contending franchises looking to maximize their competitive windows.

This move comes as the Bengals continue efforts to construct a competitive roster around their star quarterback. Cincinnati has invested significant salary cap resources in their passing attack, with Burrow and receivers Ja'Marr Chase and Tee Higgins representing three of the team's largest cap commitments. The restructuring of Burrow's contract demonstrates how modern NFL teams use financial tools to balance immediate roster construction with long-term planning.
