A federal judge has blocked a proposed restriction on mail-in voting across the United States, preventing the Postal Service from implementing a plan that would have denied ballots to voters in states refusing to turn over their voter rolls to the federal government.

Judge Emmet Sullivan of the US District Court for the District of Columbia ruled against the restriction, marking the second time that a plan to limit mail ballots across the country has faced a court setback.

The blocked restriction stemmed from an order targeting mail-in voting access. Civil rights groups, including the NAACP, challenged the proposed changes in court, arguing that the Postal Service directive would create barriers to voting. Multiple organizations joined the legal challenge, contending that the restrictions would make it harder for Americans to cast ballots by mail, particularly affecting communities that depend heavily on mail-in voting.

Mail-in voting has become a significant method of casting ballots in recent election cycles, with millions of Americans relying on the option. Voting rights advocates had opposed the proposed changes, saying they would create unnecessary obstacles for voters without good cause.

The judge's order keeps current mail ballot handling procedures in place while the case continues through the courts. The ruling ensures that existing practices for delivering mail ballots remain unchanged for now, preventing the implementation of the proposed restrictions.

The decision represents a significant setback for efforts to limit mail-in voting access. Judge Sullivan found sufficient grounds to block the restriction, siding with those who argued the changes would harm voting access.

This case is part of a broader legal battle over voting access and election administration that has intensified in recent years. As states prepare for upcoming elections, courts continue to weigh competing claims about voting methods and election security. The block demonstrates ongoing judicial skepticism toward restrictions on mail-in voting, with multiple courts now rejecting similar proposals.

The outcome comes amid heightened focus on election procedures and voting access. Legal challenges to voting restrictions have become common in the current political environment, with courts frequently called upon to determine the constitutionality of various election administration policies.

The Postal Service has not yet announced whether it will appeal the ruling. If appealed, the case could move to higher courts, potentially reaching the appellate level. The decision ensures that the current mail ballot system continues operating under existing rules while legal proceedings advance.

The ruling highlights how courts are actively shaping election policy through litigation. As voting access remains a contested issue, this decision will likely influence other ongoing legal battles over mail-in voting procedures and voter eligibility requirements across the country.

9:07 PM ET

NASA is preparing a mission to save the Swift telescope, which has been studying gamma-ray bursts and other cosmic phenomena since 2004. The spacecraft has begun sinking toward Earth as its orbit decays, prompting the agency to plan a rendezvous mission that would boost the telescope back to a higher, more stable orbit.

The rescue effort, dubbed the Swift Boost mission, represents a rare attempt to physically reposition an aging space telescope rather than allowing it to fall back into the atmosphere. The mission combines scientific preservation with an opportunity to test techniques that could have applications for national defense satellites. NASA has characterized the operation as a daring maneuver that will require precise coordination in orbit.

Swift has operated for more than two decades beyond its original five-year mission timeline, making significant contributions to astronomy by detecting and studying gamma-ray bursts, which are among the most energetic events in the universe. The telescope's orbital altitude has gradually decreased over time due to atmospheric drag, a common phenomenon for spacecraft in low Earth orbit. Without intervention, the telescope would eventually reenter the atmosphere and be destroyed.

The planned boost mission will need to be executed before the telescope's orbit decays to a point where rescue becomes impossible. NASA officials have not disclosed a specific launch date for the mission but have indicated they are working under time constraints. The operation will test capabilities that could prove valuable for extending the lives of other satellites or repositioning them as needed.

The rescue effort reflects NASA's broader strategy of maximizing the scientific return from existing space assets. Extending Swift's operational life would allow continued observations that complement newer telescopes and provide valuable long-term data sets. The mission also offers a practical demonstration of orbital servicing techniques that the agency and the Department of Defense have been developing for future applications.

Victor Willis, the original lead singer of the Village People and co-writer of the disco anthem Y.M.C.A., has died at 74. The group announced that Willis passed away on Monday, June 30, 2026, following "a short but aggressive illness."

Willis joined the Village People in 1977 after being discovered by French producer Jacques Morali while performing on Broadway. Morali had conceived the group as a concept band based on American stereotypes, telling his lawyer: "We have an idea to put together a very special group, very American and very happy." Willis performed as a naval officer or policeman as part of the band's distinctive costume concept.

As the group's frontman during its peak years, Willis helped create some of disco's most enduring hits. The 1978 single Y.M.C.A. became a global phenomenon, reaching number one in 17 countries. The band followed with other major successes including Macho Man and In the Navy. Despite widespread interpretation of these songs as gay anthems, Willis refuted this characterization and threatened legal action against news organizations making such claims. "That is a false assumption based on the fact that my writing partner was gay, and some (not all) of Village People were gay," he stated in 2024.

Willis left the Village People in 1980 as the group prepared for the poorly received feature film Can't Stop the Music. He briefly returned for the 1982 album Fox on the Box before departing again the following year. A solo album he recorded in 1979 remained unreleased until 2015.

After leaving the music industry, Willis struggled with substance abuse and legal troubles for decades. In 1993, he was charged with rape and assault but was acquitted. He faced multiple drug and firearms arrests beginning in 2005, including possession of cocaine and a handgun. Following probation violations and a string of offenses, a judge granted him leniency in 2006, citing his "potential which remains untapped," and sent him to the Betty Ford rehabilitation clinic in California.

Willis maintained a complicated relationship with Y.M.C.A.'s adoption at political rallies. He initially approved its use for Donald Trump's 2020 presidential campaign but retracted his support as the Black Lives Matter movement gained prominence. He later reversed his position again, acknowledging that Trump's use of the track had "greatly benefited the song," even as he stated his ideological opposition to the president and his support for Kamala Harris in 2024. The Village People performed at Trump's second inauguration events in 2025.

Willis was born on July 1, 1951, in Texas and grew up singing in his father's Baptist church. He met his future wife, actress Phylicia Rashad, while both starred in the Broadway musical The Wiz. They were married from 1978 to the early 1980s.

The Environmental Protection Agency approved three new pesticides this week that contain molecules with carbon-fluorine bonds, which can be difficult to break down over time. The approvals have raised questions about whether these chemicals fall under international definitions of forever chemicals, though the EPA disputes that characterization.

Carbon-fluorine bonds are known for their strength and resistance to breakdown in the environment. The agency's approval of pesticides containing these molecular structures comes as concerns about persistent chemicals in the environment continue to grow nationwide.

The EPA has pushed back against labeling these newly approved pesticides as forever chemicals. The agency's position reflects an ongoing dispute over how these substances should be classified and regulated under current environmental standards.

The three pesticides join a growing number of chemical products approved for agricultural and commercial use despite questions about their long-term environmental persistence. The approvals were issued by the Trump administration's EPA.

Forever chemicals, formally known as PFAS, have become a significant environmental concern because they do not easily break down in nature and can accumulate in water supplies, soil, and human bodies over time. International definitions of these substances often include any chemicals with strong carbon-fluorine bonds, which makes them resistant to natural degradation processes.
