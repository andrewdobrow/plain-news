The Senate Judiciary Committee is scheduled to vote Tuesday on Todd Blanche's nomination to serve as attorney general, following a significant development that cleared obstacles within the Republican caucus.

Blanche, who currently holds the position as acting attorney general, provided written assurance to two Republican senators that a planned $1.8 billion fund intended to compensate Trump supporters would be abandoned. This commitment satisfied Republican Sens. John Cornyn of Texas and Thom Tillis of North Carolina, who had previously threatened to block his confirmation.

The fund in question, described as an "anti-weaponization fund," had become a sticking point during negotiations. By agreeing in writing to drop the initiative, Blanche secured the support of the two senators whose opposition could have derailed his nomination in committee.

However, the path to confirmation remains uncertain despite this breakthrough. Several other Republican senators have not announced their positions on the nomination. Sen. Susan Collins of Maine, a centrist Republican facing a competitive reelection campaign in a Democratic-leaning state, has not disclosed how she will vote.

Democratic senators have raised concerns about Blanche's record. Sen. Dick Durbin and Rep. Robert Garcia criticized his handling of matters related to the Epstein case, specifically his role in the Epstein files and involvement in moving Ghislaine Maxwell to a minimum-security prison. Garcia called Blanche "too corrupt and untrustworthy" for the position and stated that Democrats would continue opposing the nomination.

The committee vote represents a procedural milestone in the confirmation process. If Blanche advances from the committee, his nomination would proceed to the full Senate floor for a vote by all 100 senators. The timing of that floor vote would depend on Senate leadership decisions.

Blanche's path to permanent confirmation has required navigating competing priorities within the Republican conference. His status as Trump's former personal lawyer drew scrutiny throughout the process, adding another layer of complexity to the proceedings.

Even as the committee prepares for Tuesday's vote, questions persist about whether Blanche has secured enough support beyond Cornyn and Tillis. The positions of other undecided Republicans could prove decisive in determining whether he reaches the 50-vote threshold needed for Senate confirmation, particularly if any Republicans ultimately oppose him on the floor.

The nomination underscores the continued division within Republican ranks over key judicial and executive branch appointments, despite the party's Senate majority.

The escalation comes amid shifting military momentum in the conflict. Ukraine has moved from requesting international defense support to offering other nations expertise in countering attacks, leveraging domestically developed drone technology. Ukrainian long-range strikes have disrupted energy and manufacturing facilities inside Russia, with three Russian regions reporting strikes on Wednesday.

Along the 780-mile frontline, Russia's military advance has slowed each month since October, according to the Institute for the Study of War. Moscow's spring offensive has stalled, and the Russian army recorded a net loss of territory last month for the first time since 2024. The thinktank noted that "Ukrainian defensive lines are holding, and Ukrainian forces have managed to contest the tactical initiative in several areas even as Russia continues to lose disproportionate amounts of manpower to achieve minimal gains."

This latest assault comes despite US President Donald Trump's recent claims that the war could end soon. Medicaid work requirements exclude homeless people from exemptions

5:00 AM ET

Work requirements for Medicaid recipients have rolled out across multiple states, but homeless individuals were notably excluded from the federal exemption list, putting a vulnerable population at risk of losing health coverage.

Supporters of work requirements have argued for years that the most vulnerable groups would receive protections from these rules. The federal government created a list of exempted populations that includes pregnant women, students, and people with disabilities. However, homeless people did not make that list of automatic exemptions, creating significant practical problems for individuals already facing substantial barriers to employment.

Health advocates and policy experts point to concrete obstacles that homeless people encounter when trying to meet work requirements. Without stable housing, individuals often lack reliable access to transportation, mail delivery, and the identification documents needed to prove they are working or qualify for exemptions. These administrative hurdles can lead to coverage loss even when someone is actually employed or should be protected under existing rules.

The Medicaid program serves millions of low-income Americans, and work requirements represent a significant shift in how states manage the program. In recent years, several states have put these rules in place or sought federal approval to do so. Generally, these programs require non-elderly adults without disabilities to work, volunteer, or participate in job training for a specified number of hours each month to maintain their Medicaid eligibility.

Removing homeless people from the exemption list has serious health consequences. Homeless populations experience higher rates of chronic illness, mental health conditions, and substance abuse disorders compared to the general population. Without Medicaid coverage, these individuals often turn to emergency rooms for basic medical care or skip treatment entirely. This can worsen existing health problems and drive up overall healthcare expenses when conditions deteriorate and require emergency intervention.

The practical impact on homeless communities remains significant. Many people experiencing homelessness struggle with conditions that make maintaining employment difficult, yet they receive no automatic protection under the new work requirement rules. Documentation requirements create additional hurdles, as homeless individuals may lack the stable addresses or contact information that verification systems typically require.

Healthcare providers and homeless services organizations have expressed concern about the coverage losses that could result. The overlap between homelessness and health vulnerabilities means that losing Medicaid access can have cascading negative effects on individual and public health outcomes.

As more states implement these requirements, the exclusion of homeless people from protections highlights a gap in how policymakers designed the exemption framework. Whether this gap was intentional or an oversight, the result is the same: a population facing significant health risks and practical employment barriers now has no guaranteed protection under Medicaid work requirement rules.

US States Sue to Block Trump Tariffs on 60 Trading Partners

A coalition of 25 US states filed a lawsuit Monday against the Trump administration to block tariffs ranging from 10% to 12.5% on goods from 60 trading partners. The states argue the tariffs are an illegal attempt to circumvent import duties that the Supreme Court struck down in February. The states are requesting that the US Court of International Trade halt the tariffs, decl...

5:08 AM ET

A coalition of 25 US states filed a lawsuit Monday against the Trump administration to block tariffs ranging from 10% to 12.5% on goods from 60 trading partners. The states argue the tariffs are an illegal attempt to circumvent import duties that the Supreme Court struck down in February.

The states are requesting that the US Court of International Trade halt the tariffs, declare them unlawful, and order refunds for duties already collected. According to the lawsuit, the tariffs on 59 countries and the European Union account for 99.4% of total US imports.

The states involved in the action include New York, Arizona, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Hawaii, Illinois, Massachusetts, Maryland, Maine, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico, North Carolina, Oregon, Rhode Island, Virginia, Vermont, and Washington, along with the governors of Kentucky and Pennsylvania.

The Trump administration justified the tariffs by claiming that the affected countries have not done enough to prevent the importation of goods produced through forced labor. The new tariffs went into effect in July after temporary tariffs expired and the earlier "Liberation Day" tariffs were ruled unconstitutional.

"After losing at the Supreme Court, the administration is once again trying to illegally raise taxes on families and businesses with a new round of tariffs," said New York Attorney General Letitia James. "No matter how the administration tries to justify it, the law and our Constitution are clear that the president does not have the power to impose sweeping tariffs on whatever countries he wants."

The latest tariffs use Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, a federal law designed to target countries that use forced labor. Major trading partners affected include the UK, Australia, Canada, Japan, Taiwan, and China.

The White House defended the tariffs as a necessary response to unfair trade practices. "The United States is using its lawful authority to obtain the elimination of unreasonable acts, policies, and practices that burden US commerce," White House spokesman Kush Desai said. He added that countries' failure to impose and enforce prohibitions on forced labor imports is unreasonable and must be addressed.

New York Governor Kathy Hochul argued the administration is using forced labor as a cover for broader tariff policies. "President Trump's illegal tariffs are nothing more than a tax on hardworking families, driving up the cost of groceries, household essentials, building materials, and countless everyday goods that New Yorkers rely on," Hochul said.

The lawsuit follows a separate legal challenge filed by the Liberty Justice Center on behalf of two US small businesses claiming Trump exceeded his executive authority. The case will move through the federal court system, with legal experts divided on whether the administration has exceeded its trade powers.

A few minutes ago

Microsoft has issued a warning about a Russian hacking group targeting travelers through compromised hotel Wi-Fi networks. The operation, which Microsoft calls CaptiveCrunch, is attributed to Midnight Blizzard, a group believed to be linked to Russian intelligence services.

The attack exploits hotel Wi-Fi infrastructure to intercept guests when they connect to the network. Hackers redirect users to fake login pages designed to steal usernames and passwords, and may attempt to install malware on travelers' devices. By compromising the router itself, attackers can redirect users to fraudulent sites and potentially access other devices connected to the network, such as phones and computers.

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre has warned about similar operations targeting commonly sold internet routers. According to the agency, these attacks follow a pattern where actors target a wide pool of victims before filtering for users of potential intelligence value. Experts note that edge devices like routers and security cameras often represent weak points in network security because they frequently lack updates and proper monitoring.

"Edge devices are quite often forgotten about, and they can become a weak point," said Alan Woodward, a professor at the University of Security at the University of Surrey. Once attackers access a router, they can redirect users to fake sites and establish themselves on networks to probe for vulnerabilities in connected devices.

The group behind the attacks is probably APT28, also known as Fancy Bear, which was almost certainly linked to Russian intelligence services. APT28 was responsible for cyber-attacks on the German parliament in 2015, when large amounts of data including confidential emails and schedules of German MPs were stolen.

The warning highlights growing concerns about the security of internet routers. The US Federal Communications Commission has recently banned the sale of all consumer-grade routers made outside the United States, stating that foreign-made routers pose unacceptable risks to national security. The agency noted that malicious actors have exploited security gaps in foreign-made routers to attack American households, disrupt networks, and facilitate espionage and intellectual property theft.

However, privacy experts caution that an outright ban may not fully address vulnerabilities in existing routers already in use. A more significant problem is that many internet routers currently in service are at the end of their operational lives and no longer receive security updates.

Microsoft recommends that travelers use virtual private networks when connecting to hotel Wi-Fi and avoid entering sensitive credentials on public networks. The company also suggests enabling multi-factor authentication and remaining cautious of unexpected login prompts. Organizations with employees who travel frequently should update their security policies to address the heightened risk of compromised hotel networks. For small businesses and individuals, experts recommend keeping routers updated and monitoring networks for unusual activity.

Spotify's premium subscriber base grew to 300 million in the second quarter of 2026 after adding 7 million paying customers, surpassing the company's earlier projections for the quarter. The streaming service reported 777 million total monthly active users across both its paid and free tiers as of the end of Q2, slightly below the company's guidance of 778 million.

The milestone underscores Spotify's continued expansion of its premium tier, which remains the primary source of revenue for the company compared to its free, advertising-supported service.

Beyond subscriber growth, Spotify is pursuing new revenue opportunities through artificial intelligence. The company has partnered with Universal Music Group on a licensing agreement that will enable subscribers to create song covers and remixes using AI technology. This marks the first instance of Spotify allowing listeners to generate content through artificial intelligence on its platform.

The feature will likely be offered as a paid add-on within Spotify's app. Premium users will gain access to remix tools and the ability to create AI-generated covers of songs from participating artists. Spotify indicated that the tool could generate additional income for artists and songwriters beyond their regular royalty payments. However, the companies did not disclose the financial specifics of the deal or identify which artists would participate.

Universal Music Group's roster includes major artists such as Taylor Swift, Ariana Grande, and Billie Eilish, though specific participation details remain unclear. Alex Norström, co-chief executive of Spotify, described the initiative as addressing "hard problems for music." He emphasized that the approach is grounded in "consent, credit and compensation for the artists and songwriters that take part."

Lucian Grainge, chief executive of Universal Music, framed the collaboration as supporting "human artistry" while creating "additional revenue opportunities for artists and songwriters." He noted that the initiative represents a responsible approach to AI that will benefit the entire music ecosystem.

The partnership reflects Spotify's broader strategy to expand beyond traditional music subscriptions and integrate artificial intelligence into its services. In May, the company launched a beta feature allowing AI agents to create personalized podcast briefings for individual users. These daily briefings remain private to each listener.

The music industry continues to grapple with concerns about AI, particularly regarding copyright and the role of artificially generated music. In response, Spotify introduced a verification system in April to help users distinguish between human artists and AI-generated content. The company's "Verified by Spotify" badge, marked with a green checkmark, appears on artist profiles and search results. According to Spotify, the badge indicates that a music profile has been reviewed and meets the platform's authenticity standards.

The verification effort addresses what Spotify characterizes as an essential concern in the AI era: the ability to trust the authenticity of the music listeners encounter on the platform.

By compromising the router itself, attackers can redirect users to fraudulent sites and potentially access other devices connected to the network, such as phones and computers.

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre has warned about similar operations targeting commonly sold internet routers. According to the agency, these attacks follow a pattern where actors target a wide pool of victims before filtering for users of potential intelligence value. Experts note that edge devices like routers and security cameras often represent weak points in network security because they frequently lack updates and proper monitoring.

"Edge devices are quite often forgotten about, and they can become a weak point," said Alan Woodward, a professor at the University of Security at the University of Surrey. Once attackers access a router, they can redirect users to fake sites and establish themselves on networks to probe for vulnerabilities in connected devices.

The group behind the attacks is probably APT28, also known as Fancy Bear, which was almost certainly linked to Russian intelligence services. APT28 was responsible for cyber-attacks on the German parliament in 2015, when large amounts of data including confidential emails and schedules of German MPs were stolen.

The warning highlights growing concerns about the security of internet routers. The US Federal Communications Commission has recently banned the sale of all consumer-grade routers made outside the United States, stating that foreign-made routers pose unacceptable risks to national security. The agency noted that malicious actors have exploited security gaps in foreign-made routers to attack American households, disrupt networks, and facilitate espionage and intellectual property theft.

However, privacy experts caution that an outright ban may not fully address vulnerabilities in existing routers already in use. A more significant problem is that many internet routers currently in service are at the end of their operational lives and no longer receive security updates.

Microsoft recommends that travelers use virtual private networks when connecting to hotel Wi-Fi and avoid entering sensitive credentials on public networks.
