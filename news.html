The Senate voted early Friday morning to pass a $69.5 billion budget reconciliation package to fund immigration enforcement operations through 2029. The legislation passed 52-47 following an overnight session that included contentious debates over amendments.

The bill represents a significant expansion of resources for immigration enforcement and border security operations over the next three years. The measure now moves to the House for final passage before President Donald Trump can sign it into law.

The contentious debate centered on a proposed $1.8 billion anti-weaponization fund that the Trump administration intends to create to compensate political allies who claim they were prosecuted by the Biden-era Justice Department. Several Republicans objected to the fund and worked to block it through amendments.

During the marathon voting session, six Republican senators broke ranks with their party to support an amendment sponsored by Senator Bill Cassidy of Louisiana designed to block the fund. Those senators included Susan Collins of Maine, Lisa Murkowski of Alaska, Dan Sullivan of Alaska, and Joni Ernst of Iowa.

Cassidy spent considerable time during the overnight session perfecting the language of his amendment to prevent the administration from establishing the compensation fund. He consulted with Senate Parliamentarian Elizabeth MacDonough to ensure the amendment could withstand procedural challenges.

The division revealed internal Republican disagreements over spending priorities and the anti-weaponization fund, yet party leadership maintained sufficient support for passage. Despite the splits, Republicans held together enough votes to pass the measure without Democratic support.

The funding package comes amid broader debates over immigration enforcement and departmental operations. Democrats had previously blocked a separate Department of Homeland Security funding bill, demanding reforms to Immigration and Customs Enforcement operations. Those proposed changes included requirements that agents wear identification and body cameras, as well as restrictions preventing them from operating near schools, medical facilities, churches, polling stations, childcare facilities, and courts.

Democrats also sought guarantees that local and state law enforcement agencies could investigate potential crimes committed by federal authorities, including excessive use of force. Republicans supported body cameras but resisted other proposals, arguing they would restrict agents' ability to conduct operations effectively.

Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer stated his party wanted "commonsense" changes in how immigration enforcement operates. "From day one, I've said unless there were really strong, meaningful reforms to rein in ICE and stop the violence, there will not be Democratic votes to fund ICE," Schumer said.

Republican leaders expressed confidence the House will pass the measure in coming days. The bill's passage represents a victory for Republican leadership despite the internal tensions revealed during the overnight voting marathon and amendments debate.

A few minutes ago

John Bolton, former national security adviser under President Donald Trump, has reached a plea agreement in federal court on charges of mishandling classified documents. Under the agreement, which is subject to court approval, Bolton will plead guilty to one count of illegal retention of sensitive national security information.

As part of the plea deal, Bolton must pay a $2.25 million fine. The sentencing guidelines allow for anywhere between no prison time and five years, though final sentencing will be determined by the court. Bolton is scheduled to appear in court on June 26 to formally enter his guilty plea.

The Justice Department filed the criminal charges against Bolton in October 2025. The case centers on notes and diary entries Bolton created during his tenure as national security adviser from April 2018 to September 2019. Prosecutors alleged he maintained classified materials on a computer in his Bethesda, Maryland home and in his Washington DC office from April 2018 through August 2025. Materials recovered during an August raid included briefings about weapons of mass destruction and intelligence related to foreign adversaries' leadership and foreign policy matters.

Bolton's legal team, led by attorney Abbe Lowell, has characterized the case as politically motivated. Lowell argued that the notes were personal memoirs rather than officially classified documents. The defense points to the timing of the prosecution and charges against other Trump critics, including former FBI director James Comey and New York attorney general Letitia James, as evidence of political targeting.

Bolton's legal troubles stem partly from his 2020 memoir, "The Room Where It Happened," which offered a critical behind-the-scenes account of his time in the Trump administration. The Justice Department initially sought to block the book's publication on national security grounds through a civil complaint in June 2020. The FBI subsequently investigated Bolton during the Biden administration regarding both the memoir and a 2021 foreign hack of his personal email.

Bolton left the Trump administration in September 2019 and later became a vocal critic of the former president. Trump has dismissed Bolton as a "bad guy," claiming he was unaware of the criminal charges filed against him.

The case reflects broader government efforts to enforce stricter protocols around classified information handling among senior officials. It also comes as Bolton's foreign policy predictions about Trump's second term have proven prescient, with the former adviser noting that more than half the world's countries currently lack US ambassadors and American foreign relationships have taken a backseat to Trump's personal priorities.

Yesterday, 8:03 PM ET

President Trump has announced federal support for the coal industry through multiple executive actions, framing the investments as necessary to strengthen domestic energy production and security.

During a White House ceremony, Trump signed an executive order directing the Department of Defense to purchase long-term power from coal plants serving military installations and other mission-critical facilities. "We're going to be buying a lot of coal through the military now," Trump said at the event, which included coal executives, miners, and administration officials including EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin and Interior Secretary Doug Burgess.

The Peabody Energy CEO James Grech presented Trump with an award from the Washington Coal Club during the ceremony. "We stand here today representing the thousands of coalminers across the country to express our deep gratitude to you, sir, for the actions you've taken to support our industry," Grech said.

Beyond the military purchasing agreement, Trump announced that the Department of Energy would allocate $175 million to six projects designed to upgrade and extend the lifespan of coal-fired power plants serving rural and remote communities in West Virginia, Ohio, North Carolina and Kentucky.

Trump framed the initiative within a broader energy agenda. "Under our leadership, we're becoming a massive energy exporter. We're lifting up our hard-working American miners like nobody has ever done before," he stated.

This action represents the latest administration effort to revive the struggling coal sector. In September, the White House announced it would open 13.1 million acres of public land to coal mining and provide $625 million for coal-fired power plants.

The coal industry has faced sustained decline over the past three decades. According to the Energy Information Administration, US coal production in 2023 was less than half its 2008 level. Coal plants generated only about 15 percent of US electricity in 2024, down from roughly 50 percent in 2000, as utilities have shifted toward natural gas and renewable energy sources.

The administration's coal investments come amid rising energy prices following military operations against Iran. The timing reflects broader administration priorities, with Trump receiving various gifts and honors from business leaders since taking office. In recent months, the president has granted business exemptions and trade considerations following such presentations.

The executive orders demonstrate Trump's willingness to use presidential authority to support industries aligned with his economic agenda, particularly sectors facing market pressures. The coal investments signal an administration commitment to domestic fossil fuel production despite the industry's long-term decline in US electricity generation.

Microsoft's artificial intelligence chief stated that Anthropic's AI models are too expensive for widespread use, according to remarks made at the company's Build 2026 developer conference. The comments come as Microsoft continues to expand its own AI offerings and partnerships, including its longstanding investment in OpenAI. The statement highlights growing tensions in the AI industry over pricing structures and model accessibility.

The criticism arrives as Anthropic has been experiencing rapid growth. The company announced that its annualized revenue crossed $47 billion in May, up sharply from roughly $9 billion at the end of 2025. Anthropic has positioned itself as a major competitor in the large language model market, offering what it describes as safer and more reliable AI systems. The company is preparing for an initial public offering.

Despite Microsoft's public criticism of Anthropic's pricing, the companies have significant business ties. Microsoft integrates Anthropic's AI tools into systems it provides to the US military and has joined other tech giants in supporting Anthropic's legal challenges against Pentagon decisions. In an amicus brief submitted to federal court, Microsoft argued that blocking Anthropic from government work would create serious disruption for suppliers whose products rely on the AI company's technology. Google, Amazon, Apple and OpenAI also signed onto the brief supporting Anthropic.

The Pentagon designated Anthropic as a supply-chain risk in response to failed contract negotiations over a $200 million deal to deploy Anthropic's AI on classified military systems. Anthropic had insisted its technology should not be used for mass surveillance of US citizens or to power autonomous lethal weapons. The company's leadership explained that Anthropic does not have confidence that its Claude model would function reliably or safely if used to support lethal autonomous warfare.

Microsoft used its Build 2026 conference to announce several AI-related initiatives, including Project Solara, an Android operating system designed for AI agents rather than traditional applications. The company also unveiled new tools for building AI applications using Microsoft Fabric and Microsoft databases. The conference focused on positioning Windows as a trusted platform for AI development.

The debate over AI model pricing has become increasingly significant as companies race to deploy artificial intelligence across their products and services. Higher costs for accessing advanced AI models can limit which businesses can afford to integrate them, potentially affecting the pace of AI adoption across different industries. Microsoft's own AI strategy has centered on making tools accessible to developers and enterprises through its Azure cloud platform.

The comments from Microsoft's AI leadership suggest the company may be steering customers toward its own AI solutions or those of partners like OpenAI, where Microsoft has invested billions of dollars. As competition intensifies in the AI sector, pricing and cost efficiency are becoming key differentiators alongside technical capabilities and model performance.

China Cracks Down on Micro Drama Content

Authorities are targeting soft porn, violence, and materialism in the popular short-form video format.

1:16 AM ET

Chinese authorities have initiated a crackdown on micro dramas, targeting content that features sexual material, violence, and materialistic themes. The short-form video format has surged in popularity but drawn criticism for sensationalist content.

Micro dramas are brief video series that have gained significant viewership in China. The government action reflects ongoing efforts to regulate online content and entertainment media. Authorities have not specified the exact enforcement measures or penalties for platforms hosting such content.
