A few minutes ago

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency has instructed US federal agencies to fix a virtual private network vulnerability within three days as ransomware attackers actively exploit it. Check Point reported that hackers have compromised dozens of organizations by taking advantage of flaws in several of its VPN products deployed across government networks.

CISA's emergency directive requires agencies to patch the vulnerability or disable the affected systems within 72 hours. The agency only issues such directives when vulnerabilities present immediate and serious risks to federal networks. This three-day timeline represents one of the shortest remediation windows CISA has established, underscoring how dangerous the current attacks are.

The vulnerability has already enabled unauthorized access to both government and private sector systems. Check Point's security products are widely used throughout federal agencies for remote access capabilities, which makes this flaw particularly concerning for national cybersecurity.

Ransomware groups have increasingly used VPN vulnerabilities as their primary way to breach networks. By exploiting weaknesses in remote access systems, attackers gain initial entry to networks. From there, they can spread laterally through connected systems, extract sensitive data, and deploy encryption tools that lock organizations out of their own files until they pay ransom demands.

The extensive deployment of Check Point products across government agencies means the vulnerability could affect far more than individual systems. It potentially threatens entire networks across federal government infrastructure. Agencies now face pressure to balance the urgent need to patch systems against the operational challenges that may occur if they take VPN systems offline during remediation.

The emergency directive reflects the serious nature of the threat currently unfolding. Federal cybersecurity officials determined that the active attacks and widespread vulnerability required immediate action rather than a standard patching timeline. By compressing the response window to just 72 hours, CISA signaled that waiting could result in significant additional breaches across government networks.

Organizations using Check Point VPN products will need to prioritize this patch deployment. The combination of active exploitation, government-wide deployment, and the vulnerability's role in enabling ransomware attacks creates a critical situation that demands rapid response.

Federal agencies must now coordinate patch deployment while maintaining continuity of operations. Many government organizations rely heavily on VPN access for remote work, meaning they cannot simply disable these systems without careful planning. The three-day window requires agencies to have established procedures ready and security teams prepared to work quickly through the remediation process.

This incident demonstrates how vulnerabilities in widely used security products can create cascading risks across entire sectors. When software used by dozens of organizations contains exploitable flaws, the impact multiplies rapidly. The CISA directive aims to close this window of vulnerability before ransomware groups can cause additional damage to federal infrastructure.

Social Security trust fund projected to run out by 2032 without congressional action

A key trust fund that finances Social Security benefits is expected to deplete its reserves in less than seven years unless Congress takes action to shore up the program, according to a warning issued by the Social Security Trustees. The projection marks a deadline for lawmakers to address the long-term solvency of the program that provides retirement and disability benefits to...

12:53 PM ET

A key trust fund that finances Social Security benefits is expected to deplete its reserves in less than seven years unless Congress takes action to shore up the program, according to a warning issued by the Social Security Trustees. The projection marks a deadline for lawmakers to address the long-term solvency of the program that provides retirement and disability benefits to tens of millions of Americans.

The trust fund depletion would not mean Social Security stops paying benefits entirely, but it would trigger automatic cuts to payments if no legislative fix is enacted. Once the reserves are exhausted, the program would only be able to pay out what it collects in payroll taxes, which would cover a portion of scheduled benefits but not the full amounts currently promised to beneficiaries.

Social Security faces a funding shortfall due to demographic shifts, including longer lifespans and the retirement of the baby boom generation, which have increased the ratio of beneficiaries to workers paying into the system. The program currently pays benefits to more than 67 million people, including retirees, disabled workers, and survivors of deceased workers. Any reduction in benefits would affect a broad swath of the American population, particularly older adults who rely on Social Security as their primary or sole source of income.

The Trustees' report puts pressure on Congress to consider solutions, which could include raising the payroll tax cap, increasing the retirement age, adjusting cost-of-living calculations, or reducing benefits for higher earners. Each option carries political risks, and past efforts to reform Social Security have stalled due to disagreements over how to balance the program's finances. The 2032 timeline gives lawmakers a narrowing window to negotiate changes before automatic cuts take effect.

The warning comes as fiscal policy debates intensify in Washington, with competing priorities including tax policy, defense spending, and other entitlement programs. Social Security has long been considered politically untouchable, but the approaching insolvency date may force action in the coming years.

A few minutes ago

The Social Security retirement trust fund is projected to be depleted by 2032, one year earlier than previous estimates, according to a new report from the program's trustees. If Congress does not act before the fund runs out, benefits for roughly 68 million Americans could be cut by an average of 22 percent.

The accelerated depletion timeline marks a significant shift from last year's projections, which anticipated the fund would last until 2033. The trust fund pays retirement benefits to older Americans and represents one of the federal government's largest financial obligations. The program has faced long-term funding challenges as the ratio of workers paying into the system relative to beneficiaries receiving payments has declined.

The trustees' report serves as the official assessment of Social Security's financial health and is closely watched by policymakers and retirees. The one-year acceleration in the depletion date increases pressure on Congress to address the program's structural funding gap. Without legislative intervention, the program would only be able to pay benefits from incoming payroll tax revenue, which would cover roughly 78 percent of scheduled payments.

Several policy options exist to shore up the program's finances, including raising the payroll tax rate, increasing the income cap subject to Social Security taxes, raising the retirement age, or reducing benefits for higher earners. Each approach carries political challenges, and Congress has repeatedly delayed action on Social Security reform despite warnings about the trust fund's trajectory.

The 22 percent average benefit reduction would affect current and future retirees across all income levels unless lawmakers pass legislation to close the funding gap. Social Security provides the majority of income for most older Americans, making any potential cuts a matter of significant economic consequence. The trustees' report provides a six-year window for Congress to craft a solution before automatic reductions would take effect.

2:00 PM ET

The Social Security retirement trust fund is projected to be depleted by 2032, one year earlier than previous estimates, according to a new report from the program's trustees. If Congress does not act before the fund runs out, benefits for roughly 68 million Americans could be cut by an average of 22 percent.

The accelerated depletion timeline marks a significant shift from last year's projections, which anticipated the fund would last until 2033. The trust fund pays retirement benefits to older Americans and represents one of the federal government's largest financial obligations. The program has faced long-term funding challenges as the ratio of workers paying into the system relative to beneficiaries receiving payments has declined.

The trustees' report serves as the official assessment of Social Security's financial health and is closely watched by policymakers and retirees. The one-year acceleration in the depletion date increases pressure on Congress to address the program's structural funding gap. Without legislative intervention, the program would only be able to pay benefits from incoming payroll tax revenue, which would cover roughly 78 percent of scheduled payments.

Several policy options exist to shore up the program's finances, including raising the payroll tax rate, increasing the income cap subject to Social Security taxes, raising the retirement age, or reducing benefits for higher earners. Each approach carries political challenges, and Congress has repeatedly delayed action on Social Security reform despite warnings about the trust fund's trajectory.

The 22 percent average benefit reduction would affect current and future retirees across all income levels unless lawmakers pass legislation to close the funding gap. Social Security provides the majority of income for most older Americans, making any potential cuts a matter of significant economic consequence. The trustees' report provides a six-year window for Congress to craft a solution before automatic reductions would take effect.

Apple is considering removing existing apps from the App Store that it deems stale, low-value, or unable to attract users. The policy would target apps with minimal engagement or infrequent updates.

The exact criteria Apple will use to evaluate apps and the timeline for implementation remain to be determined. Developers maintaining smaller user bases could be affected by this change.
