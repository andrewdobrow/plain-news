Federal authorities disrupted an alleged terrorist plot targeting a UFC event at the White House, the Justice Department announced. Five men were arrested across multiple states in connection with the conspiracy to attack the "Freedom 250" event.

The arrests took place over the weekend in Ohio, Missouri, Nebraska, and California. According to charges filed by the Department of Justice, the five men allegedly planned a coordinated assault involving both aerial and ground-based weapons.

The suspects reportedly intended to deploy drones armed with explosives around the UFC event to force an evacuation. Following the evacuation, they allegedly planned to position snipers to fire upon "high value targets" within the fleeing crowd.

Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche emphasized the swift response by law enforcement. "The FBI, our law enforcement partners and our U.S. Attorneys did what they do every day to make America safe through quick response and vigilance in investigating, disrupting, and dismantling this alleged plan before it could be carried out," Blanche said. He added that the administration would "take immediate and aggressive action to identify and prosecute those who incite and plan acts of violence."

Court filings unsealed by the government reveal that the group expressed grievances about various issues, including corruption, the Epstein files, and data centers. These motivations were cited in the charges against the men.

The investigation identified the conspiracy before the event took place, allowing authorities to prevent the planned operation. The UFC Freedom 250 event proceeded as scheduled after the threat was neutralized.

Security experts have pointed to this incident as evidence of evolving terrorist tactics. The use of drones equipped with explosives represents a method that law enforcement agencies increasingly monitor as a security concern.

The FBI led the investigation that uncovered the plot, working alongside other law enforcement partners and U.S. Attorneys' offices to build their case. The multi-state nature of the operation required coordination between federal agencies across different jurisdictions.

The incident highlights the security challenges posed by large-scale public events held at sensitive government locations. The White House decision to host the UFC event meant implementing enhanced security measures to protect attendees and government officials present.

The investigation remains active as officials work to determine whether additional individuals were involved in planning or supporting the alleged attack. Authorities continue to gather evidence and interview witnesses to establish the full scope of the conspiracy.

The successful disruption of the plot underscores the ongoing work of federal law enforcement to identify and prevent violent attacks before they occur. The charges represent the government's commitment to prosecuting those involved in planning violence against public events and officials.

A few minutes ago

The head of the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention warned that the current Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo could become the worst on record if containment efforts fail to slow transmission rates.

About a month after the latest outbreak was identified, caused by the Bundibugyo virus, confirmed cases had reached 676 with 136 deaths, mostly concentrated in the DRC's Ituri province. According to the World Health Organization, the number of victims is roughly doubling every week.

The outbreak has created heartbreaking situations on the ground. When an orphanage in Bunia took in a newborn baby after his mother died from Ebola, staff members hoped to save his life. The infant survived only two weeks. Four nuns who cared for him subsequently fell ill with the virus. All children and staff at the orphanage are now being monitored for symptoms.

Health workers battling the outbreak face significant obstacles. Frontline responders report shortages of personal protective equipment and vehicles needed to transport bodies. While testing has improved, workers say it still needs to happen faster and closer to patients. In neighboring Uganda, which has reported 19 cases and two deaths, intensive contact tracing has kept the outbreak under control, according to health officials.

Community resistance poses another major challenge. Misinformation is spreading, with some people denying the virus exists or blaming aid organizations for spreading it. People are reluctant to allow sick relatives to receive care in healthcare isolation units, partly due to concerns about funeral rites. According to one study after the 2016 outbreak, unsafe funeral practices for Ebola victims created an average of 2.58 secondary cases per exposure to body fluids.

Dr. Jean Kaseya, director general of Africa CDC, highlighted a critical gap in contact tracing. With about 600 confirmed cases in one area, experts would expect to monitor roughly 24,000 contacts. However, only 4,955 contacts had been listed, with just 57 percent being monitored. Kaseya warned this represents a substantial risk for continued community transmission, noting that some confirmed cases remain outside hospitals.

Conflict in the worst-affected regions and attacks on aid workers and treatment centers are also slowing response efforts. A local MP for Bunia noted on social media that international funding pledges have not been sufficiently visible on the ground in Ituri.

A joint response plan by Africa CDC and the WHO estimates that 518 million dollars will be needed over six months to bring the outbreak under control. Kaseya said some early financial pledges were not fulfilled, but approximately 212 million dollars in funding and support in kind has been committed.

Scientists are developing vaccines against Bundibugyo, and existing antivirals show promise for treating the virus.

The decision reflects growing conc...

2:14 PM ET

The Bank of Japan has raised its key interest rate to 1 percent, marking the highest level in 31 years as policymakers attempt to control inflation pressures linked to the Iran war. The central bank increased its short-term policy rate by a quarter percentage point from 0.75 percent, signaling a significant shift in Japan's monetary approach.

The decision reflects growing concerns about price pressures spreading through the economy. Bank of Japan officials warned that companies are passing on rising oil costs to each other at a relatively fast pace. Despite recent declines in oil prices following a U.S.-Iran peace agreement framework, the central bank proceeded with the rate increase, indicating confidence that inflationary risks warrant action even as immediate oil price pressures ease.

Central bank governor Shinichi Uchida acknowledged at a press conference that the signing of a U.S.-Iran memorandum was "a welcome move" while noting uncertainty about how quickly oil supplies would recover. He emphasized that while the risk of sharp economic deterioration from the Middle East conflict has diminished, underlying inflation concerns persist. With core inflation approaching the central bank's 2 percent target, officials said it was important to ensure that inflation stabilizes at desired levels.

The rate increase lifts Japan's borrowing costs to levels not seen since 1995, when the central bank was reducing rates following the collapse of asset prices in the late 1980s. This represents a dramatic turnaround from 2016, when Japan implemented negative interest rates in an effort to stimulate the economy and combat decades of deflation.

The move comes as major central banks respond to inflationary pressures. The European Central Bank recently raised its main interest rates, while the U.S. Federal Reserve and Bank of England are expected to hold borrowing costs steady at upcoming meetings. Japan's approach remains gradual compared to more aggressive tightening seen elsewhere.

The government has introduced a relief package to help households facing elevated fuel costs, which likely contributed to the central bank's confidence in proceeding with the rate increase. Higher borrowing costs will affect businesses and consumers across Japan, potentially moderating economic activity while helping to stabilize prices.

Markets reacted positively to the decision, with some investors viewing it as measured rather than hawkish. The Nikkei share index reached 70,000 points for the first time during Tuesday's session, closing at a new record high. The index has gained approximately one-third of its value so far this year.

The rate increase marks a watershed moment for an economy that spent much of the past three decades struggling with deflation and stagnation. Economists will monitor how Japanese businesses and households respond to higher borrowing costs and whether additional rate increases follow.

Trump administration transfers special education and civil rights enforcement from Education Department

The Trump administration announced Tuesday that it is shifting special education programs and civil rights enforcement out of the Department of Education. The Department of Justice will take over civil rights enforcement and student privacy protection, while the Department of Health and Human Services will assume responsibility for special education initiatives for students wit...

3:02 PM ET

The Trump administration announced Tuesday that it is shifting special education programs and civil rights enforcement out of the Department of Education. The Department of Justice will take over civil rights enforcement and student privacy protection, while the Department of Health and Human Services will assume responsibility for special education initiatives for students with disabilities.

This reorganization represents a significant change in federal oversight of student protections. Civil rights enforcement has historically been handled by the Education Department, covering discrimination complaints related to race, sex, disability, and other protected categories in schools and universities. Student privacy protections under federal law, including regulations governing educational records, will now move to the Justice Department.

The decision affects millions of students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, which guarantees eligible children access to free and appropriate public education. Moving these programs to Health and Human Services places them within an agency focused on health and welfare services rather than educational policy. This shift will impact oversight of funding, compliance monitoring, and technical assistance that schools receive for special education services.

The announcement aligns with the Trump administration's broader effort to restructure the Education Department, which the president has promised to close. The transfer of these core functions raises questions about how enforcement priorities and program administration may change under new agency arrangements.

The source material indicates that the administration has already begun shifting its approach to civil rights enforcement. The Education Department announced it had terminated agreements that previous administrations reached with five school districts and a college aimed at upholding rights and protections for transgender students. The affected districts include Cape Henlopen in Delaware, Fife in Washington, Delaware Valley in Pennsylvania, and La Mesa-Spring Valley, Sacramento City Unified, and Taft College in California. Under the Biden and Obama administrations, the department interpreted Title IX to include protections for transgender and gay students.

Kimberly Richey, assistant secretary for civil rights at the education department, said the action reflected the administration's view that prior administrations had imposed "unnecessary and unlawful burdens" on schools. She characterized the previous approach as a "relentless pursuit of a radical transgender agenda."

Al Goldberg, a Sacramento City Unified spokesperson, said it remains unclear what effects Monday's decision would have on the district's policies or funding, though the district stated it remains committed to supporting LGBTQ+ students and staff.

The administration did not provide a detailed timeline for completing the transfers or specify how staff and budgets would be reallocated. Schools, parents, and advocacy organizations will need to adjust to new points of contact and potentially different procedures as the reorganization takes effect.
