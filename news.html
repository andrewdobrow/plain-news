Trump Administration Discussing Government Stake in AI Companies

President Trump has confirmed these discussions and indicated he will meet with AI executives next week to discuss the proposal. The potential arrangemen...

10:25 PM ET

The Trump administration is exploring the possibility of acquiring equity stakes in leading artificial intelligence companies as part of a profit-sharing arrangement with the federal government, according to multiple reports. President Trump has confirmed these discussions and indicated he will meet with AI executives next week to discuss the proposal.

The potential arrangement would grant the federal government ownership positions in major AI firms, including OpenAI, in exchange for government support of their operations. Trump framed the concept as a mechanism for taxpayers to benefit from companies that receive government assistance or utilize public resources. The administration has been actively discussing such an arrangement with OpenAI, according to CNBC reporting.

The proposal comes after Senator Bernie Sanders published an opinion piece in The New York Times proposing that artificial intelligence should be classified as a public resource. Sanders argued that American citizens should own stakes in AI companies, sparking broader discussion about who benefits from advances in AI technology development.

Next week's meetings will bring together Trump administration officials and top AI company leaders to examine how government investment and profit-sharing could work in practice. According to Politico, the discussions center on how the federal government might obtain equity stakes in exchange for infrastructure support, regulatory accommodations, or other assistance related to AI development.

This approach represents a significant shift from historical American industrial policy, which typically steers clear of direct government ownership stakes in private technology companies. Proponents of the plan argue that AI development has depended substantially on publicly funded research, government-provided computing infrastructure, and regulatory frameworks that justify public equity ownership. Those with reservations have raised concerns about potential government interference in private enterprise and the precedent that federal ownership of commercial technology firms could establish.

The rationale for government involvement reflects arguments that AI companies benefit considerably from public investment and resources. Supporters maintain that this justifies compensation to taxpayers through equity participation and profit-sharing arrangements.

The upcoming meetings are expected to clarify whether the administration intends to pursue formal legislation or executive action to establish such a program. The discussions may also address how government stakes would be valued, managed, and structured to ensure effective oversight and appropriate returns for the public.

The proposal raises several unanswered questions about implementation. Issues such as how the government would exercise its ownership rights, what voting power these stakes would provide, and how profits would be distributed remain unclear. The mechanics of determining which companies would be eligible for such arrangements and how government support would be quantified also require clarification.

These developments signal the administration's willingness to experiment with unconventional approaches to economic policy regarding emerging technologies, particularly those deemed critical to national interests.

A National Park Service ranger died after falling into a crevasse on Denali, the park service announced. The ranger was conducting a patrol on the mountain, known officially as Mount McKinley, when the accident occurred. The death marks the fourth fatality on the mountain this climbing season.

The ranger worked for the National Park Service and was from Washington state, according to reports. The fall happened during routine patrol operations on the Alaska mountain, which stands as the highest peak in North America at more than 20,000 feet. Crevasses are deep cracks in glacial ice that pose one of the most serious hazards to climbers and mountaineers.

Denali's climbing season typically runs from late spring through early summer, when weather conditions are most favorable. Despite this, the mountain remains extremely dangerous, with unpredictable weather, high altitude, and glacial terrain creating constant risks. Park rangers regularly patrol climbing routes to assist mountaineers, monitor conditions, and conduct rescues when necessary.

The climbing season has proven particularly deadly this year, with three other fatalities already recorded before this incident. Rangers who work on Denali undergo specialized training in mountaineering, wilderness medicine, and high-altitude rescue operations. Their patrols are considered essential for climber safety, though the work itself carries significant risk.

The National Park Service has not released additional details about the circumstances of the fall or the recovery operation. Denali National Park attracts more than 1,000 climbers each year who attempt to reach the summit, with success rates varying widely depending on weather and conditions. The mountain's extreme environment and technical climbing requirements make it one of the most challenging climbs in the world, even for experienced mountaineers and trained professionals.

The Justice Department confirmed in a court filing Friday that the Trump administration's nearly $1.8 billion "anti-weaponization" fund will not continue. Two DOJ attorneys submitted the filing in the Eastern District of Virginia, asking the court to reject a legal challenge brought by a former federal prosecutor and others.

The fund was established to compensate individuals who claimed they were wrongly targeted by government investigations that the Trump administration characterized as politically motivated. The program faced swift legal and political opposition after its announcement last month as part of a settlement in the president's lawsuit against the IRS over the leak of his tax returns.

The backlash came from multiple directions. US District Judge Leonie Brinkema temporarily blocked the administration from transferring money from the fund after the nonprofit Democracy Forward filed a lawsuit seeking to dissolve it. Several states, including California, New York, Illinois, and Connecticut, proposed measures to tax any distributions from the fund to their residents.

The fund drew particular criticism because of its lack of oversight and secrecy. The terms did not require disclosure of how much money would be paid to whom. Administration officials acknowledged that payouts could include pardoned January 6 rioters, prompting sharp rebuke from across the political spectrum.

"Trump's nearly $2bn Maga slush fund is his most brazen act of self-dealing yet and one of the most corrupt schemes ever launched by a president," said US Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, who vowed that Senate Democrats would not allow it to stand.

Even some Republicans expressed alarm. Senate Majority Leader John Thune told reporters that "the best way to handle it is if the administration decides to shut the fund down themselves." Former Vice President Mike Pence called the possibility of compensating rioters who assaulted police officers and vandalized the Capitol "deeply offensive."

The fund had faced questions about whether the executive branch possessed the authority to create a compensation mechanism without congressional approval. Legal experts raised concerns about the fund's structure and implementation from its inception.

The Justice Department's court filing confirms the administration's decision to discontinue the program. The filing asked the court to dismiss the legal challenge, arguing that discontinuation resolves the underlying dispute. The department did not provide details about whether any payments had already been distributed from the fund or explain what will happen to the allocated money.

The termination of the fund removes one significant element from ongoing litigation over Trump administration policies, though the court must still rule on the DOJ's motion to dismiss the case.
