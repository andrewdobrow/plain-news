12:01 PM ET

Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr has warned that regulatory relief measures for large banks have primarily benefited shareholders rather than borrowers, raising concerns about financial stability as the industry enters a period of deregulation.

Barr cautioned that loosening rules on major financial institutions during a period of economic strength could introduce vulnerabilities to the banking system. His comments come as policymakers continue to debate the appropriate level of oversight for large financial institutions.

The regulatory relief measures implemented in recent years were designed to reduce compliance costs and encourage banks to increase lending to businesses and consumers. However, Barr's analysis suggests that banks have used the profits gained from deregulation primarily to reward shareholders rather than expand lending activity. This outcome contradicts the original intent of the relief measures.

Barr's primary concern focuses on the timing of deregulation during what he described as a financial boom period. When markets are performing well and bank profits are strong, reducing regulatory requirements may leave the banking system exposed to significant risks when economic conditions change. He argued that regulatory standards should be designed to account for cyclical economic risks and maintain safeguards even during periods of apparent stability.

The debate over banking regulation has intensified as financial institutions have reported strong earnings while questions persist about whether looser rules compromise the overall safety of the financial system. Barr's position represents a more cautious approach within the Federal Reserve, emphasizing the need to balance industry concerns about regulatory burden against the critical importance of preventing financial crises.

His warnings come against the backdrop of previous banking sector turmoil that required government intervention to prevent broader economic damage. The current regulatory trends, according to Barr's analysis, may be moving too far in favor of industry preferences at the potential expense of systemic stability. This creates a tension between different regulators and political leaders who must weigh competing priorities for financial oversight.

The discussion also reflects broader questions about the relationship between banking regulation and economic outcomes. While deregulation advocates argue that reducing compliance costs helps banks operate more efficiently, Barr's concerns highlight the potential dangers of reducing protections during economically favorable conditions. His position suggests that regulators should maintain stronger safeguards during booms to prevent excessive risk-taking that could threaten stability during downturns.

As policymakers continue to debate the appropriate regulatory framework for the financial system, Barr's public warnings signal that disagreement exists within the Federal Reserve about how aggressively to pursue deregulation. His emphasis on maintaining adequate safeguards during periods of economic strength suggests that future policy debates over banking regulation will remain contested among senior officials responsible for managing financial stability.

3:00 PM ET

Chemists have demonstrated for the first time how RNA could have replicated itself on early Earth, potentially solving a long-standing puzzle in origin-of-life research. The breakthrough addresses a fundamental question that has challenged scientists for decades: how the first genetic material could have reproduced without the complex cellular machinery that exists today.

The research shows a mechanism by which RNA molecules could have copied themselves in conditions similar to those believed to have existed on the primitive Earth. RNA is considered a crucial molecule in the emergence of life because it can both store genetic information like DNA and catalyze chemical reactions like proteins. This dual functionality has led many scientists to propose that an "RNA world" preceded the DNA-based life forms that exist today.

The self-replication of RNA has been a bottleneck in origin-of-life studies because modern cells use elaborate protein machinery to copy genetic material. Understanding how this process could have occurred without that machinery has been a major challenge. The demonstration provides experimental evidence for a pathway that could have allowed early RNA molecules to reproduce themselves without requiring the presence of evolved biological systems.

The work represents a significant advance in understanding how life could have emerged from non-living chemistry. By showing a plausible mechanism for RNA self-replication under early Earth conditions, the research helps fill a critical gap in theories about how the first living systems developed. The findings support the RNA world hypothesis, which proposes that self-replicating RNA molecules were a key stepping stone in the transition from chemistry to biology.

The demonstration offers a concrete answer to one of the most fundamental questions in science: how life began. While many steps in the origin of life remain uncertain, this research provides experimental backing for a process that must have occurred if RNA-based life preceded modern cellular organisms.

The Triple Crown has been called the most difficult feat in American sports, requiring a horse to win three prestigious races held at different distances in different states over a five-week span. Anthony Head, the British actor best known for playing Rupert Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, has died at age 72. He passed away peacefully from complications related to pneumonia, surrounded by his family, according to a statement from his daughters Emily and Daisy Head.

"It has been, and forever will be, an honour and a privilege to be his daughters, and to have witnessed first-hand the impact both he and his work have had on so many," they said.

Head played Giles, the school librarian and mentor to Sarah Michelle Gellar's title character, throughout Buffy the Vampire Slayer's seven-season run from 1997 to 2003. The role established him as a beloved figure in television and introduced him to audiences worldwide. Sarah Michelle Gellar paid tribute to Head on Instagram, beginning with a quote from her Buffy character: "Tell Giles I figured it out and I'm ok." She then wrote, "Well I don't have it figured out and I'm not ok. But I know I'm the lucky one because I knew you."

Beyond Buffy, Head's career spanned decades in British and American television, theater, and film. He had a recurring role in the BBC sketch show Little Britain and appeared in the BBC production of Merlin. His film credits included The Iron Lady and The Inbetweeners Movie. More recently, he played former football club owner Rupert Mannion in the Emmy-winning Apple TV+ series Ted Lasso, where he developed strong on-screen chemistry with co-creator Jason Sudeikis.

Born in Camden, London, Head came from an artistic family. His father founded Verity Films as a documentary filmmaker, while his mother was an actress who appeared in the 1960s BBC crime drama Maigret. His brother also pursued acting.

Head began his professional career on stage, starring in the 1978 West End revival of Godspell. He later took roles in films including the 1981 adaptation of Lady Chatterley's Lover and 1987's A Prayer for the Dying. In the early 1980s, he sang backing vocals for British pop group Red Box and appeared on their 1986 debut album.

He was perhaps best known in the United Kingdom and United States for his role in Nescafe Gold Blend television advertisements during the 1980s. The stage remained central to his career, with notable roles including Freddie Trumper in the original West End production of the Abba musical Chess in 1988, and Dr. Frank-N-Furter in the 1990 West End production of The Rocky Horror Show.

During Buffy's production, Head commuted between Los Angeles and England, where his partner and two young daughters lived. "I'd try to go home to them every three or four weeks," he told the Guardian, noting that the production team accommodated his schedule.

His daughters stated that Head "loved his job very much and he always considered himself incredibly lucky to have been able to work alongside such exceptionally talented people, in such wonderful productions, across a career that spanned several decades."

The research shows a mechanism by which RNA molecules could have copied themselves in conditions similar to those believed to have existed on the primitive Earth. RNA is considered a crucial molecule in the emergence of life because it can both store genetic information like DNA and catalyze chemical reactions like proteins. This dual functionality has led many scientists to propose that an "RNA world" preceded the DNA-based life forms that exist today.

The self-replication of RNA has been a bottleneck in origin-of-life studies because modern cells use elaborate protein machinery to copy genetic material. Understanding how this process could have occurred without that machinery has been a major challenge. The demonstration provides experimental evidence for a pathway that could have allowed early RNA molecules to reproduce themselves without requiring the presence of evolved biological systems.

The work represents a significant advance in understanding how life could have emerged from non-living chemistry. By showing a plausible mechanism for RNA self-replication under early Earth conditions, the research helps fill a critical gap in theories about how the first living systems developed. The findings support the RNA world hypothesis, which proposes that self-replicating RNA molecules were a key stepping stone in the transition from chemistry to biology.

The demonstration offers a concrete answer to one of the most fundamental questions in science: how life began. While many steps in the origin of life remain uncertain, this research provides experimental backing for a process that must have occurred if RNA-based life preceded modern cellular organisms.
