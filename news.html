The Justice Department has approved Paramount's $111 billion acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery without conditions, clearing a major regulatory hurdle for the media merger. The Antitrust Division signed off on the deal, which would combine the studios behind CNN, HBO, Paramount Pictures, and numerous other entertainment properties under David Ellison's Paramount Skydance.

The approval allows the companies to move forward with completing the transaction, though the merger still faces potential legal challenges before closing. Warner Bros. Discovery owns cable networks including CNN and HBO, the Warner Bros. film studio, and streaming service Max. Paramount operates CBS, Paramount Pictures, Paramount+, and cable channels including MTV and Nickelodeon.

The combined company would create one of the largest media conglomerates in the United States, consolidating significant film and television production capabilities along with streaming platforms competing against Netflix, Disney, and Amazon. The merger comes as traditional media companies face pressure from cord-cutting and the shift to streaming services.

Regulators determined the deal would not substantially reduce competition in the media industry, despite the size of the combined entity. The Justice Department's unconditional approval suggests officials did not find sufficient antitrust concerns to require divestitures or operational restrictions.

The transaction still requires additional steps before completion and could face opposition from other parties. Media industry consolidation has drawn scrutiny in recent years as streaming services compete for subscribers and traditional television viewership declines. The merger would give the combined company greater leverage in negotiations with distributors and advertisers while providing a larger content library to support streaming growth.

4:57 PM ET

A federal judge rejected a last-minute request from the Kennedy Center to delay the removal of former President Trump's name from the Washington performing arts venue. The decision keeps a previous court order in place, requiring the name be taken down within 14 days.

US District Judge Christopher Cooper ruled that only Congress has the authority to change the Kennedy Center's official name. In his 94-page opinion, Cooper wrote that the Kennedy Center's founding law "makes crystal clear that the Center is to be named for President Kennedy, and it cannot bear any other formal name or public memorial based on the Board's unilateral say-so."

The judge found that the Kennedy Center's board of trustees overstepped its legal authority when it voted to add Trump's name to the facility. "Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name, and only Congress can change it," Cooper stated.

Following the ruling, workers set up scaffolding at the Kennedy Center building to prepare for the physical removal of Trump's name from signage and official materials. The work was expected to be completed before the 14-day deadline established by the court.

In response to the decision, Trump posted on his Truth Social platform that he would work with Congress to transfer ownership of the Kennedy Center to the federal government. "I have instructed the Department of Commerce to make all necessary arrangements with Congress to allow a full and complete transfer of this Institution," he wrote.

The lawsuit was filed by Joyce Beatty, an Ohio Democratic representative and member of the Kennedy Center's board. She welcomed the ruling, stating that "this administration's efforts to rename and close the Center have no basis in law." Beatty emphasized that the Kennedy Center "belongs to the American people, not to Donald Trump."

The naming controversy began after Trump took office and appointed himself chair of the Kennedy Center's board of trustees in February. He then removed existing board members and replaced them with his appointees. In December, the restructured board voted to add Trump's name to the institution.

The move sparked significant backlash from the arts community. The Tony Award-winning musical "Hamilton" canceled its scheduled run, and other performing groups protested the decision. The executive director of the National Symphony Orchestra resigned to take a position elsewhere, and Richard Grenell, a Trump appointee who played a key role in the board overhaul, stepped down as president.

The Kennedy Center receives federal funding and operates as the nation's premier performing arts venue. It has traditionally maintained bipartisan support, though it has become increasingly tied to the Trump administration's cultural agenda during his presidency.

The rocket and artificial intelligence company debuted with a valuation exceeding $2.2 trillion after opening at $150 per share and climbing throughout the trading session.

The company had aimed to raise $75 billion through the IPO by selling 555.6 million shares at $135 each, giving it an initial market value of $1.77 trillion. The strong performance on the first day of trading pushed the valuation even higher, surpassing the previous IPO record held by Saudi Aramco, which floated in 2019 at $1.7 trillion and raised $25.6 billion.

The successful debut comes after SpaceX confidentially filed to go public in April. The company, founded in 2002, has grown into one of the world's most valuable businesses. Elon Musk, the company's founder and CEO, did not sell any of his shares in the offering and retained 82.4 percent of the voting power in SpaceX.

Musk's wealth soared as a result of the IPO. According to the Bloomberg rich list, his net worth reached $1.11 trillion, making him the world's first trillionaire. His stake in SpaceX alone was valued at $542 billion before the public listing.

At SpaceX's headquarters on Friday morning, Musk addressed the historic moment. "It is certainly hard to believe that a little company that started in a warehouse in El Segundo is now going public with the largest IPO ever," he said. He reiterated the company's mission to make humanity multiplanetary and remove fiction from science fiction.

SpaceX operates across three main business divisions: rocket launching services, the Starlink satellite broadband network, and artificial intelligence operations including the X social media platform. While the overall business was loss-making, recording a $4.9 billion loss in 2025 on $18.7 billion in revenue, the Starlink division remains profitable. Revenue grew by a third compared to the previous year.

The company has secured major contracts with NASA and commercial clients while developing reusable rocket technology. It also acquired Musk's xAI company to build solar-powered infrastructure supporting AI operations, with plans to launch "orbital compute" by 2028.

Due to SpaceX's massive size, it is expected to receive substantial weighting in the S&P 500 stock index, meaning most people with investment accounts or pension funds will be exposed to the company's stock performance.

Industry observers expect the successful SpaceX offering to pave the way for other major private technology companies to pursue public listings. Anthropic and OpenAI are among firms reportedly preparing their own stock market debuts in the coming months.

SpaceX had planned to sell 555.6 million shares and raise $75 billion from the sale. The IPO was billed as the biggest stock market launch in history, with up to a quarter of the shares reserved for individual investors rather than institutional funds and banks, which is a larger allocation than is typical for major IPOs.

The space exploration and satellite company had been one of the most anticipated public offerings in years. Investors showed strong enthusiasm for the company's dominance in commercial spaceflight and its Starlink satellite internet network. The robust first-day performance reflected confidence in SpaceX's prospects across space transportation and telecommunications sectors.

Nasdaq rules were adjusted to allow SpaceX to join the index on a fast-track basis. This decision meant the company would benefit from "forced buying" by tracker funds and index funds, providing additional purchasing pressure on the shares. Richard Hunter, head of markets at interactive investor, explained that this guaranteed significant additional buying interest, though it remained uncertain whether SpaceX would have a disproportionate effect on the index weighting.

The company has evolved from its early struggles to become the dominant player in commercial spaceflight. Its reusable rocket technology has substantially reduced the cost of space access, making it a central player in both government and commercial space operations.

For UK-based investors, platforms including AJ Bell and Hargreaves Lansdown offered access to the IPO. Some UK investment trusts, including Edinburgh Worldwide and Baillie Gifford US Growth, already held stakes in SpaceX before the public listing.

The successful debut marked a significant shift for a company that had remained private for over two decades. The IPO validates the commercial spaceflight business model, demonstrating that private companies can build profitable operations in an industry once dominated entirely by government agencies.

Opening alongside Amy Jones, the pair added 135 runs together before Jones was caught in the 14th over attempting to clear the boundary. Nat Sciver-Brunt then accelerated the innings with an unbeaten 46 from 22 balls, striking back-to-back boundaries in the final over.

Wyatt-Hodge finished unbeaten on 97 runs before getting back on strike to complete her century with a sweep into the gap behind square. The milestone held special significance for the batter, who celebrated by cradling her bat like a baby in tribute to her daughter Daisy, born just three weeks earlier. The Edgbaston crowd rose to applaud the moment.

Sri Lanka's response proved far less effective. The batting order crumbled against England's bowling attack, with the visitors reaching only 132 all out. Freya Kemp delivered a career-best performance of four for 22, dismantling Sri Lanka's middle order with consistent bowling that removed Kaushani Nuthyangana, Sugandika Kumari, and Harshitha Samarawickrama.

Kemp struck early in Sri Lanka's innings, taking a sharp catch at backward square leg to dismiss Imesha Dulani. She angled a delivery into left-hander Samarawickrama to bowl her as Sri Lanka fell to 68 for five. Sophie Ecclestone wrapped up the innings with a double-wicket final over.

Wyatt-Hodge contributed further to the victory in the field, taking a crucial catch to dismiss Sri Lanka captain Chamari Athapaththu, racing back from square leg to claim a difficult catch under the Edgbaston lights as Athapaththu top-edged into the sky.

Sri Lanka's bowling proved costly, with debutants Malki Madara and Mithali Ayodhya conceding 51 and 40 runs respectively from their opening spells. The visitors bowled two no-balls and allowed 11 wides. England's fielders also benefited from Sri Lankan mistakes, with Jones dropped twice during her innings.

The victory marks a strong start for England in their World Cup defence, showcasing the batting firepower that had faced recent questions. The team established themselves as tournament favorites heading into the group stage, with this commanding performance against the Sri Lankan opponents providing early momentum as they pursue another world title.

The judge found that the Kennedy Center's board of trustees overstepped its legal authority when it voted to add Trump's name to the facility. "Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name, and only Congress can change it," Cooper stated.

Following the ruling, workers set up scaffolding at the Kennedy Center building to prepare for the physical removal of Trump's name from signage and official materials. The work was expected to be completed before the 14-day deadline established by the court.

In response to the decision, Trump posted on his Truth Social platform that he would work with Congress to transfer ownership of the Kennedy Center to the federal government. "I have instructed the Department of Commerce to make all necessary arrangements with Congress to allow a full and complete transfer of this Institution," he wrote.

The lawsuit was filed by Joyce Beatty, an Ohio Democratic representative and member of the Kennedy Center's board. She welcomed the ruling, stating that "this administration's efforts to rename and close the Center have no basis in law." Beatty emphasized that the Kennedy Center "belongs to the American people, not to Donald Trump."

The naming controversy began after Trump took office and appointed himself chair of the Kennedy Center's board of trustees in February. He then removed existing board members and replaced them with his appointees. In December, the restructured board voted to add Trump's name to the institution.

The move sparked significant backlash from the arts community. The Tony Award-winning musical "Hamilton" canceled its scheduled run, and other performing groups protested the decision. The executive director of the National Symphony Orchestra resigned to take a position elsewhere, and Richard Grenell, a Trump appointee who played a key role in the board overhaul, stepped down as president.

The Kennedy Center receives federal funding and operates as the nation's premier performing arts venue. It has traditionally maintained bipartisan support, though it has become increasingly tied to the Trump administration's cultural agenda during his presidency.
