Federal judge blocks Trump administration immigration restrictions affecting 39 countries

2:31 PM ET

A federal judge ruled Friday that the Trump administration unlawfully barred applicants from 39 travel-ban countries from receiving decisions on asylum applications, work permits, green cards, and citizenship applications.

The decision represents a significant legal setback for the administration's immigration enforcement agenda. The blocked policies had created uncertainty for thousands of people seeking to enter or remain in the United States through legal immigration channels. The restrictions had prevented nationals from countries including Cuba, Haiti, and Venezuela from accessing various forms of immigration relief and humanitarian protections.

The court found that the administration's measures lacked legal authority. Immigration advocates had challenged the restrictions in court, arguing they exceeded executive power and violated procedural requirements. The ruling restores access to immigration benefits for affected nationals while legal challenges continue through the courts.

People from the 39 designated countries can now resume filing applications for asylum, temporary protected status, and other forms of humanitarian relief that had been suspended under the administration's freeze. They will be able to proceed through normal immigration processes.

The timing of the ruling is significant. The Senate passed legislation early Friday morning to fund the Trump administration's controversial immigration crackdown, voting 52-47 to approve $70 billion in funding for US Immigration and Customs Enforcement and the Department of Homeland Security. The vote ended a partial government shutdown that had lasted since February. No Democrats supported the measure.

The administration has not yet announced whether it will appeal the judge's decision. The case is one of several ongoing legal battles over immigration policy changes implemented since the start of Trump's second term. Courts have previously blocked or limited multiple immigration-related executive actions on procedural or constitutional grounds.

The ruling comes as the Trump administration continues efforts to enforce what it calls "mass deportations" of immigrants in the country without authorization. The administration has prioritized what it describes as removing "the worst of the worst" from the country.

According to data obtained by The Guardian through court-ordered records requests, immigration agents filled out deportation forms for thousands of people arrested between fiscal year 2023 and August 2025. The data included biographical details, criminal history, and information about the children of those arrested. The Guardian matched the arrest records with deportation data to track the impact of enforcement actions on families with children under age 18.

The federal judge's decision blocking the restrictions on nationals from the 39 countries marks an important legal challenge to the administration's broader immigration enforcement strategy, even as Congress approved significant new funding for immigration enforcement agencies.

Iran launched a deadly drone attack on Kuwait International Airport, killing at least one person and wounding 63 others, according to Kuwait's military. The strike damaged a terminal at the airport and marked the first deadly attack in the Gulf region since a ceasefire took effect on April 8.

The assault came as part of an escalating exchange between US and Iranian forces. US Central Command said it had targeted an unladen tanker, the M/T Lexie, in international waters near Kuwait as the vessel moved toward Iran's Kharg Island. An American aircraft fired a missile to disable the tanker's engine after the crew ignored repeated warnings over 24 hours. Iran responded with the drone strike on Kuwait's passenger terminal.

The incident intensified further exchanges of fire. US forces said they had shot down three attack drones launched by Iran toward civilian ships in regional waters. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed it had attacked the US Fifth Fleet headquarters in Bahrain with missiles and drones in response to the strike on Qeshm Island, though US Central Command denied repelling such an attack. The US military also said it had attacked sites on Qeshm.

Kuwait's defence ministry described the attack as "criminal Iranian aggression which resulted in significant material damage to the building and injuries." Civil aviation authorities suspended traffic and transferred incoming flights to alternative airports. Kuwait's deputy foreign minister summoned Iran's chargé d'affaires to formally protest the attacks and ordered a reduction in Iranian embassy staff, declaring two diplomats persona non grata with 24 hours to leave.

Iran's foreign ministry blamed Kuwait and Bahrain for the strikes, stating they had allowed the United States to use their territory for aggressive plans against Iran. The statement emphasized the responsibility of Kuwait and Bahrain's leaders for "last night's acts of aggression."

The latest escalation jeopardizes ongoing ceasefire negotiations between Washington and Tehran. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio claimed during congressional testimony that war in Iran "is over" and suggested a deal was within reach. However, Trump said in an interview that talks were continuing and he was not seeking escalation, adding that Iran appeared "pretty close" to signing a peace agreement and "it could happen over the weekend."

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu warned that Iran was "playing with fire" and that Trump could order a "full-scale return to military action" if negotiations fail. A military adviser to Iran's supreme leader threatened more missile and drone strikes if the US renewed its attacks, posting that "every shot fired and every attack will be met with a deluge of missiles and drones."

The exchange of strikes demonstrates the fragile state of regional security and the difficulty of achieving diplomatic progress amid active military hostilities.

The Nasdaq closed down 2 percent while the S&P 500 fell just over 1 percent, with technology stocks driving much of the decline. The "magnificent seven" AI-related companies, including Nvidia, Amazon, Apple, Microsoft, Tesla, Alphabet, and Meta, all experienced losses. The market turbulence intensified after investor Michael Burry, known for predicting the 2008 financial crash, took short positions against Palantir and Nvidia. Palantir's chief executive, Alex Karp, criticized Burry and other short-sellers for "trying to call the AI revolution into question." The announcement sparked further selling pressure on these major tech stocks.

Asian markets followed the US declines on Wednesday, recording their sharpest slide in seven months. Stock indices in Japan and South Korea dropped more than 5 percent from their recent record highs. European markets in the UK, France, and Germany also declined slightly on Wednesday morning.

The pessimistic sentiment reflects growing skepticism about artificial intelligence investments. Bank executives from Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs cautioned that a market correction could be approaching, joining Jamie Dimon of JP Morgan Chase, who warned in October about the potential for a market crash within six months to two years. Jim Reid, an analyst at Deutsche Bank, noted there was a "growing chorus discussing whether we might be on the verge of an equity correction," adding that "the last 24 hours have brought a clear risk-off move, as concerns over lofty tech valuations have hit investor sentiment."

Some analysts have questioned the returns on artificial intelligence investments, pointing out that the vast majority of AI spending has concentrated on a small group of technology companies, particularly OpenAI and Nvidia, with limited results so far.

The market turmoil also extended to other risk assets. Bitcoin briefly dipped below $100,000 for the first time since June as investors moved money away from riskier investments. Bitcoin had reached a record high above $126,000 in early October but declined 3.7 percent that month, marking its worst monthly performance in the last decade.

The broad sell-off underscores heightened investor anxiety about whether recent market gains have outpaced fundamental value in technology and AI-related companies.

The NCAA has announced that four former Alabama State basketball players from the 2024-25 team have been ruled permanently ineligible after accepting payment from gamblers to fix the outcome of a game during that season.

The ruling affects the player who scored the winning basket in Alabama State's first NCAA tournament victory, along with three of his teammates. All four players are now barred from any future NCAA competition.

The decision represents one of the most significant game-fixing cases in recent college basketball history. The NCAA's announcement did not specify which game was targeted or provide details about the amounts of payment involved in the scheme.

Game-fixing scandals have periodically emerged in college basketball over the years, but cases involving multiple players from the same team remain relatively uncommon. The involvement of the player who delivered the decisive moment in Alabama State's tournament breakthrough adds another layer to the case, as it raises questions about the integrity of a historic moment for the program.

The NCAA's investigation uncovered evidence that the players accepted money from individuals with gambling interests to manipulate the outcome of at least one game during the 2024-25 season. The organization's enforcement process ultimately determined that the players violated NCAA rules regarding amateurism and ethical conduct.

Permanent ineligibility represents the NCAA's most severe penalty for student athletes. It prevents players from competing in any NCAA-sanctioned sport for the remainder of their collegiate careers and eliminates their ability to transfer to other NCAA institutions while maintaining eligibility.

The ruling does not appear to have resulted in additional penalties against Alabama State University itself at this time, though the NCAA sometimes takes additional action against institutions in game-fixing cases depending on whether they determine that the school failed in its compliance responsibilities.

The case underscores ongoing concerns about the intersection of gambling and college athletics. As sports betting has expanded across the United States in recent years, colleges and the NCAA have heightened efforts to educate athletes about the dangers of associating with gamblers and the serious consequences of attempting to fix games.

The NCAA has strict rules prohibiting athletes from engaging with gambling operations in any capacity that could compromise the integrity of competition. Even accepting payment to lose intentionally or manipulate game outcomes by specific margins violates these standards and triggers permanent ineligibility.

The announcement serves as a reminder of the NCAA's commitment to maintaining competitive integrity, though it also highlights challenges the organization faces in policing athlete conduct off the court. The case involves players who sought financial benefit through illegal gambling schemes rather than through authorized compensation channels available to athletes.

Anthony Head, the British actor best known for playing Rupert Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, has died at age 72. He passed away peacefully from complications due to pneumonia, surrounded by his family, according to a statement from his daughters Emily and Daisy Head.

"It has been, and forever will be, an honour and a privilege to be his daughters, and to have witnessed first-hand the impact both he and his work have had on so many," they said.

Head's most iconic role came as the mentor and Watcher to Sarah Michelle Gellar's character throughout Buffy the Vampire Slayer's seven-season run. In the show, Giles served as the school librarian at Sunnydale High School and became a beloved father figure to the main character. Gellar paid tribute to Head on Instagram, beginning with a quote from her character: "Tell Giles I figured it out and I'm ok." She then wrote, "Well I don't have it figured out and I'm not ok. But I know I'm the lucky one because I knew you."

Beyond Buffy, Head built a diverse career spanning theatre, television, and film. He was born in Camden, London, to artistic parents. His father was a documentary filmmaker who founded Verity Films, and his mother was an actress who appeared in the 1960s BBC crime drama Maigret. Head's early passion for performance led him to begin his professional career on stage, including starring in a 1978 West End revival of Godspell.

Head appeared in film roles including The Iron Lady and The Inbetweeners Movie. On television, he had a recurring role in the BBC sketch comedy Little Britain, playing a British prime minister constantly fawned over by an assistant. He also appeared in the BBC fantasy series Merlin.

More recently, Head joined the cast of Apple TV's Ted Lasso, where he played Rupert Mannion, a former football club owner. He regularly appeared in scenes with co-creator Jason Sudeikis, and the pair received praise for their on-screen chemistry.

Despite his rise to global fame, Head maintained perspective about celebrity. He told the Guardian that his success taught him "not to get caught up in the hype," noting that while some co-stars competed for magazine covers, he preferred to focus on his work.

During the filming of Buffy the Vampire Slayer, Head spent five years based in Los Angeles while his partner Sarah and their two young daughters remained in England. He made frequent trips home, arranging his shooting schedule to allow for regular visits.

His daughters expressed that while their grief is profound, "we know his legacy will live on in the shows he was a part of and in the audiences that love them. How lucky we are to know we are able to watch him doing what he loved, even when he is no longer with us."
