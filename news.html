Workers began removing Donald Trump's name from the Kennedy Center's facade early Saturday morning, hours after a federal court deadline passed on Friday night. The removal work started in the pre-dawn hours after an emergency appeal to block the removal was rejected by an appeals court.

The Kennedy Center had missed a two-week court-ordered deadline to remove the words "The Donald J Trump and" from its exterior by Friday at 11:59pm. U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper had previously ruled that Trump's name had been illegally added to the building and ordered its removal.

Trump's name was added to the Kennedy Center last December after his hand-picked board of trustees voted unanimously to rename the venue. The board consisted of trustees Trump selected after removing the 18 members appointed by former President Joe Biden. Trump also made himself chair of the board and seized control of the venue in February.

The Kennedy Center was originally designated by Congress as a living memorial to President John F. Kennedy and opened in 1971. Judge Cooper wrote in his 94-page opinion that "Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name, and only Congress can change it." The legal challenge was brought by Joyce Beatty, a Democratic representative from Ohio and ex-officio member of the Kennedy Center's board.

Workers erected scaffolding Friday evening and draped tarpaulin over the temporary structure at 2am to block public views. Despite the covering, workers were observed removing the letters around 3am. A crowd of more than 100 people gathered at a "Hands Off the Arts" rally in front of the center to witness the removal, with attendees chanting "Shame!" and "Take it down."

Trump and his legal team mounted a last-minute effort to keep his name on the building less than 36 hours before the deadline. At 1pm Friday, Judge Cooper ruled that Trump's lawyers had failed to demonstrate they were likely to win an appeal or that the Kennedy Center would suffer "irreparable harm" if the name was removed.

Justice Department lawyers then appealed Cooper's decision at 3:46pm, arguing to the appeals court that it made no sense to alter the signage only to potentially revert the name again after a successful appeal. This second emergency appeal was denied shortly after 7pm.

The Kennedy Center had already removed Trump's name from its website and sent emails offering tickets to events at "the Kennedy Center" without reference to Trump. The venue continued operating its regular programming schedule during the removal process.

Judge Cooper also issued a temporary block on Trump's demand to close the Kennedy Center for two years of renovations, which was scheduled to begin in July. Trump subsequently announced he was giving up control of the venue.

Yesterday, 8:06 PM ET

The Justice Department has cleared the way for Paramount Skydance to acquire Warner Bros. Discovery in an $111 billion deal that will reshape the media landscape. The approval came after an eight-month antitrust investigation, concluding that the merger poses no threat to competition or consumers.

The combined company will control major film studios, streaming platforms, cable networks, and news operations. Paramount brings CBS, CBS News, Paramount Pictures, and the Paramount+ streaming service to the transaction. Warner Bros. Discovery contributes HBO, Max, the Discovery Channel, and Warner Bros. Studios. The merger will place CNN and CBS News under the same corporate ownership.

The Justice Department's antitrust division completed its analysis after reviewing over two million documents from more than 80 custodians and receiving extensive input from third parties across the media and entertainment sector. In a statement, the agency said it found the transaction unlikely to harm competition in streaming video on demand, linear television, or studio development and distribution of theatrical films.

Paramount responded positively to the approval, calling the deal "pro-competitive" and saying it will create "a stronger company better positioned to compete against dominant technology platforms." The company characterized the merger as beneficial for consumers, creators, and the entertainment industry.

However, obstacles remain. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority has opened an investigation into the merger, setting a deadline of August 7 to decide whether a more detailed review is needed. European regulators are also examining the funding behind the deal, which includes $24 billion in commitments from Gulf sovereign-wealth funds. Australia has already approved the merger, determining it is unlikely to substantially lessen competition in theatrical film distribution.

Staff at CNN and CBS News have raised concerns about the combination, worried about potential job cuts and editorial changes. Paramount has promised $6 billion in cost savings from the merger. There is also speculation about potential changes to CNN's editorial direction under Paramount Skydance leadership, though David Ellison pledged in March that the network's editorial independence would be protected.

State-level opposition could pose additional challenges. California Attorney General Rob Bonta stated that the merger "remains under investigation" by his office and is "not a done deal." Multiple state attorneys general could potentially file a lawsuit to block the transaction in coming weeks.

Critics of the deal have expressed concerns about media consolidation. Craig Aaron, co-chief executive of Free Press, called the approval troubling, saying it concentrates too much media power in one corporation. Democratic Senator Elizabeth Warren also criticized the decision, arguing the merger allows aligned billionaires to control media consumption and pricing.

The deal now moves toward final closing procedures, including shareholder votes and remaining regulatory requirements across jurisdictions where both companies operate.

Yesterday, 11:00 PM ET

The Commerce Department has directed Anthropic to shut down its Fable and Mythos AI models, citing national security concerns over a potential vulnerability in the Fable 5 system. The government action targets what officials describe as a jailbreak that could pose security risks.

Anthropic has publicly disputed the government's decision to recall the models. In a blog post, the company stated it disagrees that discovering a narrow potential jailbreak justifies pulling a commercial model deployed to hundreds of millions of people. The company's response signals tension between AI safety protocols and government oversight.

The shutdown comes amid a broader conflict between Anthropic and the Trump administration. The Pentagon previously designated Anthropic as a "supply chain risk," the first time this blacklisting tool has been used against a U.S. company. In response, Anthropic filed two lawsuits against the Department of Defense, alleging that the government's decision was unlawful and violated the company's First Amendment rights.

According to Anthropic, the conflict stems from the company's attempt to implement safeguards against the military's potential use of its AI models for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous lethal weapons. The company accused the Trump administration and Pentagon of pursuing "punitive actions" against it for refusing to comply with ideological demands from the government. In its California lawsuit, Anthropic stated that "the constitution does not allow the government to wield its enormous power to punish a company for its protected speech."

Despite the legal dispute, Anthropic emphasized its commitment to national security. The company noted that its AI model Claude has been deeply integrated into the Department of Defense over the past year and was previously the only AI model approved for use in classified systems. Anthropic stated it had collaborated with the Pentagon to modify its systems for specific military needs and wanted to continue such negotiations.

A company spokesperson said that seeking judicial review did not change Anthropic's "longstanding commitment to harnessing AI to protect our national security," but that legal action was necessary "to protect our business, our customers and our partners." The company indicated it would pursue dialogue with the government alongside its court challenges.

In its lawsuit, Anthropic alleged that the government's actions were "harming Anthropic irreparably" by seeking to "destroy the economic value created by one of the world's fastest-growing private companies." However, Anthropic's CEO had previously told CBS News that the supply chain risk designation's impact was "fairly small" and the company would be "gonna be fine."

The shutdown represents a significant intervention in the commercial AI sector, affecting models that had reached widespread deployment. The incident raises questions about the balance between innovation and security concerns, as well as the extent of federal authority over AI development and deployment.

The record-breaking bonuses accompany what promises to be an unprecedented sporting event. The White House South Lawn has been transformed into a massive arena featuring a 92-foot steel structure nicknamed "the Claw," thousands of seats arranged in a mini coliseum configuration, and an octagon fighting cage.

The event coincides with President Donald Trump's 80th birthday and the nation's 250th anniversary celebration. About 4,300 military personnel are expected to attend the seven-fight card, which will unfold on the iconic lawn typically reserved for presidential travel and official state functions.

The setup has drawn legal scrutiny. Public Integrity Project filed a lawsuit in federal court seeking an emergency injunction to halt the event, arguing the administration violated multiple federal laws to accommodate what the group describes as a private, for-profit sporting enterprise masquerading as a patriotic celebration. The lawsuit names the National Park Service and Department of the Interior as defendants.

The legal challenge centers on whether the UFC event qualifies for exemptions under a temporary National Park Service rule designed for 250th anniversary events. The rule applies only to events "planned, organized and executed" by the federal government. The lawsuit contends that UFC Freedom 250, which CEO Dana White has indicated was Trump's idea, fails this test and instead constitutes a celebration of the UFC brand.

The suit also raised concerns about the Claw structure, alleging it was erected without required congressional authorization or mandatory environmental review. The massive steel structure has rerouted presidential travel and is visible from the front of the White House.

Trump's stock holdings in TKO Group Holdings, the UFC's parent company, also featured in conflict of interest allegations. Financial disclosures show Trump purchased between $25,000 and $50,000 worth of TKO stock in March.

A federal judge refused to block the event on Friday, allowing organizers to proceed with Sunday's spectacle.

The White House called the lawsuit "obstructionist, baseless, and dilatory," stating the event "will undoubtedly go down as one of the most historic sporting events in our Nation's history."

Weather could complicate Sunday's proceedings. The National Weather Service forecast for June 14 indicates a chance of showers and thunderstorms, with conditions expected to deteriorate into the evening when the main event is scheduled to begin.

5:08 AM ET

Malorie Blackman, author of the Noughts & Crosses series, and Julia Donaldson, creator of The Gruffalo, have been recognised as dames in the latest honours list. A rugby league star also received a knighthood in the same announcement.

Blackman's Noughts & Crosses series stands as a landmark work in young adult fiction. The novels are set in an alternate Britain called Albion, colonised centuries earlier by Africa. In this inverted world, Black citizens hold political and economic power while white citizens form the underclass, facing segregation and structural disadvantage. The series explores themes of racism and social division through this alternative history framework.

The first book in the series was Blackman's 50th publication. It arrived at a significant cultural moment. "I sat down at my computer really angry," Blackman explained in an interview about the work's origins. The 1990s saw the murder of Stephen Lawrence and the Macpherson report's findings on institutional racism within the Metropolitan Police. "It was my way of channelling that anger," she said.

Before publication, Blackman encountered resistance to the project. "People were telling me, 'Oh, no one wants to read about racism.' And I thought – that's interesting. You haven't read it. You don't know what it is. You're already making assumptions," she recalled.

The series eventually expanded far beyond its original trilogy, incorporating books including Knife Edge, Checkmate, Crossfire and Endgame. As global events unfolded, including Brexit, Donald Trump's election and a worldwide pandemic, the fictional world of Albion reflected contemporary reality back to readers. "The world just kept giving me material," Blackman noted.

On publication, Noughts & Crosses proved radical for British young adult literature. Few novels had tackled racism with such force while trusting young readers with moral complexity. The work subsequently appeared in the BBC's Big Read poll of the nation's favourite novels, was adapted for stage and television by the BBC, and became a staple of school curricula across the UK.

When asked if she had predicted the book's success, Blackman was modest about the outcome. "When you sit down to write, you don't know if it's going to sink or swim," she said. "You don't know even if anyone's going to read it. And the fact that so many people have, and have come up to me and said what it meant to them, I feel very, very lucky."

Julia Donaldson's picture books, illustrated by Axel Scheffler, have sold millions of copies worldwide and become foundational texts for early childhood reading. Both authors' recognition in the honours list acknowledges their profound influence on British literature and reading culture across generations.

Authors Malorie Blackman and Julia Donaldson Named Dames in Honours List

Malorie Blackman, author of the Noughts & Crosses series, and Julia Donaldson, creator of The Gruffalo, have been recognised as dames in the latest honours list. A rugby league star also received a knighthood in the same announcement.

Blackman's Noughts & Crosses series stands as a landmark work in young adult fiction. The novels are set in an alternate Britain called Albion, colonised centuries earlier by Africa. In this inverted world, Black citizens hold political and economic power while white citizens form the underclass, facing segregation and structural disadvantage. The series explores themes of racism and social division through this alternative history framework.

The first book in the series was Blackman's 50th publication. It arrived at a significant cultural moment. "I sat down at my computer really angry," Blackman explained in an interview about the work's origins. The 1990s saw the murder of Stephen Lawrence and the Macpherson report's findings on institutional racism within the Metropolitan Police. "It was my way of channelling that anger," she said.

Before publication, Blackman encountered resistance to the project. "People were telling me, 'Oh, no one wants to read about racism.' And I thought – that's interesting. You haven't read it. You don't know what it is. You're already making assumptions," she recalled.

The series eventually expanded far beyond its original trilogy, incorporating books including Knife Edge, Checkmate, Crossfire and Endgame. As global events unfolded, including Brexit, Donald Trump's election and a worldwide pandemic, the fictional world of Albion reflected contemporary reality back to readers. "The world just kept giving me material," Blackman noted.

On publication, Noughts & Crosses proved radical for British young adult literature. Few novels had tackled racism with such force while trusting young readers with moral complexity. The work subsequently appeared in the BBC's Big Read poll of the nation's favourite novels, was adapted for stage and television by the BBC, and became a staple of school curricula across the UK.

When asked if she had predicted the book's success, Blackman was modest about the outcome. "When you sit down to write, you don't know if it's going to sink or swim," she said. "You don't know even if anyone's going to read it. And the fact that so many people have, and have come up to me and said what it meant to them, I feel very, very lucky."

Julia Donaldson's picture books, illustrated by Axel Scheffler, have sold millions of copies worldwide and become foundational texts for early childhood reading. Both authors' recognition in the honours list acknowledges their profound influence on British literature and reading culture across generations.
