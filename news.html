Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney has approved a West Coast pipeline project designed to transport Alberta oil to the Pacific Coast, allowing the country to expand its oil exports beyond dependence on the United States. The decision represents a significant policy shift for Canada's oil industry and addresses a longstanding demand from Alberta's provincial government.

The pipeline would connect Alberta's landlocked oil reserves to the Pacific Ocean, opening new export routes to Asian markets. Canada has historically relied heavily on the United States as its primary oil export market, making diversification a key economic priority for the energy-dependent province of Alberta. The project's approval signals federal support for expanding the country's oil infrastructure despite ongoing debates about fossil fuel development.

The pipeline route would run to British Columbia's South Coast, requiring coordination between federal authorities and provincial governments. Alberta officials have pitched the project to British Columbia as an economic opportunity that would create construction jobs and generate long-term revenue through increased oil shipments. The pipeline's capacity and construction timeline were not specified in the initial announcement.

The approval comes as Canada seeks to balance its energy sector interests with environmental commitments. Oil remains a critical component of Canada's economy, particularly in Alberta, where petroleum extraction and processing employ thousands of workers and contribute significantly to provincial revenues. Expanding export capacity could increase production levels and generate additional revenue for both the provincial and federal governments.

The decision positions Canada to compete more effectively in global oil markets by reducing its reliance on US refineries and export infrastructure. Asian markets, particularly in China, Japan, and South Korea, represent growing demand centers for crude oil. The pipeline would provide Canadian producers with alternative buyers and potentially better pricing for their product, though construction challenges and regulatory requirements could delay the project's completion by several years.

The pipeline approval comes at a time when Carney faces other challenges to Canada's unity. Independence movements are gaining traction in two provinces, threatening the country's territorial integrity. A recent court decision in Alberta blocked a separatist-led referendum petition after a judge found that First Nations communities had not been properly consulted about the potential impacts of secession on their treaty rights.

Meanwhile, uncertainty surrounds Canada's major trade relationship with the United States. The Trump administration has refused to renew the North American trade pact, opting instead for annual reviews rather than a longer-term commitment. This shift in US trade policy adds another layer of complexity to Carney's efforts to manage Canada's economic relationships and maintain national stability while pursuing the country's oil export ambitions.

Scientists build synthetic cell that grows and divides

Researchers have created a synthetic cell capable of feeding, growing, and reproducing, representing a significant milestone in bioengineering. The cell, called SpudCell, was constructed from chemical compounds rather than modified from natural organisms, and scientists believe it is the first to demonstrate a complete cell cycle including growth, genetic replication, and divis...

Yesterday, 8:33 PM ET

Researchers have created a synthetic cell capable of feeding, growing, and reproducing, representing a significant milestone in bioengineering. The cell, called SpudCell, was constructed from chemical compounds rather than modified from natural organisms, and scientists believe it is the first to demonstrate a complete cell cycle including growth, genetic replication, and division.

Dr. Kate Adamala, who led the research at the University of Minnesota, explained the importance of understanding every component of a cell. "It is not as robust, as fast, or as good at most of its functions as a natural cell, but it is proof of principle that molecules can reconstitute behaviours that up until now we only associated with natural living cells," she said.

The team built SpudCells from basic components to ensure complete knowledge of each part. They started with tiny water-filled spheres called liposomes, measured in thousandths of a millimetre, and added synthetic DNA to provide basic functions. The name SpudCell references both Sputnik and Adamala's Polish heritage, as she noted with humor.

SpudCells operate in a liquid containing vital chemicals like ATP, the energy-carrying molecule that living cells normally produce. To grow, SpudCells fuse with smaller "feeder" liposomes containing enzymes, ribosomes, and molecules needed for protein production. The cell's genetic instructions enable it to copy its genome and divide.

The researchers demonstrated that SpudCells with genetic advantages could outcompete original cells, mimicking evolutionary principles. However, the synthetic cells have significant limitations. They cannot build their own protein-making machinery, control metabolism, or eliminate waste. When dividing, they often distribute DNA incorrectly and stop functioning after several generations.

Prof. Tom Ellis from Imperial College London called the work probably the field's "biggest breakthrough in recent times." He noted that synthetic cells help determine life's minimum requirements and provide "a fully understood system for testing biological circuits and computer models of cellular life."

The work raises prospects for artificial organisms designed to produce drugs, foods, fuels, and other materials. However, it also prompts questions about ethical boundaries and safety protocols as synthetic biology advances.

Adamala emphasized that SpudCells are not alive but could serve as a foundation for building life. She described watching them divide as striking. "They're among the most beautiful images I've seen, but obviously I'm biased. To most people, looking at it under the microscope, it doesn't look like much. It's a blob."

Adamala and colleagues are launching an institution called Biotic to advance synthetic cells further. According to co-founder Prof. Drew Endy from Stanford University, the goal is building "an operating system for life" made from genes and biochemistry.

The study was released as a preprint before peer review, allowing other laboratories to examine the work immediately.

Scientists build synthetic cell that grows and divides

Researchers have created a synthetic cell capable of feeding, growing, and reproducing, representing a significant milestone in bioengineering. The cell, called SpudCell, was constructed from chemical compounds rather th...

1:00 AM ET

Researchers have created a synthetic cell capable of feeding, growing, and reproducing, representing a significant milestone in bioengineering. The cell, called SpudCell, was constructed from chemical compounds rather than modified from natural organisms, and scientists believe it is the first to demonstrate a complete cell cycle including growth, genetic replication, and division.

Dr. Kate Adamala, who led the research at the University of Minnesota, explained the importance of understanding every component of a cell. "It is not as robust, as fast, or as good at most of its functions as a natural cell, but it is proof of principle that molecules can reconstitute behaviours that up until now we only associated with natural living cells," she said.

The team built SpudCells from basic components to ensure complete knowledge of each part. They started with tiny water-filled spheres called liposomes, measured in thousandths of a millimetre, and added synthetic DNA to provide basic functions. The name SpudCell references both Sputnik and Adamala's Polish heritage, as she noted with humor.

SpudCells operate in a liquid containing vital chemicals like ATP, the energy-carrying molecule that living cells normally produce. To grow, SpudCells fuse with smaller "feeder" liposomes containing enzymes, ribosomes, and molecules needed for protein production. The cell's genetic instructions enable it to copy its genome and divide.

The researchers demonstrated that SpudCells with genetic advantages could outcompete original cells, mimicking evolutionary principles. However, the synthetic cells have significant limitations. They cannot build their own protein-making machinery, control metabolism, or eliminate waste. When dividing, they often distribute DNA incorrectly and stop functioning after several generations.

Prof. Tom Ellis from Imperial College London called the work probably the field's "biggest breakthrough in recent times." He noted that synthetic cells help determine life's minimum requirements and provide "a fully understood system for testing biological circuits and computer models of cellular life."

The work raises prospects for artificial organisms designed to produce drugs, foods, fuels, and other materials. However, it also prompts questions about ethical boundaries and safety protocols as synthetic biology advances.

Adamala emphasized that SpudCells are not alive but could serve as a foundation for building life. She described watching them divide as striking. "They're among the most beautiful images I've seen, but obviously I'm biased. To most people, looking at it under the microscope, it doesn't look like much. It's a blob."

Adamala and colleagues are launching an institution called Biotic to advance synthetic cells further. According to co-founder Prof. Drew Endy from Stanford University, the goal is building "an operating system for life" made from genes and biochemistry.

The study was released as a preprint before peer review, allowing other laboratories to examine the work immediately.
