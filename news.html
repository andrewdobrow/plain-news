Trump administration launches large-scale denaturalization effort targeting fraud cases

The Trump administration has directed the Justice Department to pursue hundreds of denaturalization cases against naturalized U.S. citizens, marking what officials describe as the largest such effort in American history. A few minutes ago

The Trump administration has directed the Justice Department to pursue hundreds of denaturalization cases against naturalized U.S. citizens, marking what officials describe as the largest such effort in American history. The Justice Department has already identified 384 foreign-born citizens whose citizenship it seeks to revoke, with the process expected to begin in the coming weeks.

Denaturalization allows the U.S. government to ask courts to remove citizenship from people who obtained it illegally. Cases typically involve applicants who lied to officials, entered false marriages, or committed crimes after becoming citizens. The process is ordinarily rare because it requires significant resources and funding.

According to reporting, the 384 identified individuals represent only the beginning of the administration's plans. Francey Hakes, a top Justice Department official, described this group as "the first wave of cases," characterizing the push as a "White House initiative." A White House spokesperson countered that the effort "isn't a White House initiative—it's federal law."

To handle the caseload, civil litigators across 39 regional Justice Department offices will be assigned to file denaturalization cases. Officials acknowledged that the scope may require additional staffing from other division offices. Legal experts have warned this could divert resources from other Justice Department priorities, including healthcare fraud cases.

The Justice Department must prove that a person lacks "good moral character" before a judge to justify denaturalization. Last year's Justice Department memo outlined broad categories of targets, including people with alleged ties to terrorism, gang members, and cartel members. Legal experts expressed concern that these criteria could be overly broad, noting the Trump administration's history of falsely accusing immigrants of gang affiliation and targeting political activists based on insufficient evidence.

Recent denaturalization filings have targeted immigrants with varied circumstances, including a Marine accused of a sex crime, an Argentine man accused of falsely claiming another nationality, and a Nigerian man convicted of tax fraud.

Denaturalization has a complex history in the United States. Throughout the 20th century, journalists, activists, and labor leaders were often targeted as alleged anarchists and communists. Politically motivated denaturalization declined after the late 1960s, when the Supreme Court ruled that denaturalization could only proceed upon proof of fraud or willful misrepresentation. The practice then narrowed, focusing primarily on former war criminals, including Nazis who misrepresented themselves on citizenship applications.

Denaturalization cases increased under the Obama administration and accelerated significantly during the first Trump administration, which attempted to examine 700,000 files. Between 2017 and late 2025, the U.S. stripped just over 120 naturalized citizens of their citizenship.

When denaturalization occurs, individuals revert to their previous immigration status. Because immigration matters are classified as civil rather than criminal proceedings, immigrants facing denaturalization have no right to legal representation.

Israel responded with airstrikes that struck Iranian defense systems across multiple areas and hit a petrochemical complex. However, Iranian officials rejected suggestions that Netanyahu had defied Trump's instructions. Esmaeil Baqaei, a foreign ministry spokesperson, stated that "No one believes that the Zionist regime would carry out any action without prior coordination and cooperation with the United States." This skepticism reflects concerns that a covert U.S. endorsement of the Israeli attack could damage stalled peace talks that Trump has claimed could produce a deal within days.

Iranian hardliners in parliament have pressured negotiators to abandon talks altogether, while others argue certain aspects of any potential deal need clarification. Stock markets in Asia, heavily dependent on oil imports, fell sharply in early trading, with concerns about broader regional conflict affecting energy supplies. European stocks also declined, particularly in semiconductor and chipmaking companies.

The de-escalation comes after heightened tensions threatened to spiral into wider conflict. The United States played a central role in mediating between the two long-time adversaries, though international observers noted the fragility of any ceasefire arrangement.

9:26 AM ET

Massachusetts has passed legislation that prohibits companies from selling precise location data about residents. The new privacy rights bill establishes broad restrictions on how businesses handle location information collected from smartphones and other devices.

The law represents a blanket ban across the state, preventing companies and startups from selling precise location data that tracks where individuals go. This type of data has become increasingly valuable to advertisers, data brokers, and analytics firms, who use it to build detailed profiles of consumer behavior and movement patterns.

The legislation positions Massachusetts among a small group of states taking direct action on location data privacy. While several states have passed comprehensive privacy laws in recent years, few have implemented outright bans on selling precise location information. The Massachusetts approach goes further than laws requiring consent or opt-out mechanisms by prohibiting the sale of this data entirely.

Precise location data differs from general location information by pinpointing where someone is within a few meters, rather than just identifying their city or region. This granular tracking has raised concerns among privacy advocates, who argue it can reveal sensitive details about people's lives, including visits to medical facilities, places of worship, or political gatherings. The data can also be used to identify where people live and work.

The bill's passage comes as lawmakers nationwide grapple with how to regulate the rapidly growing data broker industry. Companies have built billion-dollar businesses around collecting and selling personal information, often without explicit knowledge from the individuals being tracked. The Massachusetts law aims to close one avenue for this data collection by making it illegal to sell precise location information about state residents, regardless of how it was obtained or what purpose it serves.

The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act authorizes the government to conduct electronic surveillance and gather intelligence on foreign powers and their agents. The law requires periodic congressional reauthorization, and lawmakers are currently debating whether to extend its provisions as they approach expiration.

Bacon's public call for Trump to reconsider the appointment reflects divisions within the Republican Party regarding intelligence gathering authorities. Some GOP members have expressed doubts about FISA's implementation, particularly after previous revelations about surveillance applications that targeted American citizens. The Nebraska congressman appears concerned that Pulte's appointment could complicate efforts to secure the necessary votes for reauthorization.

The position of acting Director of National Intelligence carries significant responsibility, overseeing the 18 agencies that comprise the intelligence community and serving as the principal intelligence advisor to the president. Leadership changes at intelligence agencies typically occur during periods of congressional debate over surveillance programs, creating potential complications for legislative outcomes.

Bacon's intervention suggests he believes the appointment could influence how Congress votes on extending FISA authorities. Intelligence officials maintain that these surveillance powers remain essential to counterterrorism efforts and national security operations. However, the reauthorization debate has intensified as concerns about government surveillance and civil liberties continue to draw scrutiny from various quarters.

The timing of Bacon's statement highlights the tension between those who view FISA as necessary for protecting the nation and those who question its scope and implementation. A student astronomer has helped identify the source of mysterious radio signals that have been detected coming from space. The signals originate from an accreting white dwarf binary system that also emits periodic X-ray radiation.

The discovery provides scientists with what researchers are calling a breakthrough in understanding these cosmic signals. The white dwarf binary system produces both radio and X-ray emissions in a pattern that allowed astronomers to trace the signals back to their source.
