Federal and state officials confirmed the presence of the New World screwworm in a calf in southern Texas, approximately 50 miles from the Mexico border. This marks the first confirmed detection of the parasitic fly in the state since 1966 and represents a significant concern for the nation's cattle industry at a time when beef prices are at record highs.

The screwworm fly's larvae feed on the blood and flesh of warm-blooded animals like cattle. Female flies target open wounds to lay their eggs, and when the larvae hatch they quickly consume blood and tissue. Without treatment, infected animals will die. While the risk to humans is low, the parasite can spread rapidly in areas with large concentrations of livestock.

The United States successfully eradicated the screwworm in the 1970s after it caused tens of millions of dollars in losses. For more than a year, the USDA and Texas officials have warned ranchers about the parasite's advancement through Mexico, where it has traveled more than 1,100 miles from the south despite ongoing control efforts.

Texas Agriculture Commissioner Sid Miller expressed frustration with the USDA's response, stating that despite billions of sterile flies being dispersed, the screwworm still reached Texas. He argued the agency missed an important component of its containment strategy and called for increased deployment of the screwworm adult suppression system, known as Swass, which combines attractants, bait, and targeted insecticides.

Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins pushed back against concerns, insisting there is no threat of mass infestation and no reason to believe the parasite will become established in the country. She noted the Texas case was the only confirmed case so far.

The USDA has previously relied on releasing sterile male flies to break the breeding cycle, since female screwworm flies mate only once in their months-long lives and do not reproduce with sterile males. In March, Rollins announced the USDA would partner with the Army Corps of Engineers and Mortenson Construction to build a new sterile fly production facility at Moore Airbase in Edinburg, Texas.

Past eradication campaigns proved highly successful, leading the US to shut down most sterile fly breeding facilities decades ago, with only one remaining in Panama. In Mexico, high treatment costs have forced some ranchers to use crude measures like applying gasoline or lime to open wounds to remove worms.

The detection has prompted neighboring states to issue alerts and monitor for potential spread across state lines as officials work to assess the scope of the outbreak and prevent the parasite from establishing a permanent presence in the region.

5:00 PM ET

Security researchers have discovered a critical vulnerability that allows hackers to compromise Windows computers through certain USB-connected speakers without any user interaction. The exploit, called Pwnd Blaster, targets Creative's Sound Blaster Katana V2X speaker system, which retails for approximately $300.

The attack works by exploiting the Bluetooth functionality built into the speaker when it is connected to a PC via USB. Researchers demonstrated that an attacker within Bluetooth range can send malicious commands through the speaker to the connected computer, gaining unauthorized access without the user touching the device or clicking anything. The vulnerability represents a new attack vector because it bypasses traditional security measures that rely on detecting suspicious user behavior.

Creative, the manufacturer of the affected speaker, does not consider the behavior a security vulnerability. The company has not indicated whether it plans to release a firmware update or other fix to address the issue. This stance has drawn criticism from security professionals who argue that any device capable of compromising a computer without user consent represents a significant security risk.

The discovery has prompted concern among PC users about the security implications of connected peripherals. Security experts recommend that users consider disconnecting USB microphones and speakers when not in use, particularly in environments where sensitive data is handled. The attack highlights how peripheral devices with wireless capabilities can create unexpected security holes in otherwise protected systems.

The Pwnd Blaster exploit adds to growing concerns about the security of Internet of Things devices and connected peripherals. As more consumer electronics incorporate wireless connectivity and direct system access through USB connections, the potential attack surface for malicious actors continues to expand. Security researchers continue to investigate whether similar vulnerabilities exist in other USB-connected audio devices from different manufacturers.

British actor Anthony Head, best known for playing Rupert Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, has died at age 72. He passed away peacefully from complications related to pneumonia, surrounded by his family, according to a statement from his daughters Emily and Daisy Head.

"It has been, and forever will be, an honour and a privilege to be his daughters, and to have witnessed first-hand the impact both he and his work have had on so many," they said.

Head gained prominence for his role as the mentor and school librarian in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, serving as a father figure to Sarah Michelle Gellar's character throughout the show's seven-season run. His portrayal made him recognizable to audiences worldwide. More recently, he appeared in the comedy series Ted Lasso, where he played former football club owner Rupert Mannion and received praise for his on-screen chemistry with co-creator Jason Sudeikis.

His television career extended across multiple genres and decades. He took a recurring role in the BBC sketch show Little Britain, appearing as a British prime minister constantly fawned over by a flirtatious assistant played by David Walliams. He also appeared in the BBC production of Merlin. Beyond television, Head featured in films including The Iron Lady and The Inbetweeners Movie.

Before achieving international fame, Head established himself on the British stage. He starred in the 1978 West End revival of Godspell and later took the lead role as Freddie Trumper in the original West End production of the Abba musical Chess in 1988. He also performed as Dr Frank-N-Furter in the 1990 West End production of The Rocky Horror Show.

In the 1980s, Head sang backing vocals for the British pop group Red Box and was featured on their 1986 debut album The Circle and the Square. He became widely recognized in the UK and US for his appearances in Nescafé Gold Blend television advertisements, which depicted the slow-burn courtship between a British couple.

Head was born in Camden, London, and raised by artistic parents. His father was a documentary filmmaker who founded Verity Films, while his mother was an actor who played Madame Maigret in the 1960s BBC crime drama Maigret. His brother also pursued an acting career.

When filming Buffy the Vampire Slayer required him to move to Los Angeles, Head maintained his commitment to his family in England. He arranged his work schedule to return home every three or four weeks, spending considerable money on airfares to see his partner Sarah and two young daughters.

Sarah Fisher, Head's partner, died in December 2025 at age 61. His daughters noted that his legacy will continue through the shows he appeared in and the audiences who cherish them.

Researchers demonstrated that an attacker within Bluetooth range can send malicious commands through the speaker to the connected computer, gaining unauthorized access without the user touching the device or clicking anything. The vulnerability represents a new attack vector because it bypasses traditional security measures that rely on detecting suspicious user behavior.

Creative, the manufacturer of the affected speaker, does not consider the behavior a security vulnerability. The company has not indicated whether it plans to release a firmware update or other fix to address the issue. This stance has drawn criticism from security professionals who argue that any device capable of compromising a computer without user consent represents a significant security risk.

The discovery has prompted concern among PC users about the security implications of connected peripherals. Security experts recommend that users consider disconnecting USB microphones and speakers when not in use, particularly in environments where sensitive data is handled. The attack highlights how peripheral devices with wireless capabilities can create unexpected security holes in otherwise protected systems.

The Pwnd Blaster exploit adds to growing concerns about the security of Internet of Things devices and connected peripherals. As more consumer electronics incorporate wireless connectivity and direct system access through USB connections, the potential attack surface for malicious actors continues to expand. Security researchers continue to investigate whether similar vulnerabilities exist in other USB-connected audio devices from different manufacturers.
