Federal Judge Blocks Trump's $100,000 Fee for H-1B Visas

A federal judge has ruled that the Trump administration's $100,000 annual fee on H-1B visa applications is unlawful, striking down a policy that would have dramatically increased costs for employers seeking to hire foreign skilled workers. US District Judge Leo Sorokin in Boston issued a 42-page ruling in response to a lawsuit filed by 20 Democratic state attorneys general. Th...

6:59 PM ET

A federal judge has ruled that the Trump administration's $100,000 annual fee on H-1B visa applications is unlawful, striking down a policy that would have dramatically increased costs for employers seeking to hire foreign skilled workers.

US District Judge Leo Sorokin in Boston issued a 42-page ruling in response to a lawsuit filed by 20 Democratic state attorneys general. The judge determined that the fee constituted an unlawful tax that violated federal administrative law and the constitution. Since the constitution grants Congress, not the president, exclusive power to levy taxes, Trump lacked the authority to impose the charge through executive action.

In his decision, Sorokin cited the 2026 Supreme Court case Learning Resources v Trump, which previously limited Trump's aggressive tariff strategy, as precedent for his ruling. The Trump administration had attempted to justify the fee under the Immigration and Nationality Act, but Sorokin found that this legislation did not grant the president authority to impose such a tax.

Trump announced the fee in September through presidential proclamation, claiming the H-1B program had enabled the "large-scale replacement of American workers." Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick stated at the time that major tech companies supported the measure and urged firms to train American workers rather than hire from abroad. The $100,000 fee represented a dramatic 20-to-50 fold increase compared to existing visa fees, making it one of the highest fees in the US immigration system.

The ruling provides significant relief to the technology sector, which relies on H-1B visas more heavily than any other industry. According to the decision, Amazon alone secured more than 10,000 H-1B visas approved in the first half of 2025, while Microsoft and Meta each exceeded 5,000 approvals. Roughly two-thirds of H-1B positions are computer-related roles.

The H-1B program, created in 1990, allows US employers to hire skilled foreign workers in specialty occupations for up to six years. The program issues 65,000 visas annually, plus an additional 20,000 for advanced degree holders. Demand for these visas typically far exceeds the available supply.

The court's decision means employers will not face the $100,000 charge when applying for H-1B visas. The ruling affects companies across multiple sectors that depend on the program to recruit international talent. The Trump administration has not yet announced whether it will appeal the decision, though legal analysts widely expect an appeal attempt.

The judgment represents a major setback for the administration's effort to reshape immigration policy through increased costs and restrictions on visa programs. Companies that had prepared for the fee increase can now proceed with their international hiring plans without the substantial additional expense.

The United States Department of Defense has designated several major Chinese technology companies as supporting the Chinese military, adding them to a blacklist that restricts American investment. The list includes electric vehicle maker BYD, e-commerce giant Alibaba, and search engine company Baidu, according to the Pentagon announcement.

The designation marks a significant escalation in U.S. efforts to limit Chinese military advancement through commercial technology partnerships. Companies placed on the list face restrictions on receiving American capital, as U.S. investors are prohibited from holding stakes in entities deemed to be supporting Chinese military modernization efforts. The Pentagon's classification stems from findings that these firms provide technological support or resources that enhance the capabilities of China's armed forces.

BYD, which has become one of the world's largest electric vehicle manufacturers, and Alibaba, which operates major cloud computing and e-commerce platforms, represent cornerstone companies in China's technology sector. Baidu, often called China's Google, operates artificial intelligence research divisions alongside its search engine business. The inclusion of such prominent firms signals Washington's broadening interpretation of which commercial activities constitute military support.

The blacklist had previously included these companies before being temporarily adjusted, making this move a restoration rather than an initial designation. The Pentagon maintains the list as part of regulations requiring annual updates on Chinese military companies operating in commercial markets. American institutional investors and pension funds that hold shares in these companies will need to divest their positions to comply with the restrictions.

The designation comes during a period of heightened tension between Washington and Beijing over technology access and national security concerns. U.S. officials have increasingly focused on preventing American capital and technology from contributing to Chinese military capabilities, particularly in areas like artificial intelligence, advanced manufacturing, and aerospace. The Chinese government has previously criticized such measures as economic interference and protectionism disguised as security policy.

The filing represents a preliminary step toward the company's eventual public debut on Wall Street, where it is expected to achieve a valuation exceeding $850 billion, making it one of the most highly valued listings in market history.

In its announcement, OpenAI acknowledged the unconventional nature of going public with a confidential filing. "We expect it to leak so we're just announcing it," the company stated. The confidential filing process allows regulators to review the company's financial disclosures privately before the public and investors gain access to the documents.

The company emphasized that no timeline has been set for its market debut. "We have not decided on timing yet; it may be a while because there are things we want to do that are likely easier as a private company," OpenAI noted. The statement reflects the company's assessment that remaining private offers certain advantages, though going public would provide access to additional capital.

OpenAI's IPO filing follows its main competitor Anthropic, which submitted its own confidential filing about a week earlier. The two companies have been locked in a competitive race to reach the public markets, with both developing large language models and competing across the AI industry. Anthropic's Claude chatbot represents a direct challenge to OpenAI's ChatGPT, which launched publicly in 2022 and rapidly accumulated hundreds of millions of users.

The timing reflects favorable market conditions for AI companies seeking public investment. OpenAI itself raised $122 billion in a funding round completed in March, valuing the company at approximately $852 billion. The company achieved this substantial valuation despite challenges in turning a profit and missing certain revenue and user growth targets.

OpenAI's path to this IPO milestone has involved significant legal and business developments. The company recently won a lawsuit brought by Elon Musk, who sought to force OpenAI to revert to its original non-profit structure. A jury panel ruled the case fell outside the statute of limitations, clearing a potential legal obstacle to the company's public market ambitions.

The company faces additional scrutiny from over a dozen lawsuits alleging that ChatGPT has contributed to mental health crises and violent incidents. Despite these legal challenges, OpenAI has continued to secure major partnerships with technology firms including Microsoft, Google, and Nvidia, as well as deals with the Trump administration.

The IPO announcement comes during a significant period for artificial intelligence companies seeking public markets. SpaceX, which owns Elon Musk's xAI company, is also reportedly preparing for a public offering at an expected valuation of $1.75 trillion. Together, these filings signal growing investor confidence in the AI sector's future potential.

Arthur Miller's Death of a Salesman dominated the 2026 Tony Awards, taking home six awards and establishing itself as the most awarded play revival in Tony history. The stripped-back production, directed by Joe Mantello, won best revival of a play along with multiple acting honors during the ceremony hosted by singer Pink.

Lesley Manville won the award for leading actress in a play for her performance in Oedipus, making her Broadway debut. "I'm a bit overwhelmed, it was my first time on Broadway so this is such a big deal," she said on stage. John Lithgow took home the leading actor in a play award for his role as Roald Dahl in the antisemitism drama Giant. At 80, Lithgow made history as the oldest man to ever win a competitive acting Tony. "It's an extraordinarily important play of this moment," he said.

Laurie Metcalf also earned recognition from Death of a Salesman, winning her third Tony for featured actress in a play. Nathan Lane accepted the best revival award on behalf of the cast, describing the play as one that "continues to teach us who we are as humans and Americans." Director Joe Mantello praised Miller's story as one that "still talks to us through time."

Beyond Death of a Salesman, several other productions earned major recognition. Bess Wohl's Liberation was named best play after recently winning the Pulitzer Prize for drama. "This is the honour of a lifetime," Wohl said. Her win marks the first time a female playwright has won the Tony for best play since 2009, making her only the fourth woman to receive this honor.

In the musical categories, Schmigadoon!, the stage version of a cancelled Apple TV series, won best musical. Producer Lorne Michaels said in his acceptance speech, "Sometimes singing, dancing, a lot of jokes and a happy ending is really all you need."

A new take on Ragtime won four Tonys, including best revival of a musical. Joshua Henry and Caissie Levy won for leading actor and leading actress in a musical, respectively. This marked Henry's first win after four previous nominations. "Every artist in this room, every artist at home, fight, fight, fight to be heard," Henry said.

The Lost Boys, an adaptation of the 1980s vampire film, picked up four awards including featured acting honors in a musical. Cats: The Jellicle Ball, a queer ballroom reinvention of Andrew Lloyd Webber's classic, won three awards. Costume designer Qween Jean made history as the first openly transgender winner of a Tony Award.

The ceremony celebrated a record-breaking Broadway season that generated nearly 1.91 billion dollars in ticket sales. Pink opened the show flying in as Peter Pan and led a performance of her Moulin Rouge hit "Lady Marmalade" with guests including Lea Michele and Megan Thee Stallion.
