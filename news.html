The depletion stems from the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint for global oil shipment, dur...

8:00 AM ET

US crude oil inventories have dropped to their lowest level in more than two decades as the conflict with Iran disrupts global supply chains. The stockpiles posted their sixth consecutive weekly decline, according to data from multiple sources tracking the drawdown.

The depletion stems from the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint for global oil shipment, during the ongoing Iran conflict. The strait's closure has forced the US to tap its reserves while simultaneously cutting off a major supply route for crude imports. Financial Times reported the reserves have fallen to levels not seen since 2004, raising concerns about the nation's energy security during the prolonged standoff.

Despite what analysts are calling the biggest supply shock in history, oil prices have not reached the triple-digit levels some predicted. Bloomberg reported that prices remain well below the $200 per barrel threshold that typically accompanies such severe disruptions. The muted price response suggests other market factors, including demand adjustments and alternative supply routes, are offsetting some of the supply pressure.

The sustained inventory decline marks an unusual pattern for US crude reserves. Six straight weeks of falling stockpiles indicate both the severity of the supply disruption and the extent to which domestic consumption and international commitments are drawing down available reserves. MarketWatch noted that while the headline numbers appear dire, the full story of US energy security involves multiple factors beyond just crude inventory levels.

The situation presents a strategic challenge for US energy policy as the conflict with Iran continues with no clear resolution timeline. The combination of depleted reserves and ongoing supply disruptions leaves less cushion for future shocks or extended conflict scenarios.

7:50 AM ET

A flesh-eating parasite thought to be eradicated has returned to Texas, triggering emergency responses and trade restrictions that are reshaping North American cattle markets. The reemergence of the new world screwworm has created starkly different economic conditions on either side of the U.S.-Mexico border.

The screwworm, absent from the United States since the 1960s, was detected in two calves in Zavala County, southwest of San Antonio. The parasitic larvae burrow through living flesh and can kill animals if left untreated. The discovery prompted Texas Governor Greg Abbott to declare a state of disaster and mobilize resources to contain the outbreak.

Authorities have launched an aggressive containment strategy. The Texas Animal Health Commission established an infested zone and imposed animal movement restrictions to prevent spread. Officials are dispersing millions of sterile screwworm flies in the affected area, where the two infections were found about five miles apart. The sterile males prevent wild females from reproducing, a method that has proven effective in controlling the pest.

The response extends beyond Texas borders. Canada announced a temporary ban on livestock from Texas after the infections were confirmed. Animals that were in Texas within 21 days before crossing into Canada will not be accepted, though Canadian officials noted the parasite is unlikely to become established in the country's colder climate.

The outbreak arrives as the U.S. cattle industry faces an existing trade crisis. The U.S.-Mexico border has been closed to live cattle imports for more than a year as the disease moved northward from Central America. This closure has already contracted the $100 billion U.S. beef industry to a 75-year low. The United States formerly imported more than 1 million cattle annually from Mexico, representing approximately 4 to 5 percent of all cattle sold for U.S. beef production.

For Texas ranchers, the situation presents mounting economic pressure. The border restrictions prevent livestock from flowing to Mexican processing facilities, creating a supply glut at home while cutting off Mexican buyers from their usual cattle sources. Mexican producers and processors are capitalizing on the limited supply and reduced competition, driving prices upward south of the border.

The Texas Animal Health Commission emphasized that preparations for a screwworm resurgence had been underway for over two years. The agency stated that combining sterile fly releases with enhanced surveillance, movement restrictions, prompt treatment of wounds, and education efforts represents a proven approach to controlling and eradicating the pest.

Governor Abbott warned that it will likely be "an extraordinarily challenging summer" for infected areas. As Texas enters peak screwworm season, authorities have not indicated when border closures might be lifted, leaving the timeline for normalizing trade uncertain as officials work to control the first U.S. screwworm cases in decades.

A few minutes ago

President Trump this week granted a full pardon to former Republican Representative Stephen Buyer of Indiana, who was convicted of insider trading for using nonpublic information to purchase stocks after leaving Congress.

A White House proclamation dated June 4 announced that Buyer would receive a "full, complete, and unconditional pardon." The announcement provided no explanation for the decision or details about whether Buyer had requested the pardon.

Buyer served in the House representing Indiana before his conviction. He was found guilty of insider trading for using information not available to the public to make stock purchases following the end of his congressional service. Federal prosecutors successfully built a case against him on the charges.

The pardon represents one of several clemency actions issued by Trump during his current term. Presidential pardons are an executive power granted by the Constitution that allows the president to forgive federal crimes and restore certain civil rights to convicted individuals. The president may exercise this authority without providing justification or receiving approval from Congress or the courts.

The timing of the pardon follows standard procedures for announcing presidential clemency. It was issued midweek with the public announcement coming on Saturday, consistent with how the White House typically handles such proclamations.

Buyer had faced potential prison time and fines as a result of his conviction, though the White House proclamation did not specify the current status of any sentence he may have been serving.

The pardon joins other high-profile clemency decisions made during Trump's tenure. Former members of Congress who have been convicted of federal crimes occasionally receive pardons from sitting presidents, though such actions frequently attract attention from government watchdog groups and political critics. The lack of explanation from the White House in Buyer's case follows Trump's broader approach of issuing clemency actions without providing public reasoning for his decisions.

The insider trading conviction centered on allegations that Buyer leveraged confidential information obtained through his position in Congress to make financial gains after leaving office. Such actions constitute a serious breach of the public trust, as lawmakers are prohibited from trading on information they access through their official duties.

The pardon represents a notable move given that insider trading convictions are typically viewed as clear violations of federal law designed to protect market integrity and prevent public officials from profiting from their positions. Buyer's case had been successfully prosecuted through the federal court system before the pardon eliminated the conviction.

1:00 PM ET

A flesh-eating parasite thought to be eradicated has returned to Texas, triggering emergency responses and trade restrictions that are reshaping North American cattle markets. The reemergence of the new world screwworm has created starkly different economic conditions on either side of the U.S.-Mexico border.

The screwworm, absent from the United States since the 1960s, was detected in two calves in Zavala County, southwest of San Antonio. The parasitic larvae burrow through living flesh and can kill animals if left untreated. The discovery prompted Texas Governor Greg Abbott to declare a state of disaster and mobilize resources to contain the outbreak.

Authorities have launched an aggressive containment strategy. The Texas Animal Health Commission established an infested zone and imposed animal movement restrictions to prevent spread. Officials are dispersing millions of sterile screwworm flies in the affected area, where the two infections were found about five miles apart. The sterile males prevent wild females from reproducing, a method that has proven effective in controlling the pest.

The response extends beyond Texas borders. Canada announced a temporary ban on livestock from Texas after the infections were confirmed. Animals that were in Texas within 21 days before crossing into Canada will not be accepted, though Canadian officials noted the parasite is unlikely to become established in the country's colder climate.

The outbreak arrives as the U.S. cattle industry faces an existing trade crisis. The U.S.-Mexico border has been closed to live cattle imports for more than a year as the disease moved northward from Central America. This closure has already contracted the $100 billion U.S. beef industry to a 75-year low. The United States formerly imported more than 1 million cattle annually from Mexico, representing approximately 4 to 5 percent of all cattle sold for U.S. beef production.

For Texas ranchers, the situation presents mounting economic pressure. The border restrictions prevent livestock from flowing to Mexican processing facilities, creating a supply glut at home while cutting off Mexican buyers from their usual cattle sources. Mexican producers and processors are capitalizing on the limited supply and reduced competition, driving prices upward south of the border.

The Texas Animal Health Commission emphasized that preparations for a screwworm resurgence had been underway for over two years. The agency stated that combining sterile fly releases with enhanced surveillance, movement restrictions, prompt treatment of wounds, and education efforts represents a proven approach to controlling and eradicating the pest.

Governor Abbott warned that it will likely be "an extraordinarily challenging summer" for infected areas. As Texas enters peak screwworm season, authorities have not indicated when border closures might be lifted, leaving the timeline for normalizing trade uncertain as officials work to control the first U.S. screwworm cases in decades.
