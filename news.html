5:22 PM ET

The US government has ordered Anthropic to suspend access to its most advanced AI models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, for all foreign nationals, citing national security concerns. The company received an export control directive without being given specific details about the security issues involved.

Anthropic announced it will "abruptly disable" these models for all users in response to the government order. The restrictions mark a significant shift in how the US regulates access to cutting-edge artificial intelligence technology, applying export controls typically reserved for physical hardware to AI systems themselves.

The move comes after Anthropic disclosed that unauthorised users gained access to its Mythos model through a third-party vendor environment. Bloomberg reported that a small group of people in a private online forum accessed the model on the same day Anthropic began releasing it to select companies including Apple and Goldman Sachs for testing. One of the unauthorised users worked as a contractor for Anthropic and deployed methods used by cybersecurity researchers to gain access. According to Bloomberg, the group was primarily interested in "playing around" with the technology rather than causing harm, and had not run cybersecurity tests on the model.

The incident raised alarms among government authorities who have expressed serious concerns about Mythos's capabilities. The UK's AI Security Institute warned that Mythos represents a "step up" from previous models in terms of cyber-threat potential. The model can identify software vulnerabilities and conduct attacks requiring multiple actions without human intervention, tasks that would normally take human professionals days to complete. Mythos successfully completed a 32-step cyber-attack simulation created by the security institute in three of ten attempts.

Kanishka Narayan, the UK's AI minister, cautioned that UK businesses "should be worried" about the model's ability to detect flaws in IT systems that hackers could exploit.

Anthropic's CEO stated that research leading to the US export restrictions was conducted for defense and cybersecurity purposes. The CEO emphasised that the work was not a jailbreak of the AI systems but rather legitimate security testing.

The government's decision to impose export controls on AI model access rather than just underlying hardware represents a new approach to technology regulation. As AI companies continue developing increasingly capable systems, US officials are taking a more proactive stance on controlling access to advanced tools. The restrictions aim to maintain American advantages in AI development while preventing potential adversaries from accessing systems that could be used for military or intelligence purposes.

The move could set a precedent for how other leading AI systems are regulated in the future as national security concerns around advanced AI capabilities intensify.

The legal dispute over the display of Trump's name raised questions about the appropriate use of naming rights at federal institutions.

The removal represents a significant moment in the relationship between the Trump name and federal properties. The New York Knicks are one victory away from capturing their first NBA championship since 1973 after completing the largest comeback in finals history on Wednesday night. The Knicks defeated the San Antonio Spurs 107-106 in Game 4, giving them a 3-1 series lead heading into Saturday's Game 5 in San Antonio.

The Knicks mounted an extraordinary rally from a 29-point deficit, a margin that seemed insurmountable for most of the evening. OG Anunoby delivered the game-winning basket with 1.2 seconds remaining, tipping in a missed three-pointer from Jalen Brunson to send the home crowd into celebration. The victory represents the greatest comeback the NBA finals has ever witnessed.

The Spurs dominated the early stages of the contest, racing to a 41-22 lead after the first quarter while making 11 of their first 16 attempts from beyond the arc. San Antonio extended their advantage to 76-49 by halftime, the largest half-time lead by a visiting team in NBA finals history. Their lead ballooned to 29 points before the Knicks began their unlikely recovery.

New York's turnaround began in the third quarter when they held San Antonio to just 14 points on 4-for-20 shooting. The Spurs managed only three points from Victor Wembanyama during this period. A decisive 28-9 run in the fourth quarter transformed the game entirely, with the Knicks converting defensive stops into fast-break opportunities that shifted momentum dramatically.

Brunson delivered the go-ahead basket with 1 minute 22 seconds remaining, putting New York up 105-104. After the Spurs retook the lead on free throws, Brunson's contested three-pointer at the buzzer missed, but Anunoby slipped through the defense for the winning tip-in.

Anunoby finished with 33 points and made seven of nine three-point attempts, both career playoff highs. Brunson added 36 points and seven assists in the victory. Jose Alvarado contributed eight crucial points off the bench during the fourth-quarter surge.

Wembanyama led the Spurs with 24 points and 13 rebounds, while Dylan Harper added 21 points. De'Aaron Fox and Devin Vassell each scored 18 for San Antonio, which was outscored 58-30 in the second half and managed just 3-for-17 shooting from three-point range after intermission.

The Knicks can secure the franchise's third championship in team history with a victory on Saturday. Only one team in NBA finals history, LeBron James's 2016 Cleveland Cavaliers, has recovered from a 3-1 series deficit to claim the title. The Spurs must replicate that feat to keep their championship hopes alive.

Gene Shalit, the longtime movie critic for NBC's Today show, has died at 100. The television personality became a recognizable figure in American households through his nearly four decades on the morning program, distinguished by his distinctive appearance and clever approach to film criticism.

Shalit joined Today in 1970 and became the show's arts editor in 1973. He remained with the program until his retirement in 2010. His physical appearance became iconic: he was known for his puffy hair and oversized handlebar mustache, features that made him instantly recognizable to viewers across the country.

Beyond his memorable look, Shalit developed a reputation as a middle-of-the-road critic with a talent for wordplay. His reviews frequently incorporated puns and wit, delivering his assessments of films with clever language that became his signature style. This combination of humor and intelligence set him apart from other critics of his era.

As a film critic on one of America's most-watched morning programs, Shalit reached mainstream audiences at a time when television was becoming an increasingly important outlet for cultural commentary. His position on Today gave him a platform that extended far beyond traditional print criticism, making him one of the most recognizable film critics in American media.

Shalit's long career spanned a significant portion of cinema history, from the classic Hollywood era through the modern age of streaming entertainment. During his decades on television, he reviewed countless films for morning audiences, helping shape how millions of Americans thought about movies.

His passing was reported by major news outlets including The New York Times, BBC, CNN, and The Washington Post. Al Roker, a longtime Today show personality, shared a tribute following the announcement of Shalit's death.

The moustachioed reviewer's retirement in 2010 marked the end of an era for a particular style of television film criticism. His presence on Today had made him a fixture in the daily routines of American viewers, and his influence on how film criticism was presented on television remained significant throughout his life.

Shalit's death represents the end of a distinctive era in morning television programming, when personalities like him could become household names through their regular appearances on network shows. His combination of distinctive appearance, intelligent commentary, and accessible humor made him a unique figure in the history of American media.
