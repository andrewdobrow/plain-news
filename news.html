5:20 AM ET

Tropical Storm Dolly has formed in the Atlantic Ocean and is moving toward the Caribbean, according to the National Hurricane Center. The storm is traveling at a fast pace as forecasters track its projected path and potential impacts on the region.

The National Hurricane Center is monitoring the system's development and trajectory. Meteorologists are issuing regular updates as the storm progresses across Atlantic waters. The formation comes during the active portion of the Atlantic hurricane season, which typically peaks in late summer.

The storm's naming has drawn attention due to its timing following recent news events. Weather experts have noted what they describe as an "astounding coincidence" regarding the storm's name, though the naming follows the predetermined list established by the World Meteorological Organization for the Atlantic basin. The list of tropical storm names is established well in advance each year and does not take into account current events or news.

Forecasters are providing tracking maps and projected storm paths to help residents and officials in potentially affected areas prepare. The Caribbean region remains in the cone of uncertainty as meteorologists continue to refine predictions about where the storm will travel and what intensity it may reach. Tropical storms can strengthen or weaken depending on ocean temperatures, wind shear, and other atmospheric conditions.

Authorities in Caribbean nations are monitoring the storm's approach. Tropical Storm Dolly joins other systems that have formed this season as ocean temperatures remain warm enough to fuel tropical development. The National Hurricane Center continues to issue updates on the storm's speed, direction, and forecast intensity as it moves across the Atlantic toward populated areas.

Meanwhile, hurricane activity continues in other regions. In the eastern Pacific Ocean, Hurricane Fausto has formed far from Mexico's Baja California peninsula. The system posed no threat to land as of the latest reports. Fausto had top sustained winds of 75 miles per hour and was moving to the west-northwest at 9 miles per hour. Ocean swells generated by the storm could pummel the Baja California coast and cause life-threatening surf and rip tide conditions, forecasters said. No coastal watches or warnings were in effect for the system.

In the Atlantic basin, Tropical Storm Arthur formed earlier in the month and brought heavy rainfall to the southeastern United States. Bertha also developed as a second tropical storm of the season, affecting coastal areas along the Gulf of Mexico.

The National Hurricane Center advises residents in potentially affected areas to monitor official forecasts and follow guidance from local emergency management officials. Residents should prepare emergency supplies and have evacuation plans ready if they live in areas that could be impacted by tropical systems.

5:02 AM ET

Oil companies and governments have consumed large portions of their strategic petroleum reserves as tensions between the United States and Iran continue to disrupt global energy supplies. The drawdown reflects growing concerns about supply security in a conflict that has created sustained volatility across energy markets.

The situation intensified this week when ceasefire negotiations between Washington and Tehran ended without agreement on Monday night. No progress was made on reopening the Strait of Hormuz, the critical shipping lane through which roughly one-fifth of global oil passes. Following the failed talks, oil prices climbed above $91 a barrel on Tuesday after President Trump threatened to bomb Oman if the country interfered with negotiations.

The rapid consumption of reserves built up over decades suggests that alternative supply routes have not fully compensated for disruptions in the Persian Gulf region. These stockpiles typically serve as emergency buffers during short-term supply disruptions, but the prolonged nature of this conflict has forced both private companies and national governments to tap reserves at an accelerating rate.

Energy analysts note that rebuilding strategic reserves takes years and requires stable market conditions. The current rate of consumption raises questions about how long existing stockpiles can sustain supply if the conflict continues or escalates further. Any additional restrictions on passage through the Strait of Hormuz could intensify pressure on remaining reserves.

The conflict's economic ripple effects extend far beyond oil markets. Rising energy prices have intensified inflation concerns, which in turn are driving up government borrowing costs to levels not seen in decades. The yield on the 30-year U.S. Treasury bond reached 5.33 percent on Tuesday, the highest since June 2007. Similar increases appeared across developed economies, with the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield climbing above 4.74 percent and the UK's 10-year gilts rising as high as 5.176 percent.

Fiscal pressures are mounting as governments ramp up defense spending in response to the conflict. Market analysts point to multiple factors pushing bond yields higher, including both the immediate inflation threat from higher oil prices and deeper concerns about government debt levels. Investors are demanding greater compensation for holding long-dated government bonds given the range of risks now present.

The broader implications for global energy policy are significant. Countries dependent on Middle Eastern oil imports face pressure to diversify supply sources and accelerate transitions to alternative energy. Meanwhile, the depletion of reserves leaves the global economy more vulnerable to future supply shocks, with fewer buffers available to stabilize prices during crises.

Scientists at the University of Utah have developed a 3D map of a previously hidden rock glacier on Mount Timpanogos that may contain up to a billion tons of ice. The discovery represents a potential significant water source for the state, with researchers estimating the glacier holds enough water to fill approximately 600 Olympic-sized swimming pools.

The rock glacier, concealed beneath layers of rock and debris, has remained largely unnoticed despite its substantial size. Using advanced mapping technology, the research team created detailed three-dimensional imagery revealing the extent of the ice formation within the mountain. The glacier's location in Utah's Wasatch Range makes it particularly valuable as a potential water resource in a region facing ongoing water scarcity concerns.

Rock glaciers differ from traditional glaciers in that they contain a mixture of ice and rocky debris, making them less visible from the surface. This characteristic allowed the Mount Timpanogos formation to remain undetected until researchers applied specialized mapping techniques. The discovery suggests that other hidden ice reserves may exist in Utah's mountain ranges, potentially altering calculations about the state's water resources.

The research has implications beyond immediate water availability. Understanding the volume and distribution of ice in rock glaciers helps scientists model how these formations respond to climate changes and what role they might play in future water supplies. The team's mapping methodology could be applied to other mountain ranges to identify additional hidden ice reserves.

The findings arrive at a time when water resources across the western United States face increasing pressure. Population growth and changing climate patterns have heightened concerns about water availability in the region. Utah's mountains may hold reserves that could help address these challenges.

The discovery also comes amid growing recognition of how climate change affects glacial systems. Mountain regions worldwide are experiencing rapid ice loss and glacial changes. In the Himalayas, for example, glaciers have retreated significantly in recent decades, with mountains in that region losing close to one-third of their ice in just over 30 years due to global heating.

The University of Utah scientists continue to analyze the data from their 3D mapping to better understand the glacier's composition, stability, and potential as a water source. As researchers work to catalog and understand water resources across western mountain ranges, discoveries like this one provide valuable insights into hidden reserves that may become increasingly important for water management and planning.

The mapping methodology developed for this project could have broader applications in identifying similar formations elsewhere, potentially revealing additional water sources in regions where water availability remains a critical concern.

The initiative focuses on stopping AI voice cloning, a practice where artificial intelligence technology recreates a real person's voice to say anything prompted by users. Additional supporters of the campaign include Luke Evans, Jen Brister, Siobhán McSweeney, and Pearl Mackie.

The performers are asking the government to establish a legal right for every person in the UK to own their voice. This protection would prevent companies and studios from using an actor's voice without permission or proper compensation. The technology has developed rapidly, making it increasingly possible for AI systems to generate convincing speech that closely mimics specific individuals after analyzing voice samples.

The entertainment industry has struggled with AI-related concerns in recent years as the technology becomes more sophisticated. Voice actors and performers worry that AI cloning could enable studios to use their voices in new content long after initial recording sessions end, or even generate performances they never actually delivered. The potential for unauthorized use represents a significant threat to performers' ability to control their work and receive fair payment.

This campaign reflects growing concerns across the entertainment sector about how artificial intelligence affects creative professionals. Similar worries have emerged regarding digital replication of facial likenesses and full performance capture technology. Industry unions and professional organizations have begun developing contract language that addresses AI usage, though many jurisdictions still lack comprehensive legal frameworks to govern these practices.

Recent legal actions highlight the broader tension between AI development and creator rights. Twitch and Amazon currently face lawsuits over allegations that the platforms used livestreams from content creators to train AI systems without permission or appropriate compensation. This case demonstrates how widespread the unauthorized use of performer data has become across different media platforms.

The Save Our Voices Now campaign represents a coordinated effort from actors working across television, film, voice work, and theater. Organizers describe AI voice cloning as a fundamental threat to performers' ability to protect their voices and earn fair compensation for their work. The group is advocating for legislation that would require explicit consent before any AI replication of a person's voice occurs and establish clear ownership rights over vocal likenesses.

The campaign joins broader efforts within entertainment to establish meaningful boundaries around how AI technology can be used. As artificial intelligence continues to advance, the industry faces mounting pressure to develop protections that safeguard performers' rights while the technology reshapes how creative work gets produced and distributed.

5:02 AM ET

The war with Iran entered its seventh month with President Trump's path to ending the conflict remaining uncertain. Trump had originally projected a four to five week military campaign when the war began in late February, but the operation has evolved into an extended engagement centered on economic pressure and control of the Strait of Hormuz.

The war began with goals of degrading Iran's missile capabilities and destroying what remained of its nuclear program. Over six months, the focus has shifted to an open-ended fight over the Strait of Hormuz, a crucial waterway for global energy transport. U.S. officials say they have steadily eroded Iran's leverage over the strait in recent months, with the American military gaining what officials describe as the upper hand in the battle for control.

Trump's proposed exits from the conflict have ranged from military victory to a negotiated peace deal tied to reopening the Strait of Hormuz. The war now centers on an economic squeeze of Iran, while the U.S. moves increasing amounts of oil out of the strait to reduce Iranian leverage. Iran and Oman are engaged in some form of negotiations, though the status and prospects of those talks remain in flux.

The extended conflict has become a major feature of Trump's second term, affecting his standing with voters domestically and the U.S. position in the Middle East. Trump is scheduled to visit NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston, where his ambitious space priorities are also facing challenges from a divided Republican Congress.

What began as a targeted campaign to address Iran's nuclear and missile programs has transformed into a protracted struggle over regional control and energy security, with no clear timeline for resolution. The war has now lasted more than twelve times longer than the president's initial estimate.

President Trump signed an order changing the name of the lake that straddles the U.S.-Canada border.

4:26 AM ET

President Trump signed an executive order on Thursday directing federal agencies to rename Lake Ontario as Lake America on US maps, documents and databases. The move is part of escalating tensions with Canada amid an ongoing trade war between the countries.

Canada's prime minister and several US governors, including New York's Tony Evers, have rejected the rename. Trump has no authority to change the name on Canada's portion of the lake or compel Canada, international bodies, or mapmakers worldwide to adopt the new name.

Democrats have established a task force to plan responses to possible presidential interference in the upcoming midterm elections.

5:00 AM ET

As the midterm elections approach, Democrats have formed a task force to prepare for various scenarios involving potential interference by President Trump. Party officials say they want to avoid what they view as a failure of imagination that preceded the January 6 attack on the Capitol.

The task force is developing response plans for contingencies related to election administration and vote counting. Crown Prince Haakon to become Norway's new king

The only son of King Harald V will assume the largely ceremonial and symbolic role of monarch.

5:31 AM ET

King Harald V of Norway died on Friday, August 28, at Oslo University Hospital at age 89. He was Europe's oldest reigning monarch. His son, Crown Prince Haakon, has succeeded him as King Haakon VIII.

The Norwegian monarchy is a largely ceremonial and symbolic role. Haakon's accession marks a generational transition for the Scandinavian royal house following his father's lengthy reign.

Families of Indian pilgrims await news after Nepal floods

Two Indian women lost contact while traveling to Tibet as deadly flash floods struck the border region.

5:31 AM ET

The families of Indian pilgrims are waiting for news after losing contact with loved ones during devastating flash floods near the Nepal-Tibet border. The flooding and landslides struck on Wednesday, killing hundreds and leaving many missing.

Among those unaccounted for are two Indian women. One was in the region preparing for what her family described as a dream tour to Tibet when communication ceased. Their families have not heard from them since the disaster began.

Actors call for legal protections against AI voice cloning

Performers including Matt Lucas and Hugh Bonneville are asking the UK government to create legal rights over voice replication technology.

7:00 AM ET

A group of UK performers, including Matt Lucas, Hugh Bonneville, and Nicola Coughlan, is calling on the government to establish legal protections for voices. They want every person in the UK to have a legal right to own their voice.

The actors are seeking new legislation that would give individuals control over how their voices are used. This comes as AI technology has made it increasingly simple to replicate voices using relatively small audio samples, raising concerns among performers about unauthorized use of their work.
