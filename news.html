Stock markets fell across Europe and Asia on Monday following a sharp sell-off in US technology stocks and renewed military conflict in the Middle East. Oil prices surged nearly 5 percent to $97.60 a barrel after Iran and Israel exchanged strikes, raising concerns about potential disruptions to global energy supplies.

Asian markets experienced the sharpest declines. South Korea's Kospi index fell nearly 9 percent at one point, forcing trading to be briefly suspended. The sell-off was led by major chip makers, with Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix dropping 9 percent and 6 percent respectively. Japan's Nikkei 225 index fell 3 percent, while Hong Kong's Hang Seng dropped 1.5 percent.

In Europe, the pan-European Stoxx 600 index fell almost 0.9 percent. Chip firms including BE Semiconductor Industries and ASML fell 4.5 percent and 3.2 percent respectively. Germany's Aixtron dropped nearly 6 percent, and Finland's Nokia fell 5 percent. London's FTSE 100 opened down 0.4 percent, with airline and jet engine stocks among the biggest fallers. Oil company shares including BP and Shell rose on the day.

The technology sell-off followed steep declines late last week in the United States. The tech-heavy Nasdaq index lost nearly 5 percent of its value, while the S&P 500 dropped 2 percent on a weekly basis, ending nine weeks of gains.

Investors are reassessing valuations in artificial intelligence stocks amid concerns about how technology giants will fund their expansion plans. According to Susannah Streeter, chief investment strategist at Wealth Club, "Fears of higher interest rates come just as tech giants are seeking fresh funding to help finance eye-watering capital expenditure plans."

Charu Chanana, chief investment strategist at Saxo investment bank, said the market was becoming more selective. "Investors now want clearer proof of earnings delivery, monetisation, capex discipline and funding returns. This looks more like a positioning reset than a regime break. The AI story is not over, but easy AI enthusiasm may be."

The rise in oil prices followed the first direct exchange of strikes between Iran and Israel since a ceasefire paused fighting in April. Traders worry that continued clashes could prevent reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical shipping channel through which roughly one-fifth of the world's oil and gas supply normally passes.

The combination of geopolitical tensions and technology sector weakness created significant headwinds for global investors. US stock futures were expected to open lower as the sell-off extended from Asian and European markets into American trading, with investors weighing economic growth prospects against rising inflation concerns.

The strikes came in response to earlier Israeli airstrikes on the Beirut area in Lebanon. Israeli forces worked to intercept the incoming Iranian missiles, and explosions were heard across Jerusalem and other areas, with some damage reported in West Bank settlements but no injuries.

Iranian officials claimed that Israel would not have launched attacks without US approval and coordination, rejecting suggestions that Netanyahu had defied Trump's instructions. An international relations specialist in Tehran pointed to videos he said showed the US involvement in the Israeli strikes, noting that Israel lacks the capability to launch certain long-range cruise missiles from the eastern Mediterranean.

The White House did not respond to questions about whether the Israeli strikes were coordinated with the US.

The resumption of hostilities also drew involvement from Yemen's Houthi rebels, who announced a missile attack on Israel on Monday, their first since early April. The group declared a complete ban on Israeli maritime navigation in the Red Sea, threatening disruption to a critical trade route.

The breakdown of the two-month truce creates complications for diplomatic efforts. Trump has claimed peace talks could produce a deal within days, but the escalation may undermine negotiations. A new study has found that people working remotely experience higher levels of social isolation, anxiety, and sadness compared to those not in remote jobs. The research challenges the assumption that remote work benefits employees without cost, revealing mental health impacts that have emerged as remote work became widespread.

The study examined mental health outcomes across different work arrangements and found measurable differences in psychological wellbeing between remote and in-person workers. Researchers noted that the social disconnection inherent in remote work appears to contribute to negative mental health outcomes, even as many workers report preferring the flexibility and convenience of working from home.

Despite the findings, researchers cautioned against using the study to justify mandatory return-to-office policies. They emphasized that forcing all employees back to physical workplaces is not the appropriate response to the mental health challenges identified in the research. The study suggests a more nuanced approach is needed to address the wellbeing concerns while preserving the benefits that remote work offers to many employees.

The research adds to a growing body of evidence examining the long-term effects of remote work on employee health and productivity. As remote and hybrid work arrangements have become standard practice for millions of American workers since the pandemic, understanding their full impact on mental health has become increasingly important for employers and policymakers.

The findings suggest that organizations may need to develop new strategies to support the mental health of remote workers, potentially through structured social connection opportunities or hybrid models that balance flexibility with in-person interaction. The study underscores that while remote work has transformed the American workplace, its effects on worker wellbeing require ongoing attention and intervention.

The challenge lies in finding solutions that do not simply reverse the shift toward remote work. Many employees have built their lives around the flexibility that working from home provides, from managing childcare responsibilities to reducing commute times. A blanket return-to-office mandate could undermine these advantages while potentially failing to address the underlying causes of isolation and anxiety.

Experts suggest that organizations should consider tailored approaches based on their workforce's needs. Some companies might benefit from hybrid schedules that require employees to be in the office a few days per week while maintaining remote work options. Others could invest in virtual team-building activities, mentorship programs, or mental health resources specifically designed for remote workers.

The findings also highlight the importance of managers being attuned to the mental health challenges their remote team members face. Regular check-ins, opportunities for informal interaction, and clear pathways to mental health support could help mitigate some of the negative effects identified in the research.

As organizations continue to navigate the post-pandemic workplace, this study provides important guidance for balancing employee preferences with their wellbeing needs.

Atlantic current system shows signs of nearing climate tipping point

A new analysis of Atlantic Ocean data suggests that a major ocean circulation system is weakening significantly and may be approaching a critical threshold. The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, which includes the Gulf Stream, plays a central role in global climate by transporting warm water northward and cold water southward. 4:35 AM ET

A new analysis of Atlantic Ocean data suggests that a major ocean circulation system is weakening significantly and may be approaching a critical threshold. The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, which includes the Gulf Stream, plays a central role in global climate by transporting warm water northward and cold water southward.

Researchers have identified an unusual cold region in the North Atlantic, known as the Atlantic cold blob, as evidence of the circulation's decline. By combining real-world ocean observations with climate models, scientists determined that the AMOC is likely to slow by between 42 and 58 percent by 2100, a level almost certain to trigger collapse.

This finding represents a significant shift in scientific understanding. Climate models previously produced widely varying predictions about the AMOC's future, ranging from no further slowdown to a 65 percent deceleration. The new research dramatically reduced this uncertainty by identifying which models most accurately reflect actual ocean conditions, particularly surface salinity in the South Atlantic.

The implications are severe. A collapse of the AMOC would shift tropical rainfall patterns that millions of people depend on for agriculture, bring extreme cold winters and summer droughts to Western Europe, and raise sea levels around the Atlantic by 50 to 100 centimeters. The system would also disrupt marine ecosystems and weather patterns across multiple continents.

Scientists have expressed serious concern about these prospects. Dr. Stefan Rahmstorf from the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research stated that "the pessimistic models, which show a strong weakening of the AMOC by 2100, are unfortunately the realistic ones." He added that the collapse tipping point could potentially occur in the middle of this century.

The AMOC is weakening due to rapid air temperature increases in the Arctic. Warmer ocean water is less dense and therefore sinks more slowly into the depths. This slowing allows more rainfall to accumulate in surface waters, further reducing their density and creating a feedback loop that slows the circulation further. Additionally, freshwater from melting ice sheets adds to this problem.

Scientists have raised concerns about preparedness for potential consequences. The system has collapsed before in Earth's history, most notably during the Younger Dryas period roughly 12,000 years ago, when North Atlantic temperatures dropped sharply within decades.

Rahmstorf noted that models do not yet account for additional meltwater from the Greenland ice cap, meaning actual conditions could be even worse than current projections. He emphasized that avoiding an AMOC collapse "must be done at all costs," given the massive potential impacts on global climate and human societies.

Republicans are preparing to advance two major legislative priorities this week as the House schedules a vote on a $69.5 billion budget reconciliation package for immigration enforcement and Congress faces a deadline to extend warrantless surveillance powers under the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act.

The budget reconciliation bill would provide funding for Immigration and Customs Enforcement and Border Patrol operations. Republicans are using the budget reconciliation process, which allows legislation to pass with a simple majority in the Senate and avoid a filibuster. The House is expected to take up the measure in the coming days.

Congress is also working to extend government surveillance powers under FISA before current authorization expires. The law allows intelligence agencies to conduct warrantless surveillance of foreign targets, though it has drawn criticism from privacy advocates and some lawmakers who argue it can sweep up communications of American citizens. Republicans have made the extension a priority, though the timeline for action remains tight.

The dual agenda comes as Republicans control both chambers of Congress and are working to advance legislative items ahead of the August recess. With only weeks remaining before the month-long break, the party faces pressure to deliver on key policy goals before attention shifts to midterm election campaigns.

The reconciliation bill represents one of potentially three party-line packages Republicans hope to pass this year. Party leaders are already discussing a third reconciliation measure that would need to clear both chambers before the November midterm elections. The tight legislative calendar and narrow margins in both the House and Senate mean Republicans will need to maintain near-unanimous support within their caucus to advance their agenda.

Family drama Dear You dominated the Chinese box office for another weekend.

2:35 AM ET
