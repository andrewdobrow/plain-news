The United States launched military strikes against Iran on Tuesday after a US Army Apache helicopter went down near the Strait of Hormuz. President Donald Trump blamed Iranian forces for downing the aircraft and said the country "must, of necessity, respond to this attack."

US Central Command confirmed that strikes began at 10pm UK time, targeting radar and air defense facilities along the Strait of Hormuz. The strikes mark a significant escalation that endangers a ceasefire agreement announced by the two countries in April.

The helicopter crashed at approximately 3:30am local time off the coast of Oman during a patrol operation. A US official told the Associated Press that the helicopter collided with an Iranian drone, though it remained unclear whether the collision was intentional. Trump initially stated the helicopter had been "shot down," though official statements said the incident was under investigation.

Both crew members were rescued in an unprecedented operation using an unmanned drone boat. The vessel, a 24-foot craft called a Corsair, located the aviators after they spent about two hours in the water. US Central Command spokesperson Captain Tim Hawkins called it the first known drone rescue at sea by the US military.

However, Trump later downplayed the incident in an interview with the Wall Street Journal, calling it "wasn't a big deal" and noting that "the pilot is fine."

Central Command described the military response as "a proportional response to unjustified Iranian aggression," signaling that officials were not seeking a return to full-scale war. The Strait of Hormuz serves as a critical waterway for global energy supplies, with roughly one-fifth of the world's petroleum passing through the narrow passage between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula.

The helicopter incident occurred as the region remained unstable following an exchange of fire between Iran and Israel on Monday, which further strained the ceasefire. Iranian state television reported that Israeli attacks killed at least two members of the country's air defense units.

The conflict that began on February 28 has shaken the global economy, raised energy prices worldwide, and made basic goods including food more expensive. Officials have struggled to convert the April ceasefire into a permanent agreement, particularly as Israel intensifies military operations in Lebanon against the Iranian-backed Hezbollah militia.

Iranian foreign minister Abbas Araghchi responded to the strikes on social media, saying Iran "will leave no attack or threat unanswered. Leave our region if you want to be safe." Iran's chief negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf stated: "We prefer the language of diplomacy, but we speak other languages far more fluently."

Hours before the strikes, Trump had expressed renewed optimism about negotiations, saying there was "a good chance" of reaching a deal within "two or three days."

The House approved legislation designed to accelerate contract negotiations for newly unionized workers, with 20 Republicans breaking ranks to support the Democratic-led measure. The bill would allow government intervention if employers and unions fail to reach an agreement within 90 days of workers voting to unionize.

Speaker Mike Johnson opposed the measure and urged House Republicans to vote against it, but the defections provided enough support to pass the bill. The vote demonstrates a continuing pattern of House Republicans bypassing their own leadership to back Democratic legislation.

Under existing law, newly unionized workers often wait months or years to secure their first contract as employers and unions negotiate terms. The legislation aims to shorten this timeline by establishing a 90-day deadline. If negotiations stall beyond that point, federal mediators can intervene to help resolve disputes.

Supporters of the bill argue it will prevent employers from deliberately prolonging negotiations as a tactic to discourage union organizing. Union leaders have long maintained that extended delays in securing first contracts undermine organizing efforts and leave workers without promised benefits they voted to obtain.

The 20 Republicans who voted for the measure represent a significant departure from traditional party opposition to pro-union legislation. Their support reflects growing labor activism in their districts and pressure from constituents who have recently organized their workplaces. The bill now advances to the Senate, where its prospects remain uncertain given the chamber's political composition.

The House vote provides a victory for labor organizations that have pushed for stronger protections for newly unionized workers. The passage comes during a period of increased union organizing across multiple industries, including retail, technology, and hospitality sectors.

This outcome continues a recent trend where House Republicans have crossed party lines to support Democratic priorities despite leadership opposition. With the chamber narrowly divided and Republicans holding a slim majority, defections from either party can significantly impact legislative outcomes. The ability of Democrats to secure Republican votes on labor issues suggests potential vulnerability within the GOP caucus on issues where constituents have expressed strong interest in union protection.

Union leaders view the bill as an important step toward protecting workers' right to organize and bargain collectively. They argue that without mechanisms to accelerate contract negotiations, employers can use delay tactics to discourage future unionization efforts, effectively undermining the organizing victories workers have already achieved through their votes.

The bill's journey to the Senate will test whether the bipartisan support demonstrated in the House exists in the upper chamber, where procedural rules and a different political calculation may affect its prospects for passage.

7:53 PM ET

Apollo Global Management and Blackstone have raised $35 billion in a new financing arrangement with chipmaker Broadcom to fund artificial intelligence infrastructure for Anthropic, the AI startup backed by Google. The deal represents one of the largest private financing packages assembled for AI hardware deployment.

Broadcom will help structure and manage chip supply arrangements under the financing platform, which is designed to support Anthropic's expansion of computing capacity. Google has provided financial guarantees underpinning portions of the agreement, according to reports. The arrangement allows Anthropic to scale its AI operations without committing the full capital upfront for expensive semiconductor orders and data center infrastructure.

The financing structure addresses a growing challenge in the AI industry: the massive upfront costs required to secure advanced chips and build the computing infrastructure needed to train and run large language models. Companies like Anthropic and OpenAI require billions of dollars in specialized hardware, primarily graphics processing units and custom AI accelerators, to remain competitive. Traditional venture capital and corporate funding often prove insufficient for these capital requirements.

Apollo and Blackstone, two of the world's largest alternative asset managers, have increasingly moved into technology infrastructure investments as AI development accelerates. The firms see opportunities to provide capital for physical assets like chips and servers that have predictable demand from well-funded AI companies. Broadcom, which designs custom chips for major technology companies, gains a financing mechanism that can help its AI customers afford larger orders.

The arrangement comes as competition intensifies among AI labs to secure computing resources. Access to cutting-edge chips has become a strategic bottleneck, with manufacturing capacity constrained and prices elevated. By securing committed financing, Anthropic aims to lock in supply and expand its infrastructure as it competes with rivals including OpenAI and technology giants developing their own AI systems. The deal also reflects growing confidence among financial institutions that AI infrastructure represents a sound investment as the technology moves from research to commercial deployment across industries.

7:02 PM ET

NASA announced the crew for its Artemis III mission on Tuesday, revealing the next phase of the agency's efforts to return humans to the Moon. The space agency selected four astronauts who will launch in 2027 for a two-week test flight in low Earth orbit, paving the way for a planned crewed lunar landing in 2028.

The crew includes Randy Bresnik of NASA as mission commander, Luca Parmitano of the European Space Agency as pilot, and NASA mission specialists Frank Rubio and Andre Douglas. NASA also named Bob Hines as a support crew member. Bresnik is a Marine colonel and former commander of the International Space Station with more than 7,000 hours of flying time. Parmitano is a fighter pilot in the Italian air force who previously experienced a near-fatal incident during a 2013 spacewalk when his helmet filled with liquid. Rubio, an Army Black Hawk helicopter pilot and flight surgeon, holds the record for the longest single spaceflight duration by a NASA astronaut at 371 days. Douglas, a systems engineer and Coast Guard reserve officer, has no previous spaceflight experience but trained as a backup crew member for Artemis II.

Unlike the Artemis II mission that looped around the Moon, the Artemis III crew will remain in low Earth orbit to test critical systems. They will practice docking procedures and evaluate life support systems for two competing lunar landers from Blue Origin and SpaceX ahead of the actual Moon landing.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman described the announcement as marking the creation of "Earth's first starfleet," emphasizing the international scope of the program. He highlighted the numerous spacecraft that will operate simultaneously, including vehicles from NASA, Russia, China, Blue Origin, and SpaceX.

The announcement also addressed recent setbacks facing the program. Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket, designed to carry its lunar lander, was destroyed in a launchpad explosion on May 28. John Couluris, Blue Origin's senior vice-president of lunar permanence, acknowledged the incident but expressed confidence that the company would rebuild and meet the 2027 launch timeline.

Jeremy Parsons, NASA's acting deputy associate administrator for the Moon to Mars program, confirmed NASA's commitment to supporting its partners through technical challenges. "While we recognize there are questions about how Blue Origin's recent anomaly impacts our plans, setbacks are a learning opportunity," he said.

The Artemis program represents an expansion of lunar exploration through international collaboration. The selection of Parmitano marks the first time a European astronaut will participate in a NASA Artemis mission, demonstrating how multiple nations are contributing to humanity's return to the Moon more than 50 years after Apollo 17 in 1972.

Serena Williams made a winning professional tennis comeback at age 44, partnering with Canadian teenager Victoria Mboko to defeat third seeds Nicole Melichar-Martinez and Erin Routliffe 7-6 (7-2), 6-2 in the first round of doubles at Queen's Club.

The victory marked Williams' return to competition after 1,375 days away from the sport. She had stepped back from professional tennis in 2022, having won 23 Grand Slam singles titles throughout her career.

The first set proved competitive, with the unseeded pair needing a tiebreak to secure the opening set. They controlled the tiebreak decisively before dominating the second set to claim the match in straight sets. The win came against seeded opponents, demonstrating that Williams retains the competitive skills that defined her legendary career.

Williams chose to return through doubles rather than singles, allowing her to ease back into competitive tennis with a partner. When assessing her performance after the match, she gave herself a modest grade, acknowledging room for improvement as she rebuilds her competitive fitness.

In discussing her comeback motivation, Williams emphasized that winning is no longer her primary concern. "I don't need to win. I've won more than most people have in their whole lives, so for me it's not important," she said. She explained that the return is largely about her children experiencing her as a competitor. She gave birth to a daughter in 2023 and has an eight-year-old daughter, Olympia.

Williams selected Mboko, the world number 9 in singles, as her partner after being impressed by the teenager's attitude and drive. "I remember seeing her play in Montreal. I was impressed with her attitude, and what I liked the most about her was that the next time she played she still kept winning," Williams said. She noted that Mboko "reminded me a lot of myself."

For Mboko, the partnership holds special significance. Growing up, Williams had been her idol. "It was really hard not to like her because she won every tournament she played," Mboko said. She added that meeting Williams in person revealed her to be "really nice and very personable, very relatable. And I think she's really funny."

Williams has committed to playing doubles in Berlin following Queen's but said her future in singles remains uncertain. When asked about returning to singles competition, she stated: "I can't say yeah, I can't say no. Right now, no." She suggested she would need additional training to compete in singles and emphasized that her current focus is on enjoying the experience.

The Queen's Club tournament serves as a grass-court warmup event before Wimbledon. Williams and Mboko will compete in the second round of the doubles draw.
