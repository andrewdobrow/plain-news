The U.S. Treasury Department moved to cut off cryptocurrency exchange MEXC from the American financial system over allegations the platform processed $16 billion in illicit transactions linked to Iran, North Korea and Russia. The action targets MEXC Global for alleged sanctions violations.

The Treasury decision blocks MEXC from accessing U.S. financial infrastructure and conducting transactions with American counterparties. The enforcement measure represents one of the largest actions against a cryptocurrency exchange based on alleged illicit transaction volume.

The alleged $16 billion in flows involve transactions connected to sanctioned countries including Iran, North Korea and Russia. The Treasury did not specify the time period over which the transactions allegedly occurred or detail the nature of the illicit activity.

In response to the Treasury action, digital asset company NatGold Digital announced it would immediately delist its NATG token from MEXC. The move reflects the immediate market consequences for platforms cut off from the U.S. financial system.

AI tools are enabling automated hacking at scale, upending the cybersecurity industry

Open-weight AI models have advanced to the point where they can automatically identi...

11:00 AM ET

Artificial intelligence systems are now enabling attackers to automate the entire hacking process in real time, fundamentally disrupting the cybersecurity industry built over the past two decades, according to Alex Stamos, chief security officer at Cognition and a longtime cybersecurity expert. Open-weight AI models have advanced to the point where they can automatically identify software vulnerabilities, write exploits, and break into targets simultaneously, collapsing what was traditionally a multi-step process requiring teams of skilled hackers.

Stamos described a wave of breaches in recent weeks affecting major technology companies, including a chain of attacks that began with a compromise of Fireworks, an AI services provider. Attackers stole API keys from Fireworks that enabled them to breach Cursor, a code editor, where they pushed a malicious plugin to 5,000 additional companies, creating what Stamos called a "permanent continuous breach" across the tech industry. He also cited the Binance hack, which he characterized as the largest heist in history, and attacks on roughly 200 companies using ARTEX, a Chinese open-source hacking tool that anyone can download.

The shift eliminates barriers that previously limited cyberattacks. Where breaking into systems once required rare technical expertise and large teams, now attackers can use locally run AI models on consumer hardware to conduct sophisticated operations. Stamos said he has been running an AI model on an Apple Mac Studio that can reverse engineer software patches and write exploit code, capabilities that previously belonged only to elite hackers. Both criminal groups and state actors in countries including Russia, China, and North Korea are deploying these AI-powered tools.

Stamos compared the current moment to the 1990s, when the internet was new and security practices were still being developed, calling it a return to "Wild West days." The multibillion-dollar cybersecurity industry that emerged in the 2000s and 2010s was built on assumptions about skill hierarchies and operational structures that AI has now rendered obsolete. While Stamos said he is not pessimistic long-term, he believes the industry faces a "dark age" as it struggles to adapt to automated, AI-enabled attacks. }

Federal judge blocks Postal Service restrictions on mail-in ballot delivery

A federal judge has blocked a proposed restriction on mail-in voting across the United States, preventing the Postal Service from implementing a plan that would have denied ballots to voters in states refusing to turn over their voter rolls to the federal government. Judge Emmet Sullivan of the US District Court for the District of Columbia ruled against the restriction, marki...

7:28 PM ET

A federal judge has blocked a proposed restriction on mail-in voting across the United States, preventing the Postal Service from implementing a plan that would have denied ballots to voters in states refusing to turn over their voter rolls to the federal government.

Judge Emmet Sullivan of the US District Court for the District of Columbia ruled against the restriction, marking the second time that a plan to limit mail ballots across the country has faced a court setback.

The blocked restriction stemmed from an order targeting mail-in voting access. Civil rights groups, including the NAACP, challenged the proposed changes in court, arguing that the Postal Service directive would create barriers to voting. Multiple organizations joined the legal challenge, contending that the restrictions would make it harder for Americans to cast ballots by mail, particularly affecting communities that depend heavily on mail-in voting.

Mail-in voting has become a significant method of casting ballots in recent election cycles, with millions of Americans relying on the option. Voting rights advocates had opposed the proposed changes, saying they would create unnecessary obstacles for voters without good cause.

The judge's order keeps current mail ballot handling procedures in place while the case continues through the courts. The ruling ensures that existing practices for delivering mail ballots remain unchanged for now, preventing the implementation of the proposed restrictions.

The decision represents a significant setback for efforts to limit mail-in voting access. Judge Sullivan found sufficient grounds to block the restriction, siding with those who argued the changes would harm voting access.

This case is part of a broader legal battle over voting access and election administration that has intensified in recent years. As states prepare for upcoming elections, courts continue to weigh competing claims about voting methods and election security. The block demonstrates ongoing judicial skepticism toward restrictions on mail-in voting, with multiple courts now rejecting similar proposals.

The outcome comes amid heightened focus on election procedures and voting access. Legal challenges to voting restrictions have become common in the current political environment, with courts frequently called upon to determine the constitutionality of various election administration policies.

The Postal Service has not yet announced whether it will appeal the ruling. If appealed, the case could move to higher courts, potentially reaching the appellate level. The decision ensures that the current mail ballot system continues operating under existing rules while legal proceedings advance.

The ruling highlights how courts are actively shaping election policy through litigation. As voting access remains a contested issue, this decision will likely influence other ongoing legal battles over mail-in voting procedures and voter eligibility requirements across the country.

Tech & Science

Scientists find two massive tears in Arabian tectonic plate beneath Iran

The ruptures offer rare insight into how oceans close and continents collide.

2:04 PM ET

Scientists have discovered two giant tears in the Arabian Plate beneath southeastern Iran, caused by contrasting rock densities within the plate. One section consists of dense oceanic basalt being pushed underground, while another section is lighter continental rock that resists subduction and forms mountains. The mismatch is creating the ruptures, according to research published in Nature Geoscience.

Researchers used 69 seismic monitoring stations to detect the tears: one horizontal rupture where the Zagros collision zone meets the Makran subduction zone, and a smaller vertical tear near the Makran Trench. The tears have been progressing for millions of years and may eventually merge, providing geologists a rare view of the transition from oceanic subduction to continental collision.
