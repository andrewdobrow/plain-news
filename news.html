10:08 AM ET

Consumer prices rose 4.2 percent in the year through May, marking the highest inflation rate in three years, according to government data released today. The Consumer Price Index increase reflects mounting cost pressures across the economy as the US-Israel war in Iran continues to affect global supply chains and energy markets.

The report arrives at a critical moment for monetary policy, coming just before Kevin M. Warsh's first meeting as chair of the Federal Reserve. The elevated inflation figure complicates the central bank's decision-making on interest rates, making the prospect of a rate cut in the near term increasingly unlikely. Fed officials have repeatedly stated that they need to see sustained progress on inflation before considering any easing of monetary policy.

Airfares experienced the steepest increase among consumer categories tracked, jumping 27 percent compared to a year earlier. The sharp rise in airline ticket prices contributed significantly to the overall inflation figure and reflects both strong travel demand and higher operational costs for carriers. The spike in transportation costs has put additional strain on household budgets already stretched by elevated prices across multiple categories.

The inflation data triggered immediate reactions in financial markets. Stock indexes fell as investors recalibrated expectations for interest rate policy, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropping 500 points. The report underscores the ongoing challenge facing policymakers as they attempt to balance economic growth against price stability in an environment shaped by geopolitical tensions and supply disruptions.

The reading represents a setback for efforts to bring inflation back to the Federal Reserve's 2 percent target. With the new Fed chair taking the helm amid these pressures, the central bank faces difficult choices about the appropriate path for monetary policy in the months ahead. Economists will be closely watching whether the elevated inflation proves temporary or signals a more persistent trend that could require continued restrictive policy.

The escalation marks a critical moment in U.S.-Iran tensions as high-level diplomatic efforts get underway in Islamabad. Vice President JD Vance is leading the American delegation to negotiations scheduled for Saturday, accompanied by Trump's special envoy Steve Witkoff and his son-in-law Jared Kushner. The Iranian delegation, headed by parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf and foreign minister Abbas Araghchi, arrived in Pakistan on Friday.

Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif described the upcoming talks as "make or break" for achieving a permanent ceasefire. Iran set preconditions for the negotiations, including a ceasefire in Lebanon and the release of previously frozen Iranian assets. If these measures are accepted, talks with the United States are expected to begin Saturday afternoon.

When asked what a successful agreement would look like, Trump said the primary goal was preventing Iran from developing nuclear weapons. "No nuclear weapon, that's 99% of it," he told reporters. On the separate issue of the Strait of Hormuz, which Iran closed following U.S.-Israeli strikes, Trump predicted the waterway would "open up automatically" and said the U.S. would have it "open fairly soon," though other nations depend on it more heavily than America does.

Vance said he was "looking forward to negotiations" and expected them to be positive, though he cautioned Iran not to "play" the United States. Trump told his vice president he had wished him luck ahead of the talks and expressed confidence in the American military's capabilities if negotiations falter.

The conflict has created significant economic ripple effects globally. World Bank President Ajay Banga warned that even a successful ceasefire would reduce global growth by 0.3 to 0.4 percentage points, with inflation rising 200 to 300 basis points. A failed ceasefire could prove far worse, potentially lowering growth by 1 percentage point and increasing inflation by up to 0.9 percentage points. Developing nations face even starker prospects, with inflation potentially reaching 6.7 percent under worst-case scenarios.

Former U.S. envoys who previously dealt with Iran suggested that the recent U.S.-Israeli military operations and Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz have strengthened Tehran's negotiating position regarding its nuclear program. Iran's parliament speaker stated the regime was prepared to reach a deal if the U.S. offered genuine agreement and respected Iranian rights.

The talks represent the highest-level U.S.-Iran meeting since 1979, with observers describing Vance's assignment as potentially consequential but fraught with difficult choices between making significant concessions to preserve the ceasefire or risking a return to conflict.

Researchers have identified a massive fan-shaped geological structure hidden under Antarctica's ice sheet, according to a study published in Nature. The formation spans an area comparable to a continent and was detected through geophysical surveys.

The structure was created through a process called rotational extension, which shaped the subglacial basin province in East Antarctica. Scientists used advanced surveying techniques to map the geological feature through thousands of feet of ice, revealing a formation that has remained concealed from direct observation.

This discovery enhances our understanding of Antarctica's geological history and the forces that shaped the continent over millions of years. The structure lies beneath one of Earth's most remote and difficult to access regions, making its detection a significant achievement in polar research.

Antarctica's ice sheet, which reaches average thicknesses of about 1.2 miles in some areas, has long hidden the continent's underlying geology from direct study. Scientists have increasingly relied on remote sensing and geophysical methods to investigate what lies beneath the ice. These tools help researchers understand both Antarctica's past and how its geology might influence ice sheet behavior in the future.

The fan-shaped basin province represents a major addition to the known geological features of East Antarctica, which remains one of the least explored regions of the continent. Understanding the subglacial geology helps scientists model how the ice sheet has evolved across geological time periods and how it might respond to changing climate conditions.

The research contributes to ongoing efforts to map Antarctica's hidden landscape. As researchers continue to develop better detection methods, they gain clearer pictures of the continent's subsurface structure. This knowledge is crucial for understanding the complex interactions between geological features and the massive ice sheets that cover them.

The basin province discovered in this research demonstrates the value of continued investment in polar science and advanced detection technology. With thousands of meters of ice covering most of Antarctica, geophysical surveys remain essential tools for studying the continent's structure. These techniques allow scientists to work around the extreme environmental challenges that prevent traditional drilling and direct observation in most regions.

The discovery also highlights how much remains unknown about Earth's polar regions. Despite decades of research, major geological formations continue to be found beneath Antarctica's ice. Each new discovery adds pieces to the puzzle of how Antarctica formed and evolved, and how its geology continues to influence the behavior of its ice sheets today.

As climate change affects polar regions globally, understanding Antarctica's geological structure becomes increasingly important for predicting how the continent's ice sheets might respond to warming conditions in the coming decades.

Everton ordered to pay Burnley £35 million over financial rule breaches

The ruling represents the largest financial penalty ever imposed on a Premier League club. The decision came from a Premier League independent disciplinary commission made up of the same three-...

10:17 AM ET

Everton have been ordered to pay Burnley nearly £40 million in compensation following a landmark legal case over breaches of the Premier League's Profitability and Sustainability Rules. The ruling represents the largest financial penalty ever imposed on a Premier League club.

The decision came from a Premier League independent disciplinary commission made up of the same three-person panel that deducted Everton 10 points for the same breach in November 2023. The compensation comprises £26 million in base damages, with £9.1 million in interest plus accrued interest added on top.

Burnley brought the legal action after being relegated to the Championship at the end of the 2021-22 season. The club argued that had Everton received their points deduction earlier, in 2021-22 rather than 2023, Burnley would have remained in the Premier League and Everton would have gone down instead. The ruling was based on a "loss of chance" principle, meaning Burnley lost the opportunity to stay up due to Everton's rule breach.

Under Premier League rules, clubs can seek compensation from others found guilty of breaking regulations. The verdict has significant implications for English football, as it now opens the door for other clubs to pursue similar legal action. Several Premier League clubs have reportedly sought legal advice in case Manchester City is found guilty in their long-running case, with the possibility that clubs could sue for missing out on titles or European qualification.

Everton responded to the ruling with a statement saying they were "surprised and angered" by a verdict they believe is "fundamentally flawed in both law and fact". The club has appealed the commission's decision. They argue they believed they were PSR compliant when Burnley were relegated in May 2022 and could have taken action such as selling players had they known otherwise. Everton also point out they have already suffered financial consequences, including a six-point deduction and around £6 million in lost revenue from being demoted in the 2022-23 season.

The Premier League has assured Everton that the compensation sum will not count towards this year's PSR calculations. The club is now owned by The Friedkin Group following Farhad Moshiri's sale in December 2024. Moshiri received only £25 million from the sale, and it remains unclear whether the new ownership will pursue him for the damages owed to Burnley.

Burnley chair Alan Pace said the club's action was always about making football fair. "Clubs that comply with the rules deserve to compete on a level playing field," he said. The independent commission confirmed that a rule was broken and a competitive advantage was improperly gained.

Trump requests short-term extension of foreign surveillance law amid intelligence director dispute

President Trump asked Congress to approve a temporary extension of Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, seeking to buy time while addressing a brewing conflict with Democrats over intelligence community leadership. Trump announced the request through a post on his TruthSocial platform. Section 702 permits U.S. intelligence agencies to collect communication...

A few minutes ago

President Trump asked Congress to approve a temporary extension of Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, seeking to buy time while addressing a brewing conflict with Democrats over intelligence community leadership. Trump announced the request through a post on his TruthSocial platform.

Section 702 permits U.S. intelligence agencies to collect communications from foreign targets operating outside the United States without obtaining a warrant. The provision has generated ongoing debate regarding privacy rights and the scope of government surveillance authority. Senate Democrats have indicated they will block renewal of the law unless Trump replaces his acting director of national intelligence, Bill Pulte, whose appointment has drawn bipartisan criticism.

The short-term extension would allow the administration to nominate and secure confirmation for a permanent intelligence director while preserving the surveillance authority. Trump signaled he plans to nominate someone to replace Pulte, though he has not disclosed a timeline or identified specific candidates.

Pulte's appointment has sparked widespread concern in Congress. Lawmakers on both sides of the aisle have raised alarm about his complete lack of national security experience and worry that he could use the office's surveillance powers to target Trump's political opponents. The controversy has created a significant obstacle to renewing the surveillance law, which is set to expire at the end of the week.

Trump met with House Speaker Mike Johnson to discuss Pulte's elevation to the position before announcing his request for the extension. The president acknowledged the backlash against Pulte's appointment but stated the acting director would "execute the immediate downsizing" of the intelligence community during his temporary role. Trump indicated he would search for a permanent director "with experience in National Security."

The Senate passed a 10-day extension of Section 702, providing a short-term bridge while the administration and Congress attempt to resolve the standoff. This extension prevents a complete lapse in foreign surveillance authority while negotiations continue over leadership of the intelligence community.

The dispute reflects broader tensions within the Trump administration regarding the balance between national security operations and political considerations. Democrats have made clear that renewing the surveillance law depends on resolving the question of who leads the intelligence community. Republicans have generally supported maintaining the surveillance authority, though concerns about Pulte's qualifications have created unusual friction within the party.

The temporary extension offers a window for the administration to nominate a more widely acceptable permanent director while keeping surveillance operations functioning. Without such an extension, the law would have expired, forcing Congress to completely restart negotiations over the surveillance program's future.
