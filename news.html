Data Centers Ordered to Use Backup Power as Heat Wave Strains US Electric Grid

The move aims to prevent blackouts as extreme heat drives up air conditioning demand while data centers consume massive amounts of electricity. Grid managers issued emergency alerts as...

11:24 AM ET

The Trump administration directed grid operators to require data centers to use backup power as triple-digit temperatures across much of the United States pushed the electric grid to its limits. The move aims to prevent blackouts as extreme heat drives up air conditioning demand while data centers consume massive amounts of electricity.

Grid managers issued emergency alerts as the heat wave stretched power systems to capacity. Data centers, which typically maintain backup generators for their own use during outages, were told to activate this reserve power to reduce strain on the main grid. The directive represents an unusual step to tap into privately held backup capacity during peak demand periods.

The power crisis highlights growing tensions between the rapid expansion of electricity-hungry data centers and grid reliability. Data centers have proliferated across the country to support cloud computing, artificial intelligence, and other digital services, but their power consumption has raised serious concerns about grid capacity and consumer costs.

Three Democratic senators have now launched an investigation into whether major tech companies are passing the soaring electricity costs of data centers on to ordinary Americans. Elizabeth Warren, Chris Van Hollen, and Richard Blumenthal sent letters to Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, and several data center operators demanding greater transparency and accountability. In some regions with significant data center activity, residential electricity bills have increased by as much as 267 percent over the past five years, according to the lawmakers.

"Through these utility price increases, American families bankroll the electricity costs of trillion-dollar tech companies," the senators wrote. They are demanding that data centers and tech companies "pay their fair share of their electricity rates" and cover a greater share of costs upfront for future energy usage.

According to the US Department of Energy, data centers could account for 12 percent of the country's power consumption by 2028. About one-third of the nation's more than 4,000 data centers are located in just three states: Virginia, Texas, and California.

A Cornell study published recently in Nature Sustainability found that data centers could annually consume as much water as 6 million to 10 million Americans and emit as much carbon dioxide as 5 million to 10 million cars.

Opposition to data centers is growing across the country. Local efforts have blocked or delayed roughly 64 billion dollars in data center projects, according to a report published earlier this year by Data Center Watch. The lawmakers noted that contracts between data centers and utility companies are almost always confidential, leaving the public unaware of why their electricity bills continue rising.

One recent Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study questioned the link between data centers and increasing electricity prices, finding that data centers may have actually helped reduce average retail electricity prices by allowing utility companies to spread fixed infrastructure costs among more power customers.

Russia deployed nearly 500 drones and more than 70 missiles in an hours-long assault on Kyiv and other parts of Ukraine in the early hours of Thursday, killing at least 21 people and injuring dozens more. City Mayor Vitali Klitschko described it as the worst Russian attack on the capital during more than four years of sustained air strikes against Ukraine.

Explosions rocked the city for several hours as waves of drones, cruise missiles, and ballistic missiles descended on multiple locations. Ukrainian air defense systems worked to intercept incoming weapons, but the sheer volume of the attack overwhelmed defenses across numerous areas. As dawn broke, fires burned at strike sites throughout Kyiv, with debris hitting residential buildings in several districts and a hotel on one of the central boulevards.

One of the most devastating impacts occurred on the left bank of the Dnipro River, where a large section of a nine-story apartment block was destroyed, reduced to rubble. Rescue teams spent the day searching through the remains hoping to find survivors. The death toll rose throughout Thursday and officials warned it could climb higher, with about 70 people hospitalized and rescue operations continuing.

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy had warned Ukrainians on Wednesday that a massive attack was likely. Speaking during a visit to Dublin, he urged people to use shelters and take precautions. In response, more than 50,000 residents took refuge in metro stations overnight, with some bringing mattresses and tents to sleep underground as the platforms became crowded.

Damage was recorded across 30 locations in the city, with most strikes hitting ordinary residential buildings. The Russian defense ministry claimed the attack targeted military facilities and energy infrastructure in retaliation for recent Ukrainian strikes, though it also hit regions outside the capital.

Upon returning to Kyiv, Zelenskyy visited one attack site and criticized international allies, saying that if they had provided air defense missiles as agreed, the damage would have been significantly less. "We are fighting alone. The victims are only Ukrainians," he stated.

The assault came as Ukraine has intensified long-range drone strikes against Russian oil refineries in recent weeks, causing fuel shortages across multiple Russian regions and prompting some areas to introduce petrol rationing. Ukrainian officials said they intended to maintain this pressure on Russian infrastructure.

Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha, visiting Japan, rejected claims that the strikes constituted legitimate retaliation. He stated that in the conflict between an aggressor and a defending nation, Ukraine has every right to strike military targets in Russia while Russia has no right to attack Ukrainian territory.

UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer called the attacks "a stark reminder that while Ukraine continues to pursue peace, Putin continues to inflict suffering and violence on the Ukrainian people."

A cybersecurity breach has compromised an intelligence-sharing network operated by the Department of Homeland Security, prompting a senior Democrat on the Senate Intelligence Committee to raise alarms about potential national security consequences.

The hacked system allows various government agencies to share sensitive intelligence with one another. The senator's warning indicates that accessed information could include details about security operations, though authorities have not publicly detailed what was actually taken during the intrusion.

This represents another significant cybersecurity incident affecting US government infrastructure. Federal agencies have repeatedly struggled to defend their networks from intrusions. Previous breaches have exposed employee records, internal communications, and classified materials across multiple departments.

The Department of Homeland Security has not yet publicly announced when the breach took place or identified which threat actors may have been responsible. As the agency that coordinates domestic security efforts and partners with state and local law enforcement agencies, its networks represent a valuable target for foreign intelligence services seeking to gather information about American security operations and vulnerabilities.

The timing of this incident reflects broader concerns about government cybersecurity practices. A series of high-profile breaches across federal agencies has prompted increased Congressional attention to network defense standards and incident response procedures. Members of Congress have called for stronger protective measures and faster detection and notification capabilities when breaches occur.

However, implementation of these security improvements has proceeded unevenly across different departments. Some agencies have made significant investments in upgraded defenses and monitoring systems, while others have moved more slowly in modernizing their cybersecurity infrastructure. Budget constraints and the complexity of protecting legacy systems have contributed to these disparities.

Intelligence-sharing platforms present particularly significant security challenges because they consolidate information from multiple agencies into centralized systems. This aggregation makes them high-value targets for attackers seeking to access classified or sensitive information that might otherwise be scattered across separate networks with different security protocols.

The breach underscores ongoing vulnerabilities in how the federal government protects critical systems. With foreign adversaries and criminal organizations continuing to develop sophisticated attack capabilities, federal cybersecurity officials face persistent pressure to stay ahead of emerging threats.

As this latest incident demonstrates, even systems handling some of the government's most sensitive information remain vulnerable to compromise. The incident will likely intensify debates within Congress about whether current funding levels, technical standards, and agency accountability measures are adequate to protect national security information.

The full scope of the breach and its potential consequences may not become public for some time, as investigations proceed into what occurred and how the intrusion was ultimately discovered.

A federal appeals court has upheld a lower court decision preventing the Trump administration from firing 19 career intelligence officers who worked on diversity, equity, inclusion and accessibility programs. The 4th Circuit Court of Appeals issued a 2-1 ruling Thursday, finding that the administration violated federal employment protections when it terminated these officers.

The intelligence officers held career positions within federal agencies and were assigned to roles focused on diversity and inclusion work when they were dismissed. They challenged their terminations in court, prompting the initial lower court to block the firings. The Trump administration subsequently appealed that decision to the 4th Circuit.

Career federal employees receive protections under civil service regulations that restrict how easily they can be removed from their positions. These protections differ significantly from those covering political appointees. The appeals court's decision suggests the administration did not follow proper procedures when it terminated these officers based on their work assignments in diversity-related roles.

The ruling represents a legal obstacle to the administration's broader effort to eliminate diversity positions across federal government agencies. One judge dissented from the majority opinion in the case, though specific details about the dissent were not disclosed in available reports.

The 4th Circuit Court of Appeals has jurisdiction over federal cases from Maryland, Virginia, West Virginia, North Carolina and South Carolina. This geographic area includes federal agencies headquartered in the Washington region, making the circuit court a significant venue for cases involving national security and intelligence agencies.

With this ruling in place, the 19 intelligence officers will retain their positions while the legal process continues. The Trump administration retains the option to pursue further appeals, potentially requesting a review by the full 4th Circuit panel or petitioning the United States Supreme Court for consideration. No announcement regarding next steps came immediately after the decision was issued.

The case highlights ongoing tensions between the Trump administration's push to curtail diversity initiatives within government and the legal protections afforded to career federal employees. Federal employment law requires that terminations follow established procedures and cannot be undertaken solely based on an employee's job assignment or role, particularly when that employee holds permanent civil service status.

The decision may have broader implications for other federal agencies facing similar efforts to eliminate diversity positions. Other branches of government employing career staff under civil service protections could face comparable legal challenges if they attempt similar terminations without adhering to required procedures.
