At least nine major companies receiving refunds after the Supreme Court struck down Trump-era tariffs directed portions of that money toward executive bonuses instead of consumer price relief, according to a ProPublica investigation published Monday. The investigation analyzed securities filings and earnings calls to identify companies that told consumers they had to raise prices because of tariffs but are now using refund money for leadership compensation.

Stanley Black & Decker, which makes vacuums, electric drills, and measuring tapes, is expected to receive at least $298 million in tariff reimbursements and indicated some will go to bonuses. The company told consumers it was forced to raise prices because of Trump's import fees. In financial disclosures, Stanley Black & Decker said the refund it received was partially offset by costs for variable incentive compensation, according to ProPublica.

TJX, the parent company of TJ Maxx and Marshalls, received $331 million in refunds and will accrue $112 million in year-end incentive compensation and discretionary bonuses for eligible associates globally because of the refunds, a company spokesperson confirmed to ProPublica. The spokesperson said the company has not disclosed details beyond that accrual because the fiscal year has not ended.

The Trump administration has refunded at least $100 billion to companies since August, about 60 percent of roughly $131 billion in tariffs collected, after the Supreme Court ruled that Trump's tariffs were illegal because they overstepped executive authority and should have required congressional approval. The administration has not specified which companies are receiving refunds despite requests for information. ProPublica found that almost all companies publicly planning to use reimbursements for customer refunds deal with other businesses rather than consumers directly, while some companies have remained vague about their plans for the money.

Five European Union countries announced plans to open a migrant deportation center in Rwanda next year, sparking criticism from Spain and human rights organizations over the legality and ethics of the arrangement. Germany, Denmark, Austria, Greece and the Netherlands said the return hub will be ready to accept transfers in 2027 and will apply to migrants staying illegally in their nations who are subject to deportation. The Dutch government said the facility will provide an alternative when deportees cannot be returned to their country of origin.

The five countries said the scheme is in line with EU and international law, but human rights groups disputed that claim. Human Rights Watch senior Africa researcher Clementine de Montjoye said Rwanda has a well-established record of repression of dissent, arbitrary detention, ill-treatment in detention and support for armed rebels in the Democratic Republic of Congo. The independent EU Agency for Fundamental Rights said any third country hosting a return hub should respect fundamental rights, including that people should not be sent to a country where they face serious risk of persecution.

Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez told Spanish broadcaster Telecinco that the plan had no future or humanity and was not the solution, according to AFP news agency. The arrangement follows a recent EU reform that allowed for the creation of hubs outside the bloc. At a joint press conference in Vienna, Austrian Chancellor Christian Stocker said the five countries wanted to relieve European asylum systems and stop traffickers arranging deadly trips across the Mediterranean.

The plan differs from a British deal scrapped in 2024 that envisaged sending people to Rwanda to have asylum claims processed. The new scheme will apply only to people who have exhausted their legal avenues to stay in the five nations. Diplomatic sources told AFP the hub is expected to initially run as a pilot hosting about 100 people, with each nation possibly sending about 20 migrants. The Dutch government said the first phase will only return single men, with a focus on people from countries of origin in the same region as Rwanda. Rwandan Interior Minister Vincent Biruta said the country is committed to working with European partners to provide a safe, legal and dignified solution for migrants.

A forensic expert has concluded that Jeffrey Epstein was killed in prison in 2019, contradicting the official ruling that his death was suicide. Professor Michael Freeman told BBC Newsnight that after reviewing autopsy files and photographs, he believes Epstein was strangled, most likely from behind. Freeman said the evidence includes a deep hemorrhage to Epstein's left shoulder, injuries consistent with being struck in the face, and apparent fingernail marks above the ligature mark on his neck that would be consistent with an attempt to remove a ligature during strangulation.

Freeman, who has served as a cause-of-death expert to state attorneys general in police custody cases including George Floyd, said he believed a ligature was used to strangle Epstein and then used to suspend the body from a bunk bed. He said the injuries and the way the body was found do not match the conclusion of suicide by hanging. Freeman obtained the files from Epstein's brother, Mark Epstein, who has maintained his brother was murdered. Freeman said his analysis was independent and unpaid, though it has not been peer-reviewed.

The official autopsy in 2019 concluded Epstein's injuries were consistent with suicide and showed no defensive wounds expected in a homicide. The medical examiner who performed the autopsy told the Department of Justice that the injuries were more consistent with suicide by hanging rather than homicide by strangulation. Chief Medical Examiner Barbara Sampson, who ruled the death a suicide, has previously said she stands firmly behind the determination.

Epstein was found dead on August 10, 2019, at the Metropolitan Correctional Center in New York while awaiting trial on sex trafficking charges. His death came a month after he had been briefly placed on suicide watch. Epstein was convicted in 2008 of soliciting prostitution from a minor and was charged in 2019 with running a network of underage girls for sex.

Thousands of customers at major U.S. banks reported trouble accessing services Monday afternoon, with Chase Bank citing a software bug in its hardware systems as a likely cause. Chase, the largest banking institution in the country, said the issue was temporary and not systemic or cyber-related. The outages also affected Wells Fargo and Bank of America, though Chase said the bug in its systems may have impacted other banks as well.

Nearly 5,000 reports of Chase Bank service disruptions appeared on the Downdetector website. Chase told customers that some transactions were taking longer than usual to complete but that they still had access to their accounts and funds. The bank, a subsidiary of JP Morgan Chase, serves more than 86 million customers at 5,000 branches across the United States and 7.4 million small businesses. US Bank said some of its systems were not directly affected, but clients using Zelle, a digital payment platform, received error messages saying their payments could not be sent. Zelle said its service was operating normally and that issues at other banks could be affecting payments. Customers also reported problems with Truist, PNC and Capital One, though those companies said they had not been impacted.

By early Monday evening, problems appeared to be easing for many customers. As of 6 p.m. Eastern Time, Chase, Bank of America and US Bank had seen a decline in the number of reported outages. The exact nature of the software bug remained unclear.

4:37 PM ET

President Donald Trump has nominated Eric Ueland as comptroller general and head of the Government Accountability Office, a position empowered to sue the administration on behalf of Congress. The nomination represents a push to confirm an ally before Democrats take control of Congress in January. Ueland currently serves as deputy director for management at the Office of Management and Budget, which has led efforts to withhold and cancel federal funding.

The Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee will hold a hearing on the nomination Thursday. Lawmakers in both parties have called for the GAO to challenge the Trump administration before the Supreme Court for withholding federal funds approved by Congress. The agency has issued more than a half-dozen findings of illegal impoundment of federal dollars since the start of Trump's second term but has not yet taken legal action.

Ueland has significant Capitol Hill experience as a former staff director for Senate Budget Committee Republicans under then-Chair Michael Enzi of Wyoming and chief of staff to former Republican Senate Majority Leader Bill Frist. He served as White House director of legislative affairs during the first Trump administration, where he brokered talks between then-Treasury Secretary Steven Mnuchin and senators on a Covid-19 economic relief package.

Democrats have criticized Republicans for what they call a sham confirmation process that breaks precedent for how the comptroller general is typically nominated. Some Republican senators, particularly those leaving office in January, could question whether it is appropriate to confirm a sitting executive branch official to a position responsible for investigating that same administration. The Supreme Court suggested last fall that the GAO is the only entity with standing to sue the Trump administration for violating the Impoundment Control Act. The agency has been operating under an acting comptroller general since last December.

US hyperscale data centers consumed as much electricity as many mid-sized countries, study finds

3:27 PM ET

Hyperscale data centers in the United States consumed approximately 82 terawatt-hours of electricity between May 2024 and April 2025, more than 148 individual nations including Austria and Venezuela, according to a study published in Nature Sustainability. Researchers used satellite imagery to identify 403 hyperscale facilities and estimated their power consumption based on electrical capacity.

The electricity demand produced 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions, comparable to Denmark's annual carbon footprint. About 54 percent of the electricity consumed by the facilities came from gas and coal. Researchers noted that two data centers using identical amounts of electricity can have dramatically different environmental impacts depending on their local power grids.
