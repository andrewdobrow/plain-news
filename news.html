Airlines face $100 billion fuel cost increase as Iran crisis disrupts energy markets

The global airline industry is bracing for an additional $100 billion in jet fuel expenses this year following the conflict in Iran and the resulting closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a vital shipping route for global oil supplies. With jet fuel prices expected to remain 70 percent higher throughout 2026, the International Air Transport Association warns that collective industry...

1:41 PM ET

The global airline industry is bracing for an additional $100 billion in jet fuel expenses this year following the conflict in Iran and the resulting closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a vital shipping route for global oil supplies. With jet fuel prices expected to remain 70 percent higher throughout 2026, the International Air Transport Association warns that collective industry profits worldwide will fall by half to $23 billion.

Industry leaders gathered at Iata's summit in Rio de Janeiro acknowledged that fare increases are now unavoidable. Willie Walsh, Iata's director general, stated: "High oil prices will inevitably mean higher ticket prices. There's just no way to avoid that." He added that industry research showed passengers were prepared to accept higher fares, though "the big unknown is how long travellers and shippers can tolerate the higher costs of connectivity."

Walsh characterized the situation as "a challenging and unpredictable time" with "wafer-thin margins," noting that "for a lot of airlines the increase in the fuel bill is potentially existential." However, he stressed that concerns about fuel shortages have diminished and compared the current situation favourably to the Covid crisis, pointing out that the industry remains profitable with traffic up 2 percent.

The burden of fare increases will not fall equally across all routes. Sean Doyle, chief executive of British Airways, explained that long-haul and business passengers would likely face steeper increases, while price-sensitive short-haul holiday flights would see the smallest price hikes. "A brand like BA, which has got a lot of long haul, a lot of corporate, a lot of premium; we'd expect maybe to have more pass-through of prices than maybe a carrier who's solely competing for leisure short haul," Doyle said.

According to Iata research, approximately half of passengers indicated willingness to spend substantially more on fares if prices track oil costs, which Walsh said "bodes well" for a strong northern summer season. Industry data shows more British and European travellers are planning to fly within the continent rather than venture farther afield given ongoing uncertainty around Gulf hubs.

Despite the fuel crisis, airlines face additional operational challenges. Iata raised concerns about the European Union's entry-exit system, which introduces biometric checks for non-EU citizens entering European borders. Rafael Schvartsman, Iata's vice-president for Europe, warned that processing times could increase from 20-25 seconds to 90 seconds per passenger, with potential system unreliability creating long queues. He emphasized that summer travel, particularly for British and American visitors to Mediterranean destinations, could face significant delays.

OPEC Plus recently approved modest increases in oil production quotas in an attempt to stabilize markets, though analysts noted this move is largely symbolic given the Strait of Hormuz closure continues limiting supply.
