Average gasoline prices across the United States have dropped below $4 per gallon following a U.S.-Iran peace agreement that will reopen the Strait of Hormuz to commercial shipping. The deal was signed by President Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian, with Pakistan's prime minister also party to the memorandum of understanding.

The agreement represents a significant development after more than 100 days of disruption to global energy supplies. Oil prices responded immediately to the news, with Brent crude tumbling to $82 a barrel, down from around $97 a barrel when peace negotiations were still uncertain. Wholesale gas prices fell approximately 6 percent.

The strait's closure during the recent conflict between the U.S., Israel, and Iran had severely disrupted energy markets and driven fuel costs higher. Two tankers carrying liquefied natural gas were already exiting the strait on the day of the announcement, heading to Pakistan and China, while a supertanker carrying Iraqi crude left the Gulf bound for China after being stranded for nearly three months.

Stock markets rose on the announcement, with investors responding positively to reduced geopolitical risk. Japan's Nikkei index rose nearly 3 percent and the pan-European Stoxx 600 index was up 1 percent. Treasury futures rallied, gold climbed 1.46 percent to $4,574 an ounce, and the pound gained almost 0.6 percent to $1.3506.

However, energy analysts cautioned that a return to normal oil flows will take months. Damaged energy infrastructure in Qatar and elsewhere requires repair before supplies fully normalize. One analyst noted that markets should focus on physical oil flows through the strait, which remain restricted despite the agreement. Another warned that markets had been "at this stage before, only for talks to break down," suggesting caution about overreacting to the deal.

Oil prices remain significantly higher than before the war began in late February, when Brent crude traded at around $70 a barrel. Analysts expect continued declines as supply chains normalize and oil inventories replenish, though seasonal demand and refinery capacity could affect the pace of future price reductions.

The agreement also had broader economic effects. Mortgage rates declined this week as market optimism about reduced geopolitical uncertainty increased. The easing of tensions provided economic stability after weeks of market volatility related to energy supply concerns and potential impacts on inflation and consumer spending.

For consumers who faced elevated fuel costs during the conflict, the price decline offers relief from the energy shock that had driven inflation fears and hawkish interest rate expectations worldwide.

Islamic State in the Sahel has claimed responsibility for an assault on Niger's international airport and adjacent air force base in Niamey, the capital, according to the SITE Intelligence Group, which monitors jihadist communications worldwide. The attack began shortly after midnight on Thursday and involved motorcycle-riding militants who used heavy weaponry and drones in what the group described as a "surprise and coordinated" strike, according to statements released through the Islamic State's Amaq news agency.

The regional Islamic State affiliate has been linked to several high-profile attacks in Niger recently, killing more than 120 people in strikes targeting the Tillabéri region in September and abducting an American pilot in October. Gunfire and explosions at the airport, located about 6 miles from the presidential palace and Base Aérienne 101, a military base previously used by U.S. and Russian troops, damaged at least three aircraft. The damaged planes included one belonging to Ivorian carrier Air Côte d'Ivoire and two from the Togolese airline Asky.

According to Nigerien authorities, the attackers arrived on motorcycles and security forces quickly repelled their advance, killing 20 of the attackers and arresting 11 others. A stash of ammunition also caught fire during the assault. Staff from Asky remained in their hotel away from the airport, though their passports remained in the custody of authorities.

Niger has been fighting a militant Islamist insurgency for a decade, with violence concentrated in border regions. The country serves as a base for Western counterterrorism operations in the Sahel, a region stretching across Africa south of the Sahara Desert where jihadist groups linked to al-Qaeda and the Islamic State have expanded their reach.

The attack comes amid significant political upheaval in Niger. A military junta has led the country since deposing democratically elected President Mohamed Bazoum in July 2023. General Abdourahmane Tchiani, the head of the junta, said on national television following the attack: "We have heard them bark, they should be ready to hear us roar." He directed his comments at neighboring West African states and France.

Since the coup, Niger has exited the Economic Community of West African States and joined with fellow junta-run states Burkina Faso and Mali to form the Association of Sahel States. The junta has previously blamed security chaos on neighboring countries and France without presenting evidence.

The airport attack occurred around the same time as a drone assault by the Islamic State West Africa Province on a military base in Nigeria's northeastern Borno state, which killed at least nine soldiers and wounded several others.

The Supreme Court has ruled that the federal government cannot broadly restrict gun rights for people who use drugs casually. The decision narrowed a federal law that had banned drug users from owning firearms, marking another expansion of gun rights by the court.

The case, U.S. v. Hemani, centered on a Texas marijuana user who challenged the restriction on gun ownership. The court sided with the individual, finding that casual drug use alone does not justify removing Second Amendment protections. The decision limits the scope of federal authority to disarm Americans based on drug consumption patterns.

According to the Supreme Court's reasoning, the law used to prosecute the marijuana user violated his Second Amendment right to bear arms and is unconstitutionally vague. The ruling builds on recent Supreme Court decisions that have expanded gun rights nationwide. The court has increasingly applied strict scrutiny to firearms regulations, requiring the government to demonstrate that restrictions align with historical traditions of gun regulation.

The case attracted attention from advocates on both sides of gun policy debates. Supporters of gun rights argued that the previous federal restriction swept too broadly, potentially affecting millions of Americans who use marijuana in states where it is legal. Gun safety advocates warned that loosening restrictions could make it easier for people with substance use issues to access firearms.

The decision creates questions about how federal gun laws will be enforced going forward. Federal law still prohibits unlawful drug users from possessing firearms, but the court's ruling suggests that casual or occasional use may not meet the threshold for disqualification. This distinction means law enforcement agencies and prosecutors will need to adjust their approach to cases involving both drug use and gun possession.

Going forward, authorities may need to gather more evidence of habitual use or impairment to justify restrictions on gun ownership. The vagueness issue identified by the court suggests that prosecutors must prove a higher degree of drug involvement than mere casual consumption to successfully prosecute cases under the federal gun restriction.

This latest decision indicates the court will continue to narrow exceptions that allow the government to prohibit gun ownership. The ruling reflects the current Supreme Court's approach to Second Amendment cases, where justices have consistently sided with broader interpretations of gun rights. The decision adds another layer to the ongoing national conversation about balancing public safety concerns with constitutional protections for gun ownership.

Vice President JD Vance said Democrats do not demonstrate sufficient gratitude for the United States. His comments sparked a response asserting that for many Americans, patriotism has never meant blind loyalty but rather a belief that the country can improve.

The exchange reflects ongoing political divisions over how Americans should express national pride and what constitutes appropriate patriotism. Critics of Vance's statement argued that questioning and seeking improvement represents a legitimate form of love for the country.

Gas prices expected to fall this summer barring weather disruptions

Prices at the pump are likely to decrease during summer months, though weather events could interrupt the downward trend.

A few minutes ago

After more than 100 days of major disruptions to global energy supplies, oil and gas markets have stabilized following a US-Iran peace deal that will reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Brent crude prices fell to $82 a barrel, while wholesale gas prices dropped about 6%.

However, prices may not return to pre-crisis levels for months. Buyers are racing to refill depleted emergency crude stockpiles, which could keep prices elevated even as supply restrictions ease. Weather disruptions could further delay any price improvements consumers see at the pump.
