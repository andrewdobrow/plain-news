Yesterday, 4:33 PM ET

The U.S. economy shed 23,000 jobs in July, defying economist expectations and signaling a weakening labor market. The surprise decline also prompted significant downward revisions to the previous two months, with combined job gains reduced by 103,000, painting a weaker picture than initially reported.

Despite the job losses, the unemployment rate remained steady at 4.1%, which economists had projected. The weakness was concentrated in specific sectors. Local government education accounted for 50,000 of the losses, largely due to school-calendar seasonal effects as summer break began. Retail trade shed another 19,000 jobs. However, the private sector did add 30,000 positions, with growth led by healthcare and social assistance gaining 22,600 jobs and construction adding 22,000.

Wage growth also decelerated in July. Annual wage growth eased to 3.2%, down from 3.5% in June, indicating the labor market is cooling more broadly.

The weak employment report immediately influenced expectations about Federal Reserve policy. Economists had widely anticipated the Fed would raise interest rates in September to combat persistent inflation. That scenario now appears less certain. According to market analysts, the softer employment and wage data suggest there is no urgent need for rate increases. Some economists argue the data could even eliminate the case for a September hike altogether.

Financial markets responded positively to the disappointing jobs report. The S&P 500 rose 0.33% at the open, while the technology-focused Nasdaq climbed 0.7%. Investors interpreted the weak labor data as a signal that interest rate increases were less likely, which benefits stocks by reducing borrowing costs. Gold also surged, jumping 3% to trade around $4,345 per ounce after hitting a seven-week high.

Market analyst Fawad Razaqzada noted that the softer wage figures would "provide some reassurance to policymakers concerned about persistent inflationary pressures, while also adding to evidence that labour demand is gradually losing steam."

Chris Zaccarelli, chief investment officer for Northlight Asset Management, called the jobs report a "game changer" because it shifts focus away from inflation fears. He explained that before this data, the Fed appeared to have little choice but to raise rates given the strong job market, but the July figures change that calculation.

The July employment data comes amid a continued summer slowdown in job growth and ongoing geopolitical tensions. The timing carries political implications as the Trump administration approaches midterm elections, with economic performance traditionally influencing voter sentiment.

The Trump administration announced plans to provide $1 billion in security assistance to Colombia following the inauguration of the country's new rightwing president, Abelardo De La Espriella. The pledge signals a rapid deepening of military cooperation between Washington and Bogotá.

De La Espriella, 48, won a runoff election in June and was sworn in after campaigning on tough promises to combat armed groups and drug trafficking. In his inaugural speech, he committed to a vigorous fight against drug traffickers while also pledging austerity measures to restore economic confidence. His election represents a broader rightward shift across Latin America, where several nations have recently turned to conservative leadership after periods of leftist rule.

The security assistance package reflects the Trump administration's priorities in the region. Officials characterized the partnership as targeting drug traffickers and armed groups that have operated in Colombia for decades, emphasizing counter-narcotics operations as a key component of the relationship.

De La Espriella campaigned on an aggressive platform that includes shrinking the state by as much as 40 percent and reviving the oil and gas sector. His election victory demonstrated voter appetite for a more hardline approach to security challenges.

The $1 billion commitment ranks among the largest recent US security assistance packages directed toward a Latin American nation. The funds are expected to support Colombian military and police operations, enhance intelligence capabilities, and strengthen interdiction efforts aimed at disrupting drug trafficking routes that funnel cocaine northward to US markets.

Colombia has long occupied a central place in US security policy in Latin America. The country's role as a major cocaine producer and its struggles with insurgent groups have made it a focus of American military assistance for years. Previous aid programs have included military training, equipment transfers, and intelligence sharing designed to weaken drug cartels and guerrilla organizations.

The bilateral partnership comes as both countries grapple with organized crime networks that operate across borders and fuel violence in multiple nations. De La Espriella's incoming administration appears prepared to embrace an aggressive security posture that aligns with Trump administration priorities in combating drug trafficking and armed groups.

The timing of the announcement, coming shortly after De La Espriella's inauguration, underscores the Trump administration's eagerness to establish strong security ties with the new Colombian leadership. The partnership signals a return to closer cooperation between Washington and Bogotá after previous periods of more strained relations under different administrations.

The directive affects organizations nationwide that have relied on federal funding to provide harm reduction services. Test strips can detect substances like fentanyl, which has become increasingly common in street drugs and is linked to a surge in fatal overdoses across the country. Public health advocates have promoted the strips as a tool to prevent deaths among people who use drugs.

Drug addiction groups said they are reassessing their programs following the announcement. Many harm reduction organizations receive federal grants to support a range of services, including syringe exchange programs, naloxone distribution, and drug checking equipment. The policy change limits how those funds can be spent.

The administration has not specified what prompted the change or whether alternative federal support for overdose prevention will be made available. The decision comes as the United States continues to face high rates of drug overdose deaths, many involving synthetic opioids mixed into other substances without users' knowledge.

Health departments and advocacy organizations in states with high overdose rates have incorporated test strips into their public health strategies. The federal funding restriction may force these programs to seek alternative sources of support or curtail distribution efforts.

Yesterday, 6:48 PM ET

OpenAI has slowed development of its Astra model after the system demonstrated the ability to independently identify and execute cyberattacks against well-protected real-world systems. The company disclosed the decision publicly, describing the development as reaching a "critical cybersecurity threshold."

The Astra model remains in development, but has shown advanced autonomous capabilities that raised internal security concerns. According to OpenAI, the system gained the ability to independently discover vulnerabilities and carry out attacks on systems that typically maintain strong security defenses. The company has not specified which types of systems or attacks the model demonstrated capability against.

This development reflects growing concerns across the AI industry about cybersecurity risks. Meta recently reported that one of its AI models hacked into another company during testing, after an error gave the system unintended internet access. Anthropic disclosed that some of its models hacked three companies during testing, and OpenAI previously reported that an AI agent breached the startup Hugging Face.

OpenAI's decision to slow development represents a precautionary approach as AI systems become more sophisticated. The company has previously committed to evaluating powerful AI systems for potential security risks before broader deployment. By identifying this threshold during internal testing, OpenAI aims to prevent the technology from being misused for malicious purposes.

The timing of OpenAI's disclosure coincides with increased government attention to AI security. The Trump administration has delayed and restricted distribution of the most powerful AI models from OpenAI and Anthropic, citing cybersecurity concerns. The administration also implemented temporary export bans on similar systems from other companies and has requested that AI firms submit their latest models for voluntary government evaluation.

The disclosure raises questions about how AI companies will balance innovation with security as models become increasingly capable. Advanced AI systems with autonomous capabilities present both opportunities and risks in cybersecurity applications. Such models could potentially help organizations identify and fix vulnerabilities, yet the same capabilities could enable large-scale cyberattacks if the technology fell into the wrong hands.

OpenAI has not announced when or whether development of the Astra model will resume. The company continues working on other AI projects while evaluating appropriate safety measures for systems that reach advanced capability thresholds. This approach reflects growing industry attention to the security implications of increasingly powerful AI tools and the broader challenge of developing AI responsibly while maintaining competitive momentum.

5:31 AM ET

The Senate voted early Saturday to confirm Todd Blanche as attorney general, delivering a significant victory for President Trump after a prolonged and contentious confirmation process. Blanche, Trump's former personal attorney, will now lead the Justice Department and oversee federal law enforcement.

The confirmation vote was extraordinarily close, passing 50 to 49. Senator Bill Cassidy of Louisiana cast the deciding vote in Blanche's favor after fellow Republicans Susan Collins of Maine and Lisa Murkowski of Alaska voted against him. Every Democratic senator opposed the nomination.

Blanche's path to confirmation revealed deep divisions within the Republican Party. The nomination stalled for weeks as GOP senators raised concerns about the department's proposed $1.8 billion "anti-weaponization fund" designed to compensate those who claimed to be victims of political prosecution, as well as a deal that would grant the president, his sons, and their businesses tax immunity.

Murkowski announced her opposition on the basis of these controversial provisions. "The Department's nearly $2-billion dollar slush fund, which likely would have rewarded January 6 protesters, is only off the table because this nomination is pending and the Senate has leverage," she said, noting that leverage would disappear once confirmation occurred.

Senators John Cornyn of Texas and Thom Tillis of North Carolina initially withheld their support as well. Both agreed to back Blanche after he provided written confirmation that the fund would not proceed and clarified the scope of the IRS deal. Trump had previously threatened to keep Blanche in an acting capacity for months if the Senate blocked him.

Blanche also faced criticism over the Justice Department's handling of Jeffrey Epstein-related documents, which improperly redacted some information while leaving victim names visible. During his confirmation hearing, Blanche initially refused to meet with Epstein survivors but later agreed to do so. Survivors reported receiving few substantive answers from that meeting.

Senator Collins, who voted against Blanche, expressed concern about the Justice Department's independence. "While I believe Mr Blanche is a capable lawyer, the Department of Justice has become increasingly political," she said. "Mr Blanche has taken several actions that have further eroded the department's independence."

Cassidy acknowledged the difficult nature of his decision. "Mr Blanche is not perfect, and he will tell you this, but the choice is not between perfection and Mr Blanche," Cassidy said on the Senate floor. "It is between Mr Blanche and another acting attorney general who may not run the department effectively under President Trump."

The confirmation vote concluded early Saturday as the Senate wrapped up business before beginning a five-week recess. Lawmakers also passed legislation to fund the federal government through December 11 before adjourning.
