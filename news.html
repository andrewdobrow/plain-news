Yesterday, 8:14 PM ET

A federal judge in Massachusetts has ordered the Trump administration to reinstall displays it removed from National Parks sites over the past year as part of a crackdown on diversity, equity and inclusion content and climate change information at federal facilities.

The ruling came in response to a lawsuit filed by park advocacy organizations against the Interior Department, National Park Service, and its leadership. The judge determined that the removals set "a dangerous precedent of censorship and sanitization" and ordered officials to reinstate the materials within 21 days.

The removals followed an executive order signed by Trump in March 2025 titled "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History." The order directed the secretary of the interior to examine monuments, memorials, and statues to determine whether they had been altered after January 2020 to represent what the administration called a "false construction of American history."

In a separate case in Pennsylvania, federal judge Cynthia Rufe ordered the National Park Service to restore a slavery exhibit that had been dismantled from Independence National Historical Park in Philadelphia. The exhibit documented the nine enslaved Africans owned by President George Washington who were brought to his official presidential residence during his terms in office. The names of these individuals were etched into a wall at the site: Oney Judge, Austin, Christopher Sheels, Giles, Hercules Posey, Joe Richardson, Moll, Paris, and Richmond.

According to Rufe's decision, historians identified the location of the President's House at the turn of the century through extensive research. The exhibit included information about how Washington rotated enslaved individuals in and out of Pennsylvania to prevent them from petitioning for freedom under state law. Two of the nine escaped their enslavement: Oney Judge fled in 1796 and eventually reached New Hampshire, while Hercules escaped after being brought to Mount Vernon.

In her ruling, Rufe opened with a quote from George Orwell's "1984" about the rewriting of historical records. She drew an explicit comparison between the novel's "Ministry of Truth" and the Trump administration's approach to historical exhibits. "As if the Ministry of Truth in George Orwell's 1984 now existed, with its motto 'Ignorance is Strength,' this Court is now asked to determine whether the federal government has the power it claims to disassemble historical truths when it has domain over historical facts," Rufe wrote. "It does not."

During a hearing, Rufe called the Trump administration's argument that a president could unilaterally order changes to national park exhibits both "horrifying" and "dangerous."

The court ordered the federal government to restore the President's House exhibit to its condition as of January 21, 2026, the day before its removal. Neither the National Park Service nor Philadelphia officials immediately responded to the ruling.

US and Iran Peace Deal Remains Uncertain Amid Conflicting Claims

Prospects for ending the war between the United States and Iran remain unclear as officials from both countries trade conflicting statements about the status of negotiations.

President Trump claimed this week that Washington and Tehran were on the verge of signing a peace agreement and announced he was canceling planned missile strikes. However, Iran's foreign ministry dismissed these claims, saying no final decision on an agreement had been reached.

The conflicting rhetoric follows two days of escalating military attacks that threatened to destabilize a fragile ceasefire. After an Apache helicopter was downed near the Strait of Hormuz, US Central Command launched strikes targeting radar and air defense facilities in the region. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps responded by firing long-range missiles at the al-Azraq base in Jordan. Kuwait reported intercepting hostile missile and drone threats, while Jordan's armed forces said they shot down five missiles.

Iran's foreign ministry spokesperson, Esmaeil Baghaei, accused the US of "harming" diplomatic efforts to resolve the conflict.

Trump later seemed to distance himself from his earlier optimistic comments about a potential agreement, posting angry social media messages describing Iranians as "very dishonorable people to deal with." This marked a significant shift from his previous statements suggesting a preliminary agreement could be signed as soon as this weekend.

US Vice President JD Vance provided limited clarity on the timeline for resolving the conflict, saying it could be concluded in a week or several months. When discussing potential negotiations, Vance emphasized the importance of addressing the Iranian nuclear program. "Right now, I feel that we are in a position to get a deal that is good for the United States economically and that really does deal with the Iranian nuclear program," he said. "Not just now, not just while Donald Trump is president, but for the long term, to where my kids can say when they're adults: 'Iran is not going to have a nuclear weapon.'"

The escalating cycle of claims and counter-claims reflects the volatility and unpredictability that has characterized recent negotiations between the two nations. Previous attempts at reaching agreements have collapsed, and officials have acknowledged the difficulty of reaching a lasting settlement.

The ongoing military tensions have broader implications for global stability. The conflict has disrupted energy markets and raised concerns about the security of critical shipping routes, particularly the Strait of Hormuz, which handles significant global oil supplies. Trading partners and regional allies have expressed concern about the potential for wider regional instability.

Both Washington and Tehran face domestic pressure to reach a settlement, though skepticism about diplomatic solutions remains high following earlier failed negotiations.

He said the agents appeared to be examining accusations related to the 2024 election, though the specific details of the investigation remained unclear. Haney characterized the search as part of what he views as a broader crackdown.

"This raid is a full-on coordinated assault weaponizing the justice department and DHS against people who are fighting for working-class voters and Black voters to make sure they have access to the ballot," Haney said.

The Cleveland FBI office did not respond to a request for comment about the search.

The timing and nature of the raid prompted swift criticism from Democratic officials and civil rights organizations. Ohio Representative Shontel Brown called it an unprecedented attack on democracy. She alleged the raids appear connected to what she described as a systematic effort to attack elections and promote unsubstantiated voter fraud claims.

Former Senator Sherrod Brown, who is running for office again this year, said reports of the raid were deeply disturbing. Cleveland Mayor Justin Bibb stated that if authorities have a legitimate basis for their actions, they should disclose it to the public.

Michael Waldman, president of the Brennan Center for Justice, characterized the action as an outrageous fishing expedition designed to intimidate people working for democracy. He said it represents an egregious misuse of law enforcement for political purposes and fits a pattern of federal inquiries targeting voting infrastructure ahead of the midterm elections.

The raid comes roughly one month after a top official in the office of the acting attorney general instructed prosecutors to prioritize voter fraud cases. Last fall, Ohio's Republican elections official referred over 1,000 individuals who appeared to be non-citizens registered in the state to the Justice Department. Federal investigators have also collected voter records across at least six Ohio counties.

Trump won Ohio's popular vote in 2024, securing 17 electoral votes for the state. Republican Bernie Moreno defeated long-serving Democratic Senator Sherrod Brown in an expensive race that helped Republicans gain control of the Senate.

Trump has repeatedly and falsely claimed that widespread voter fraud exists, recently focusing on California's vote-counting process. He has offered no evidence to support these claims, though his rhetoric has raised concerns about potential challenges to Republican losses in future elections.

According to multiple reports, the government designated these models as security risks, prompting the sweeping restrictions. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth publicly defended the classification, reinforcing the administration's position on the matter.

Anthropic has expressed disagreement with the decision. "We disagree that the finding of a narrow potential jailbreak should be cause for recalling a commercial model deployed to hundreds of millions of people," the company stated in a blog post, pushing back against the government's rationale.

The restriction represents a significant escalation in U.S. efforts to control access to cutting-edge artificial intelligence technology. The move reflects growing national security concerns about advanced AI systems and their potential applications.

The action aligns with broader Trump administration policies aimed at restricting technology transfer to foreign entities. Officials have framed similar initiatives as necessary safeguards for American technological advantage and national security interests.

However, the decision has drawn criticism from those who question both its necessity and its practical implications. Anthropic's objection suggests the company believes the security concerns may be overstated relative to the impact of restricting access to AI tools that have already been widely distributed.

The models in question represent some of Anthropic's most sophisticated AI capabilities. The restriction to U.S. users only marks a departure from the company's previous approach of offering its advanced tools on a broader scale.

This action comes as the Trump administration continues to implement policies affecting technology companies and their international operations. Multiple initiatives have targeted artificial intelligence development and distribution, with officials consistently citing national security and economic competitiveness as justifications.

The move also reflects heightened U.S. government attention to artificial intelligence as a strategic technology. Policymakers have increasingly viewed advanced AI systems as critical assets requiring government oversight and control regarding their distribution and use.

Anthropic's public disagreement with the restriction suggests potential ongoing tension between technology companies and the administration regarding the scope and necessity of such limitations. The company's assertion that the security concern relates to a "narrow potential jailbreak" indicates they view the government's response as disproportionate.

The long-term implications of this restriction remain unclear. It may signal the beginning of more aggressive government control over domestic AI companies' international operations, or it could represent a specific response to particular security concerns the government has identified.

The restriction affects both foreign users and international organizations that previously had access to Anthropic's most powerful AI tools, potentially reshaping the global AI landscape and U.S. companies' international competitiveness in the sector.

The US Department of Justice has approved Paramount's $111 billion acquisition of Warner Bros Discovery, clearing a major regulatory hurdle for a merger that will reshape the American media landscape. The antitrust division completed its eight-month investigation and determined the deal would not harm competition or consumers across streaming video, linear television, and theatrical film distribution.

The merged company will combine two major entertainment corporations with substantial assets. Warner Bros Discovery currently owns HBO, CNN, and one of the industry's most valuable film libraries. Paramount Global operates the Paramount+ streaming service, Paramount Pictures film studio, and cable networks including MTV, Nickelodeon, and Comedy Central. The deal is controlled by the Ellison family through Paramount Skydance and includes $24 billion in funding commitments from Gulf sovereign-wealth funds.

"Over the course of a rigorous eight-month investigation led by the Division's career staff, the Division received from the Parties over two million documents from over 80 custodians, substantial productions of data, as well as extensive documents, data, and advocacy from third parties across the media and entertainment ecosystem," the Justice Department stated in its decision.

Paramount described the combination as pro-competitive, saying it would create "a stronger company better positioned to compete against dominant technology platforms in an industry increasingly defined by intense competition for audiences, talent, technology, and investment."

However, significant challenges remain before the deal closes. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority opened an investigation with a August 7 deadline to determine whether further review is needed. European regulators are also examining the Gulf funding sources, with July deadlines for their decisions. Australia has already approved the transaction. Additionally, California's attorney general Rob Bonta indicated his office continues investigating, leaving open the possibility that a coalition of state attorneys general could file a lawsuit to block the merger in coming weeks.

The approval has drawn criticism from media reform advocates and lawmakers. Craig Aaron, co-chief executive of Free Press, called the decision evidence that "the fix was in at the Trump Justice Department from the start" and urged state attorneys general to pursue legal action.

Democratic Senator Elizabeth Warren called the approval "terrible news for every American who doesn't want Trump-aligned billionaires to control what they watch and how much they pay."

Journalists at both CBS News and CNN have expressed concerns about potential job cuts and editorial changes. Paramount and Warner Bros have promised $6 billion in synergies from the merger, which could lead to significant workforce reductions. David Ellison pledged in March that CNN's editorial independence would be protected, though speculation continues about potential leadership changes at the news networks.

The deal still requires additional shareholder approvals before closure.
