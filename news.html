The decision came after a volatile 24-hour period of military escalation. The U.S. had launched 49 Tomahawk missiles at Iranian targets, and Trump had threatened that morning to hit Iran "very hard tonight" and seize the country's Kharg Island, a critical oil export hub. By afternoon, he reversed course following what he described as talks with Tehran's leadership.

Trump posted on Truth Social that discussions and final points had been "approved by all parties involved," including the United States, Israel, Saudi Arabia, and numerous Gulf states. He claimed that Iran's leadership had approved a draft agreement extending the ceasefire, reopening the Strait of Hormuz, and launching 60 days of negotiations on Iran's nuclear program.

However, Iran has not publicly confirmed Trump's claims about an approved agreement. According to sources briefed on the negotiations, representatives from Iran and Qatar as mediators had resolved key gaps during Wednesday talks, but Tehran's formal position remains unclear.

The announcement prompted immediate market reactions. Brent crude futures fell 3.6 percent to $89.73 a barrel, while U.S. West Texas Intermediate crude dropped 3.6 percent to $86.83 a barrel.

Trump stated that a naval blockade would remain in effect until the agreement was finalized, and he promised to announce the time and place of signing shortly. He previously claimed that any peace deal must prevent Iran from developing nuclear weapons, which Tehran denies it is pursuing. Iran's negotiators have sought the lifting of international sanctions, unfreezing of frozen assets, and control over the Strait of Hormuz.

One of Iran's top negotiators warned that "wrong strategies and impulsive decisions will reset the entire board for the worse, explode energy infrastructure and markets and create an endless quagmire."

The cancelation drew criticism from Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, who accused the Trump administration of misleading the public about the status of the conflict. "Saying this war is over is an insult to the brave American servicemen who are risking their lives every day to fight it," Schumer said on the Senate floor. He called on Senate Republicans to support a war powers resolution to end the conflict.

Trump has claimed multiple times in recent weeks that an agreement with Iran was close, only to see negotiations stall. Both sides have continued limited strikes during the ceasefire period. The involvement of Qatari mediators indicates that indirect diplomacy between Washington and Tehran continues despite the recent military escalation and ongoing disputes over fundamental issues.

12:07 PM ET

The House of Representatives voted against a short-term extension of a key surveillance authority on Thursday, with the measure failing 218-198. The vote sets the stage for a potential lapse in Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, which allows intelligence agencies to collect communications of non-Americans located outside the United States without obtaining individual warrants.

The rejection came as Democrats announced they would block renewal of the surveillance law in protest of Donald Trump's appointment of Bill Pulte as acting director of national intelligence. Pulte, described as an inexperienced loyalist, became a focal point of controversy surrounding the reauthorization debate.

The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act provision permits intelligence agencies to gather communications on foreign targets, but has drawn criticism from civil liberties advocates and some lawmakers who argue it sweeps up Americans' communications in the process. The authority has become increasingly contentious, with both progressive Democrats and libertarian-leaning Republicans raising objections to its scope and oversight mechanisms.

House Speaker Mike Johnson condemned the vote, calling it "shameful and dangerous." In comments reported by NBC News, Johnson expressed concern that Democrats had voted to "jeopardize the safety and security of the American people." Intelligence officials have previously warned that allowing the authority to lapse could create gaps in the government's ability to monitor foreign threats, including terrorism and cyberattacks.

However, critics argue the program has been misused to access Americans' communications without proper judicial oversight. The failure to pass even a short-term extension reflects deeper disagreements over how to balance national security interests with constitutional protections against unreasonable searches.

The Friday deadline now looms without a clear path forward for reauthorization. Earlier this year, Congress had passed a short-term extension of the surveillance law, but lawmakers remain divided over the program's scope and oversight requirements.

The House vote leaves Congress with limited time to negotiate a compromise before the authority expires. Any new proposal would need to address concerns from multiple factions within Congress while maintaining what proponents describe as essential intelligence-gathering capabilities. Observers note that surveillance networks may not go completely dark immediately, but the lapse represents a significant setback for intelligence operations.

The outcome will determine whether the surveillance program continues, lapses temporarily, or undergoes significant reforms as a condition of renewal. The standoff highlights the ongoing tension between national security imperatives and privacy protections that has defined debates over Section 702 since its inception.

Wholesale prices in the United States rose 1.1 percent in May, exceeding expectations as energy prices drove the largest yearly increase since 2022. The spike reflects growing inflationary pressure stemming from rising energy costs linked to conflict in the Middle East.

The producer price index measures the cost of goods before they reach consumers, serving as an early indicator of broader inflation trends. The May increase marks a sharp acceleration from recent months and signals potential challenges ahead for businesses and consumers already facing elevated prices. Energy prices were the primary driver of the jump, reflecting volatility in global oil markets triggered by the US war with Iran that began in late February.

The surge in wholesale costs comes as the Federal Reserve prepares to meet next week to discuss monetary policy. Kevin Warsh, the central bank's new chair, and other board members will decide whether to adjust interest rates amid elevated inflation and a relatively strong labor market. Higher producer prices typically translate to higher consumer prices over time, potentially complicating the Fed's efforts to maintain stable inflation while supporting economic growth.

The data arrives amid renewed conflict between the United States and Iran, which has disrupted energy markets and pushed oil prices higher. The Strait of Hormuz, through which about 20 percent of the world's oil passes, has been effectively closed since the war began. Iran has threatened ships in the area, and maritime insurers cancelled war risk coverage, creating significant supply disruptions.

The national average gas price stood at about 4.16 dollars per gallon as of Tuesday, according to AAA. While this represents a 37 cent decrease from a month earlier, prices remain about one dollar higher than a year ago. President Trump claimed the prices were "not very high, relatively speaking" while announcing his administration was releasing oil coming out of the Hormuz Strait.

The yearly comparison shows producer prices climbing at the fastest pace in four years, underscoring how quickly inflation can reignite when supply conditions shift. The sharp uptick in energy prices has also driven consumer inflation to 3.8 percent, the highest increase since 2023. Americans have begun feeling the effects of high energy costs across many aspects of daily life, including groceries and air travel. Moody's Analytics has estimated that the war and resulting high energy prices have cost American households approximately 100 billion dollars.

Market participants are closely watching whether the inflationary spike will prove temporary or signal a more sustained trend. Businesses facing higher input costs must decide whether to absorb the increases or pass them along to customers through higher retail prices. Economists are reassessing their forecasts for consumer inflation and economic growth in light of the wholesale price acceleration. The Federal Reserve's response to this data will likely shape financial markets and economic policy in the months ahead.

Extreme rainfall killed 7% of world's rarest orangutan population

A catastrophic weather event in Indonesia has devastated the critically endangered Tapanuli orangutan population, with a new study documenting the deaths of 58 individuals following four days of intense rainfall in November 2025. The extreme precipitation, which exceeded 1,000 millimeters (39 inches) in North Sumatra province, represents a major blow to the species' survival p...

11:02 AM ET

A catastrophic weather event in Indonesia has devastated the critically endangered Tapanuli orangutan population, with a new study documenting the deaths of 58 individuals following four days of intense rainfall in November 2025.

The extreme precipitation, which exceeded 1,000 millimeters (39 inches) in North Sumatra province, represents a major blow to the species' survival prospects. The 58 deaths account for 11% of the local population in the West Block of the Batang Toru ecosystem and 7% of the entire global population, which numbers fewer than 800 individuals in the wild.

Researchers used satellite imagery analysis combined with population density estimates to assess the impact of Cyclone Senyar on the orangutans. The storm caused widespread environmental destruction, with approximately 8,300 hectares (20,510 acres) of critical forest habitat devastated by landslides triggered by the extreme rainfall. This represents 11.7% of the key forest area where these apes live.

Climate change has intensified the rainfall threat. Scientists determined that human-induced climate change caused by fossil fuel burning increased the rainfall intensity by up to 50%, transforming what might have been a severe weather event into a catastrophic one for the vulnerable primate population.

The findings carry urgent implications for the species' future. Previous research indicates that annual population losses of just 1% would eventually lead to extinction. With the Tapanuli orangutan already facing habitat destruction from mining operations, palm oil plantations, and a hydropower project, the impact of climate-driven disasters compounds existing conservation challenges.

"It is tragic to lose so many apes in this way," said Professor Serge Wich, a primatologist at Liverpool John Moores University and study co-author. "In landscapes where populations are small and fragmented, this type of weather or climate event can have population-level consequences. It is extremely worrying for the future of this ape."

Professor Jatna Supriatna of Universitas Indonesia emphasized the global stakes: "To prevent the first modern extinction of a great ape species, Indonesia must permanently protect the Batang Toru ecosystem, but our international partners must also meet their global commitments by providing immediate biodiversity-recovery financing."

The Indonesian government has temporarily paused major industrial activities in the Batang Toru area to allow scientists to investigate how to secure the species' long-term survival while also assessing risks to human populations. Researchers from Borneo Futures, World Weather Attribution, and Liverpool John Moores University recommended an immediate moratorium on land-use activities that degrade orangutan habitat, alongside expansion of protected areas to stabilize the remaining population.

The Tapanuli orangutan was only scientifically described in 2017, making it the world's newest and most endangered great ape species.
