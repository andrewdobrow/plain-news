OpenAI Delays Adult Mode as Company Focuses on Core Priorities

OpenAI has postponed the launch of its planned "adult mode" feature for ChatGPT, shifting focus to what the company considers more pressing improvements. The delay comes after CEO Sam Altman announced last October that the platform would relax content restrictions and introduce age verification systems.

The company stated: "We're pushing out the launch of adult mode so we can focus on work that is a higher priority for more users right now, including gains in intelligence, personality improvements, personalisation, and making the experience more proactive." OpenAI, which serves more than 900 million ChatGPT users, emphasized that while it still believes in treating adults appropriately, "getting the experience right will take more time."

Since announcing the adult content plans, Altman has declared a "code red" initiative to enhance ChatGPT's capabilities. This urgent push comes as OpenAI faces intense competition from rivals including Google and Anthropic.

In the meantime, OpenAI is rolling out age prediction technology to identify users under 18. When the system detects a minor, additional safety settings activate automatically, limiting exposure to graphic violence and sexual roleplay content. In the United Kingdom, where the Online Safety Act applies, OpenAI must implement strict age verification to prevent minors from accessing any pornographic images the platform generates.

The adult mode delay represents one of several significant developments at OpenAI recently. The company also made headlines after announcing a partnership with the Pentagon. Caitlin Kalinowski, a senior manager heading OpenAI's hardware division within its robotics group, resigned over the deal due to concerns about mass surveillance and autonomous weapons systems.

Kalinowski posted on X that she worried about "surveillance of Americans without judicial oversight and lethal autonomy without human authorization." While expressing "deep respect for Sam and the team," she emphasized that her primary concern involved the governance process itself. "My issue is that the announcement was rushed without the guardrails defined," she wrote, characterizing it as fundamentally a governance problem rather than a principled opposition to national security applications of AI.

OpenAI responded to the backlash by amending its Pentagon contract to explicitly prohibit domestic mass surveillance and autonomous weapons systems. Following Altman's acknowledgment that the deal appeared "opportunistic and sloppy," the company said: "We believe our agreement with the Pentagon creates a workable path for responsible national security uses of AI while making clear our red lines: no domestic surveillance and no autonomous weapons."

OpenAI noted that it recognizes employees and stakeholders hold strong views on these issues and committed to ongoing dialogue with staff, government officials, civil society organizations, and communities worldwide regarding these sensitive national security and surveillance matters.

5:01 AM ET

Rising oil prices have prompted analysts to revive discussion of demand destruction, a term describing the sustained loss of demand for a commodity caused by persistently high prices. The phenomenon occurs when consumers and businesses reduce consumption or shift to alternatives in response to cost increases that are expected to remain elevated.

The concept has historical precedent in energy markets. During previous oil price spikes, sustained high costs led to permanent shifts in consumer behavior, including increased adoption of fuel-efficient vehicles, development of alternative energy sources, and changes in industrial processes to reduce petroleum dependence. These adjustments can persist even after prices decline, creating lasting reductions in overall demand.

Current price levels have raised concerns among economists and industry analysts about whether similar patterns may emerge. The discussion reflects uncertainty about how long elevated prices might persist and what threshold could trigger broader behavioral changes among consumers and businesses. Energy markets remain sensitive to both supply constraints and shifts in consumption patterns.

However, immediate supply concerns are also affecting energy markets. Australia faces potential fuel shortages as major regional suppliers signal they may prioritize domestic needs over exports. Malaysia, Australia's top source of crude oil, has indicated it could restrict supplies to ensure local fuel availability, according to energy experts. South Korea, another major supplier to Australia, could follow suit if regional crude oil supplies continue to decline.

Kevin Morrison, an energy finance analyst at the Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis, said these warnings from suppliers are "really significant" given Australia's heavy dependence on the region for petroleum products. He noted that South Korea, Japan, and India all depend heavily on oil imports and would likely prioritize domestic markets over exports if supplies become constrained.

Within Australia, the situation has already created distribution challenges. In New South Wales alone, at least 107 petrol stations lack diesel supplies while 42 have no fuel at all out of approximately 3,000 stations statewide. Energy Minister Chris Bowen acknowledged that some service stations are running out of stock, though he stressed that supply disruptions have not yet occurred.

The federal government has not ruled out fuel rationing entirely if supplies become severely disrupted, though Bowen indicated this remains unlikely in the immediate term. State governments are preparing contingency plans. NSW has requested historical and forecast fuel data from major suppliers and is preparing to declare an energy supply emergency if necessary, which would grant regulators powers to control fuel distribution and direct supplies to essential services like hospitals and emergency responders.

The renewed focus on demand destruction and supply concerns comes as global economies navigate competing pressures of energy security, price stability, and environmental considerations. How markets and consumers respond to current price levels could have implications for future energy policy and investment decisions across multiple sectors.

6:00 AM ET

The hemp industry and its supporters are running out of time to persuade Congress to delay or block a looming ban on their products. Without congressional action, a ban on hemp-derived intoxicants will take effect in November, one year after it was included in last year's government funding legislation.

The ban was championed by Senator Mitch McConnell and has generated bipartisan interest in disruption, though time is rapidly running short for legislative intervention before the deadline arrives.

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services recently launched a pilot program to reimburse patients for hemp-derived products like CBD. However, the upcoming ban could derail this initiative. The program will make certain Medicare and Medicaid recipients eligible for reimbursement of up to $500 worth of hemp products annually and aims to evaluate whether these products could reduce other health-related costs.

The ban creates a problem because it defines hemp products differently than the 2018 Farm Bill. While the Farm Bill permits cannabis products from plants containing less than 0.3% delta-9 THC, the new ban will make all products containing more than 0.4 milligrams of total THC federally illegal. According to Jonathan Miller of the US Hemp Roundtable, this "will criminalize the vast, vast majority of hemp products, including most non-intoxicating CBD products."

Industry advocates have been meeting with lawmakers to push for alternatives. Inesa Ponomariovaite, owner of Nesa's Hemp, recently met with members of Congress. She noted that during these meetings, she had to explain the endocannabinoid system to senators unfamiliar with it. The endocannabinoid system is a network of receptors throughout the brain and body that interacts with cannabinoids and helps regulate pain, memory, cognitive processing, and energy.

Several legislative efforts aim to address the ban. In December, Oregon Senator Ron Wyden reintroduced the Cannabinoid Safety and Regulation Act, which would replace the ban with regulations ensuring hemp products are safe and free of contaminants. Indiana Representative Jim Baird introduced a separate bill in January that would delay the ban for two years.

Miller attributes the lack of progress to political polarization. "Congress isn't passing anything these days, it's so polarized and so partisan that it's hard for them to pass even the most obvious bills," he said.

Ponomariovaite argues lawmakers are focusing on the wrong issue. Instead of determining which cannabis compounds should be legal or illegal, she says regulators should prioritize contamination concerns. Hemp naturally absorbs toxins from soil, including bacteria and metals, which can contaminate extracted products. A recent Forbes Health investigation found that some popular CBD products contained mold, yeast, and fungicide.

Ponomariovaite stated she remains optimistic Congress will act before November, though she warned that if the ban takes effect, her products will become less effective.
