Yesterday, 5:51 PM ET

World Liberty Trust, a cryptocurrency venture connected to former President Donald Trump, has received preliminary approval for bank status from federal regulators. The decision marks an unprecedented development for a sitting or former president's business venture to gain banking authority.

The Trump-appointed regulator overseeing the application approved the banking license for World Liberty Financial, the crypto company backing the trust. The approval allows the venture to operate with the legal powers of a chartered bank, including accepting deposits and making loans, though it will focus on cryptocurrency-related banking services.

Critics have raised questions about potential conflicts of interest, particularly given Trump's influence over the regulatory appointment process during his presidency. The approval process for banking charters typically involves extensive review of financial soundness, management competence, and compliance capabilities. The crypto industry has sought greater access to traditional banking infrastructure for years, facing resistance from regulators concerned about the volatility and risks associated with digital assets.

World Liberty Financial has positioned itself as a bridge between traditional banking and cryptocurrency markets. The preliminary approval represents a significant step toward full operational status, though the company must still meet additional regulatory requirements before beginning banking operations. The venture joins a small number of crypto-focused firms that have obtained banking charters in recent years.

The decision comes as the cryptocurrency industry continues to seek legitimacy within the traditional financial system. Banking status would allow World Liberty Trust to offer services that pure crypto companies cannot provide, potentially giving it a competitive advantage in the market. The approval process and the Trump family's involvement in the venture have drawn scrutiny from ethics watchdogs and political opponents who question whether the regulatory decision was influenced by political considerations.

4:49 AM ET

Tropical Storm Lala is bringing hurricane-force conditions to Hawaii's Big Island with dangerous winds, heavy rainfall and high surf. The storm was located approximately 420 miles southeast of Hilo and was forecast to strengthen into a hurricane as it approached the islands.

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration reported that Lala was packing winds of 60 mph as it moved toward the archipelago. Hurricane warnings have been issued for Hawaii county, which includes the Big Island, while tropical storm warnings cover Maui, Molokai and Kahoolawe.

Forecasters predict significant rainfall across the islands, with 8 to 12 inches expected on Maui and the Big Island. Some areas could see up to 25 inches. A storm surge of 1 to 3 feet is also forecast as the storm reaches shore. NOAA's Weather Prediction Center warned that Hawaii "faces life-threatening flash floods and mudslides as Tropical Storm Lala approaches."

Governor Josh Green declared a state of emergency, stating that Lala poses a serious threat to the state, particularly Hawaii Island. "Our state and county emergency teams are coordinating closely with federal partners," Green said. "I urge everyone to secure their homes, gather essential supplies, review their family emergency plans and follow updates from trusted official sources."

Hawaii county Mayor Kimo Alameda urged residents to prepare immediately for possible damaging winds, flooding rain, high surf, power outages, loss of communications and road closures. Residents have been advised to clear gutters and drains, move valuables off the ground, seal gaps in doors and remove tree limbs that could threaten their properties. Officials also warned people to stay away from beaches and coastal areas where rough surf and rip currents pose dangers.

If Lala's center passes over the Big Island, it will mark the first direct hit by a tropical cyclone there since Tropical Storm Darby in 2016. The last major hurricane to strike Hawaii was Hurricane Iniki in 1992, which caused at least six deaths and billions of dollars in damage.

The storm's approach coincides with a powerful El Niño forming in the central equatorial Pacific Ocean. The NOAA predicts a 69 percent chance of a historic El Niño event that would exceed the strength of previous events dating back to 1950 when it peaks between October and December. El Niño conditions typically promote hurricane activity in the Pacific while suppressing it in the Atlantic.

Hawaii Volcanoes National Park closed access to Mauna Loa's summit on Thursday afternoon, with plans to close the welcome center, trails and the Nahuku lava tube during the storm.

Google Faces Major Copyright Lawsuit Over Gemini AI Training

Major publishers have filed a lawsuit against Google, claiming the company illegally used millions of copyrighted books to train its Gemini artificial intelligence system. The case, filed in federal court in New York, represents what the publishers describe as "one of the most prolific infringements of copyrighted materials in history."

The lawsuit was brought by Hachette Book Group, Cengage Learning, Elsevier, and bestselling author Scott Turow. The publishers argue that Google misused books originally provided for limited purposes through services like Google Books, Google Play Books, and Google Scholar. These platforms allowed Google to display searchable excerpts and sell ebooks, but the lawsuit claims Google went beyond these agreements by copying the works to train a commercial AI product.

According to the complaint, Google copied copyrighted books without permission or payment to build Gemini, despite internal discussions acknowledging potential legal risks. The filing states Google identified that it could face "10 billion to 100 billion dollars in potential fines" for using texts provided through Google Play Books. The publishers contend that Google's actions harm authors and the publishing industry, particularly as AI-generated content could replace sales of original works.

The suit includes a striking example of the concern. Gemini could generate "a 100-page murder mystery set in a quiet seaside town filled with secrets" in 20 minutes for 39 cents, potentially substituting for original copyrighted works on which the model trained. The publishers argue that "no publisher or author can compete with that."

The complaint names specific copyrighted books allegedly used without permission, including N.K. Jemisin's The Fifth Season and Lemony Snicket's Who Could That Be at This Hour?

This lawsuit adds to expanding legal battles over generative AI and copyright protections. Authors and publishers have filed multiple cases against Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta, alleging unauthorized use of copyrighted works for AI training. A judge previously ruled in Meta's favor in a copyright case brought by authors. However, Anthropic recently agreed to pay 1.5 billion dollars to authors who alleged their pirated books trained the AI chatbot Claude.

Earlier this year, thousands of authors including Kazuo Ishiguro, Philippa Gregory, and Richard Osman published an "empty" book protesting AI firms' use of their work without permission.

This new case follows an earlier attempt by Hachette and Cengage to join an existing copyright lawsuit against Google filed by authors and illustrators in 2023. Google opposed their participation, prompting the publishers to launch separate legal action.

The plaintiffs are seeking statutory damages, an injunction preventing continued alleged infringement, and a court order requiring Google to destroy unauthorized copies used in training its AI systems. Google did not respond to requests for comment.

Supreme Court faces emergency requests on Trump mail ballot restrictions and White House construction

The White House ballroom dispute has become a significant test of presidential authority. The administration demolished t...

6:00 AM ET

The Supreme Court is considering emergency applications from the Trump administration during the justices' summer recess, including requests to revive mail ballot restrictions and allow construction to resume on a controversial White House ballroom project.

The White House ballroom dispute has become a significant test of presidential authority. The administration demolished the historic East Wing of the White House to make way for the planned $400 million ballroom, which Trump has described as "a gift" to the American people. A federal appeals court for the District of Columbia ruled last week that the project lacked necessary approval from Congress and ordered construction to halt.

The administration has asked the Supreme Court to keep the appeals court decision on hold while it prepares a full appeal. Solicitor General D John Sauer wrote in the filing that the case "involves an extraordinary and unlawful injunction that will halt the ongoing construction of the integrated military complex including a totally secure ballroom space, at the East Wing of the White House, which is vitally required by national security."

The government argues that every component of the project functions as "an integrated whole geared toward remedying security deficiencies and bringing critical improvements to the White House." According to the administration, the ballroom structure is designed to shield a military installation located beneath it.

The lower court ordered that aboveground work remain blocked until Congress approves the project, but allowed construction on underground security structures to continue. The appeals court paused its own ruling until August 21 to give the administration time to file its Supreme Court appeal. Chief Justice John Roberts set a Tuesday deadline for parties challenging the project to respond to the administration's request.

The National Trust for Historic Preservation, which sued to block the ballroom's construction, criticized the administration's legal strategy. The organization said the administration "brazenly asserted" that even if ballroom construction were unlawful, the Supreme Court would lack authority to stop it. The group called on the Supreme Court to reject what it described as "the administration's transparent efforts to evade the rule of law, frustrate judicial review, and limit the availability of meaningful relief in the courts."

The emergency docket activity places the Supreme Court back in a prominent position during its summer recess, which typically runs from late June through early October. During this period, the court does not hear oral arguments but continues to process emergency applications and administrative matters. Individual justices or the full court can act on such requests even while not in regular session.

The Trump administration has appealed multiple lower court decisions to the Supreme Court this week, following temporary injunctions related to the president's attempts to restrict mail-in voting.

These platforms allowed Google to display searchable excerpts and sell ebooks, but the lawsuit claims Google went beyond these agreements by copying the works to train a commercial AI product.

According to the complaint, Google copied copyrighted books without permission or payment to build Gemini, despite internal discussions acknowledging potential legal risks. The filing states Google identified that it could face "10 billion to 100 billion dollars in potential fines" for using texts provided through Google Play Books. The publishers contend that Google's actions harm authors and the publishing industry, particularly as AI-generated content could replace sales of original works.

The suit includes a striking example of the concern. Gemini could generate "a 100-page murder mystery set in a quiet seaside town filled with secrets" in 20 minutes for 39 cents, potentially substituting for original copyrighted works on which the model trained. The publishers argue that "no publisher or author can compete with that."

The complaint names specific copyrighted books allegedly used without permission, including N.K. Jemisin's The Fifth Season and Lemony Snicket's Who Could That Be at This Hour?

This lawsuit adds to expanding legal battles over generative AI and copyright protections. Authors and publishers have filed multiple cases against Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta, alleging unauthorized use of copyrighted works for AI training. A judge previously ruled in Meta's favor in a copyright case brought by authors. However, Anthropic recently agreed to pay 1.5 billion dollars to authors who alleged their pirated books trained the AI chatbot Claude.

Earlier this year, thousands of authors including Kazuo Ishiguro, Philippa Gregory, and Richard Osman published an "empty" book protesting AI firms' use of their work without permission.

This new case follows an earlier attempt by Hachette and Cengage to join an existing copyright lawsuit against Google filed by authors and illustrators in 2023. Google opposed their participation, prompting the publishers to launch separate legal action.

The plaintiffs are seeking statutory damages, an injunction preventing continued alleged infringement, and a court order requiring Google to destroy unauthorized copies used in training its AI systems. Google did not respond to requests for comment.
