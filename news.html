1:34 PM ET

A federal judge has ruled that the Trump administration's $100,000 fee requirement for H-1B visas is unlawful and must be invalidated. U.S. District Judge Leo Sorokin in Boston issued the decision in response to a lawsuit filed by 20 Democratic state attorneys general challenging the fee that Trump announced in September.

The H-1B visa program allows U.S. employers to temporarily hire foreign workers in specialty occupations, typically in technology, engineering, and other skilled fields. Trump's executive order dramatically increased the cost of obtaining these visas through the new fee structure, which represented a substantial jump from previous fee levels. The judge determined that this fee requirement exceeded the administration's legal authority.

The decision affects thousands of employers across the country who depend on H-1B visas to fill positions they contend cannot be adequately filled with domestic workers. Technology companies have been among the largest users of the H-1B program, though the visas are also utilized in healthcare, finance, and other industries requiring specialized skills.

The ruling comes amid broader debates over immigration policy and labor market dynamics. Supporters of higher visa fees argue they protect American workers by ensuring that companies make genuine efforts to hire domestically before turning to foreign talent. They also contend that increased costs encourage employers to invest in training and developing the U.S. workforce.

Critics of visa fee increases argue that excessive charges harm American competitiveness by burdening businesses with additional costs without addressing underlying workforce development needs. They maintain that companies already face significant barriers and expenses in the H-1B hiring process.

The Trump administration has not yet announced whether it will appeal the ruling. The decision returns H-1B visa fees to their previous levels, though the program remains subject to annual caps limiting the number of visas issued each year. Employers who had already paid the higher fee may seek guidance on potential refunds or credits.

This marks another legal setback for Trump administration policies in court. The decision reflects ongoing tensions between the executive branch's immigration objectives and judicial oversight of regulatory authority. The case demonstrates how federal courts continue to scrutinize administrative actions that significantly affect immigration and employment practices.

The invalidation of the fee requirement represents a significant change in immigration policy direction. Businesses that had factored the higher costs into their hiring decisions and planning may need to reassess their recruitment strategies for foreign workers. The outcome also signals that courts remain willing to challenge administration immigration policies that they determine lack proper legal authority.

The H-1B program itself continues to operate under existing regulations and annual visa caps, but the removal of the additional fee structure will meaningfully reduce costs for employers seeking to hire specialized foreign workers through the program.

Countries that rely on imported energy are shifting strategies to prioritize domestic production in response to the ongoing war involving Iran, according to energy policy analysts. The conflict has created persistent volatility in global oil and natural gas markets, prompting governments to reassess their energy security. The war has disrupted traditional energy trade patterns...

1:56 PM ET

Countries that rely on imported energy are shifting strategies to prioritize domestic production in response to the ongoing war involving Iran, according to energy policy analysts. The conflict has created persistent volatility in global oil and natural gas markets, prompting governments to reassess their energy security.

The war has disrupted traditional energy trade patterns and raised concerns about supply stability. Energy-importing nations face sustained uncertainty about access to fossil fuels from the Middle East region, which has historically been a major supplier to global markets. This uncertainty has accelerated policy discussions about reducing dependence on foreign energy sources.

Governments are responding by expanding domestic energy production capabilities and infrastructure. The shift represents a strategic pivot toward energy self-sufficiency, with countries willing to accept higher near-term costs in exchange for greater control over their energy supplies. This trend marks a departure from the globalized energy trade networks that developed over recent decades.

The transition carries significant economic implications. Domestic energy production often requires substantial upfront investment in exploration, extraction infrastructure, and processing facilities. Countries pursuing this path must balance the costs of developing internal resources against the risks of continued reliance on volatile international markets. The speed and scale of these transitions will depend on each country's existing resources and industrial capacity.

The broader economic effects extend beyond energy policy. Higher energy costs associated with developing domestic supplies could affect manufacturing, transportation, and consumer prices. Meanwhile, traditional energy-exporting countries may face reduced demand for their products as importers turn inward. The long-term reshaping of global energy markets will depend on how long the current conflict persists and whether the volatility it has created becomes a permanent feature of the energy landscape.

Justice Department seeks to revoke citizenship of 17 people in denaturalization push

The Department of Justice announced it has filed denaturalization actions in federal district courts across the country seeking to strip citizenship from 17 foreign-born Americans accused of serious crimes. Officials described the effort as the largest denaturalization action in recent memory, marking an expansion of the Trump administration's immigration enforcement policies....

1:18 PM ET

The Department of Justice announced it has filed denaturalization actions in federal district courts across the country seeking to strip citizenship from 17 foreign-born Americans accused of serious crimes. Officials described the effort as the largest denaturalization action in recent memory, marking an expansion of the Trump administration's immigration enforcement policies.

Denaturalization is the legal process through which the government revokes previously granted citizenship. The practice typically targets individuals accused of obtaining citizenship fraudulently or through concealing disqualifying criminal conduct. Federal prosecutors filed cases in various U.S. District Courts, though specific allegations against the individuals were not detailed.

The announcement comes as part of a broader push to denaturalize naturalized citizens. According to reporting, the Justice Department has identified 384 foreign-born U.S. citizens whose citizenship it wants to revoke and will begin the process in coming weeks. A top Justice Department official, Francey Hakes, described the 384 people as representing "the first wave of cases" the administration intends to pursue, characterizing it as a "White House initiative." A White House spokesperson disputed this characterization, stating it "isn't a White House initiative, it's federal law."

The move represents a significant escalation in using denaturalization, a rarely employed legal tool. While the government has long possessed authority to revoke citizenship in cases involving fraud or misrepresentation, critics argue that aggressive denaturalization campaigns can create uncertainty for naturalized citizens and undermine citizenship permanence.

Between 2017 and late 2025, the U.S. stripped just over 120 naturalized citizens of their citizenship, according to reports. The 384 people now targeted represents a dramatic increase in such actions.

During a recent meeting, senior Justice Department officials told colleagues that civil litigators in 39 regional offices would be assigned to file cases. Due to the high cost and manpower required, the U.S. government has historically pursued denaturalization rarely. The scale of this initiative may divert resources from other department offices, including those pursuing healthcare and other types of fraud cases.

Recent cases filed include actions against a marine accused of a sex crime, an Argentinian man accused of falsely claiming another nationality, and a Nigerian man convicted of tax fraud.

When a person is denaturalized, they return to the status they held before becoming a citizen. Immigration is considered a civil matter, meaning immigrants do not have the right to an attorney in such cases. The government must prove that a person is not of "good moral character" before a judge to justify denaturalization.

The denaturalization process requires judges to determine whether citizenship was improperly obtained. Those facing proceedings have the right to legal representation and can contest the government's claims.
