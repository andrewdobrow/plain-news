4:08 PM ET

US stocks climbed and oil prices fell sharply after the United States and Iran announced a framework agreement to end their conflict and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, a critical shipping channel that handles approximately one-fifth of global oil trade.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average posted a new record close, rising about 1% on Monday following news of the potential breakthrough. The Russell 2000 index of small US companies also reached a new high, gaining roughly 0.8%. Stock markets worldwide responded positively to the development, with Europe's FTSE 100, CAC 40, and DAX all posting gains of between 0.8% and 1%. In Asia, Japan's Nikkei and South Korea's Kospi jumped about 5%, while China's CSI 300 rose 1.9%.

Oil prices tumbled dramatically in response. Brent crude dropped approximately 4% to around $83 per barrel on Monday, marking a three-month low. This represented the latest decline in a downward trend that began Friday when President Donald Trump announced progress toward a deal. Wholesale gas prices in Europe fell 6%.

The Strait of Hormuz closure had created severe disruptions to global energy supplies since early March, effectively removing 20 million barrels of oil per day from the market. However, Gulf producers have managed to reroute about 5 million barrels daily through alternative pipelines and regional hubs. Additionally, the US military reportedly assisted in moving roughly 2 million barrels per day through the strait via undetected tankers in recent weeks.

Trump announced Sunday that a deal was "now complete" and declared the strait would open from Friday. He wrote on social media that he was "hereby fully authorizing the toll free opening of the Strait of Hormuz" and authorizing removal of the US Naval blockade. He later clarified that oil would resume flowing after the peace deal's Friday signing.

Despite the agreement, significant details remain unclear. The exact timing of the strait's full reopening, oversight responsibilities for safe passage, and any conditions attached to the agreement have not been disclosed. Iranian officials indicated a 60-day negotiating period would follow for addressing broader issues including Tehran's nuclear program and sanctions relief.

The 15-week conflict had shaken the global economy and created widespread uncertainty in financial markets. Financial analysts view the preliminary agreement as removing a major source of market volatility. They suggest that fully restored energy flows could help reduce inflation pressures and support economic growth.

However, some analysts caution that crude oil may not fall significantly further in the near term given the complexity of the negotiations ahead, particularly regarding nuclear issues. Additionally, 38 Japanese-linked vessels remain stranded in the strait, and questions persist about waterway fees and compliance with ceasefire terms.

The British government has announced plans to prohibit children under 16 from accessing social media platforms, making the UK one of the latest countries to introduce such restrictions. Prime Minister Keir Starmer announced the ban, which would apply to major platforms including TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, Snapchat, and X.

The move follows Australia's implementation of similar age restrictions in late 2025, part of a growing international trend of governments attempting to limit young people's exposure to social media. Starmer characterized the initiative as "giving children their childhood back."

The proposal has received substantial support from parents, with nine in 10 backing an under-16 ban according to polling. However, reactions from young people themselves are more varied. A group of 12- to 16-year-olds interviewed by the Guardian expressed mixed views on the policy.

Some young people support the measure, citing concerns about addiction and mental health impacts. Kit, age 12, favors a full ban, saying "it's addictive and it can damage friendship and mental health." Others, however, worry about the practical consequences. Precisa, 13, runs a deodorant business using TikTok and Instagram for marketing and noted that a ban "will definitely be much, much harder" to manage. She advocates instead for tougher monitoring of harmful content and screen time limits.

The government consultation considers multiple approaches beyond an outright ban. These include restricting certain app features like autoplaying videos and infinite scrolling, imposing screen time limits, and controlling personalized algorithms. Some young people find these alternatives appealing. Zoe, 14, believes parental controls are sufficient and opposes a full ban. Andrew, 13, suggests banning social media access on weekdays while limiting use to one hour on other days.

A particular concern involves YouTube, which carries an official age limit of 13 but allows supervised access for younger children. Several 12-year-olds in the group use YouTube regularly for music, art videos, and sports content. Sophia expressed concern that a ban would mean she would "miss a lot of things."

Technology companies have strongly criticized the proposed ban. Meta, YouTube, and Snapchat argue that such restrictions will push teenagers toward less safe, unregulated platforms. They contend that their services, when properly regulated, offer safer alternatives.

Scientists have also raised questions about the policy. Experts point to insufficient evidence demonstrating that bans effectively reduce social media harms, and some campaigners argue the ban allows social media companies to avoid implementing meaningful safety improvements on their platforms.

The government plans to enact the new restrictions by spring 2026, following the conclusion of its online safety consultation next week.

This crash represents one of the deadliest incidents involving a B-52 in recent years. The Air Force will conduct a thorough investigation to determine what led to the loss of the aircraft and crew. The B-52 fleet continues to play a central role in U.S. strategic capabilities, with plans to keep the aircraft in service for years to come.

A Chinese rocket broke apart in orbit dangerously close to SpaceX's Starlink satellite constellation, generating an estimated 100 to 150 new pieces of space junk. The breakup occurred in an orbital region heavily populated by active satellites, raising concerns about potential collisions and the growing problem of space debris.

The incident adds to mounting concerns about orbital safety as more nations and companies launch satellites into increasingly crowded space. Starlink operates thousands of satellites in low Earth orbit to provide global internet coverage, making it one of the largest satellite constellations in operation. The proximity of the rocket breakup to these satellites heightens the risk of catastrophic collisions that could create cascading debris events.

Space debris poses a significant threat to operational satellites, space stations, and future missions. Even small fragments traveling at orbital velocities can cause severe damage to spacecraft. The new debris pieces will remain in orbit for extended periods, creating long-term hazards for other satellites operating in similar orbital paths.

The incident highlights ongoing debates about space sustainability and the need for international coordination on orbital activities. As commercial space operations expand and more countries develop space capabilities, experts have called for stronger regulations and debris mitigation standards. China has faced previous criticism for space debris incidents, including the uncontrolled reentry of rocket stages that have fallen back to Earth.

Tracking and cataloging the new debris will be a priority for space surveillance networks operated by the United States and other nations. These systems monitor thousands of objects in orbit to predict potential collisions and allow satellite operators to perform avoidance maneuvers when necessary. The growing amount of space debris complicates these operations and increases the operational costs for all satellite operators.
