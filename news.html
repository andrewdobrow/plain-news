The head of the Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has warned that the current Ebola outbreak could become the worst on record if containment efforts fail to slow transmission rates.

The outbreak, caused by the Bundibugyo virus in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, is already among the worst in decades. According to the latest UN figures, there are 676 confirmed cases and 136 deaths, with the overwhelming majority concentrated in Ituri province. The World Health Organization reports that the number of victims is roughly doubling every week.

The situation extends beyond the DRC's borders. Uganda has reported 19 cases and two deaths, though health officials say intensive contact tracing has kept the outbreak under control there. Despite the global risk remaining low, 22 countries including the US have imposed travel restrictions on people from the DRC, Uganda, or South Sudan.

Healthcare workers on the ground face significant obstacles. Frontline responders report shortages of personal protective equipment and vehicles needed to transport bodies. Testing capacity has improved but still needs to be faster and conducted closer to patients.

A major challenge is community trust. Many people in affected areas deny the virus exists or blame health workers for spreading it. This reluctance to engage with healthcare systems stems partly from cultural practices around funeral rites. According to research from the 2016 outbreak, unsafe funeral practices involving exposure to body fluids created an average of 2.58 secondary cases per incident.

The monitoring situation illustrates the scale of the problem. With about 600 confirmed cases in one area, health experts would typically expect around 24,000 contacts to monitor. Currently, only 4,955 contacts are listed, and just 57 percent of those are being monitored. This gap creates significant risk for sustained community transmission.

Dr. Jean Kaseya, director general of Africa CDC, emphasizes the problem: "It means there is a huge risk of transmission being sustained in the community. We still have some confirmed cases not admitted to hospital. They are somewhere in the community, elsewhere."

Healthcare workers themselves are among the hardest hit, as Ebola spreads through exposure to body fluids, and medical staff are most likely to encounter infected patients.

There are reasons for optimism. Scientists are rapidly testing and developing vaccines against Bundibugyo, and existing antivirals show promise. Daily coordination meetings among response organizations in Ituri are underway, and African leaders have scheduled virtual discussions to coordinate funding commitments.

However, misinformation continues to spread, and conflict in the region hampers response efforts. There have been attacks on aid workers and treatment centers. A joint response plan estimates 518 million dollars will be needed over the next six months to bring the outbreak under control.

SpaceX has exercised its option to acquire Cursor, an artificial intelligence startup, in an all-stock transaction valued at $60 billion. The move comes days after the rocket manufacturer's blockbuster initial public offering and represents a significant expansion of Elon Musk's artificial intelligence ambitions.

The acquisition marks SpaceX's effort to boost its AI division, xAI, which will gain access to Cursor's coding capabilities and developer base. Cursor, owned by San Francisco-based Anysphere, has built impressive coding models and counts more than one million users who rely on the AI-powered tool for development work. According to analysts, gaining control of a trusted developer tool offers a faster path to enterprise AI revenue than competing solely on model development.

SpaceX's timing capitalizes on extraordinary momentum from its market debut. The company listed at $135 per share on Friday and quickly saw its valuation surge to approximately $2.97 trillion at one point, briefly surpassing Amazon's $2.65 trillion market value. While shares eased back slightly by day's close, SpaceX maintained a valuation around $2.66 trillion, making it the world's fifth most valuable company. Shares have risen roughly 50 percent since the IPO.

The strong stock performance has practical advantages for SpaceX's acquisition strategy. As Bill Ackman noted, the company's high valuation means it requires fewer shares to complete large purchases, reducing shareholder dilution from the Cursor deal.

Analysts view the acquisition as a strategic move to strengthen xAI's competitive position. Matt Britzman, an analyst at Hargreaves Lansdown, described it as positive, noting that while Cursor lacks the scale of rivals Anthropic and OpenAI, it has developed impressive coding models relative to cost. Gil Luria of DA Davidson said the acquisition would improve SpaceX's standing in the frontier model race and allow Grok, its AI system, to offer enterprise customers a viable coding component.

One factor limiting Cursor's growth has been insufficient access to computing power, a constraint SpaceX can address through its data center operations. In regulatory filings for the IPO, SpaceX noted that Cursor's access to developers' data, including coding requests and design decisions, could help improve Grok's capabilities.

Anysphere, backed by prominent venture investors including Andreessen Horowitz, Thrive, Nvidia, and Google, will receive payment entirely in SpaceX stock. The deal does not use proceeds from SpaceX's IPO and is expected to close during the third quarter of 2026.

The acquisition underscores how SpaceX's soaring valuation is enabling it to pursue transformative deals in artificial intelligence, an area central to Musk's broader technology portfolio.
