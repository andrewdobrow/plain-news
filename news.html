2:44 PM ET

A federal judge has ruled that the Trump administration unlawfully barred applicants from 39 travel-ban countries from receiving decisions on asylum claims, work permits, green cards and citizenship applications.

The ruling represents a significant legal setback for the administration's immigration policies. The judge determined that the freeze on processing applications from these nations exceeded the government's authority and violated applicants' rights to have their cases reviewed.

The decision came on the same day the US Senate voted to pass legislation funding Trump's immigration enforcement agencies. The $70 billion bill passed 52-47 with no Democratic support after a marathon voting session that knocked down numerous proposed amendments. The funding supports US Immigration and Customs Enforcement and the Department of Homeland Security for three years, ending a partial government shutdown that had lasted since February.

The immigration freeze had prevented tens of thousands of applicants from advancing their cases through normal channels. The Trump administration had defended the restrictions as necessary for national security purposes. However, critics argued the policy lacked clear justification and created humanitarian hardships for people seeking legal entry to or residence in the United States.

The ruling requires the government to resume processing applications from the 39 designated countries under standard procedures. The decision does not automatically grant immigration benefits to affected applicants but allows their cases to proceed through normal review processes. The practical impact will unfold as immigration agencies work through the backlog of cases that accumulated during the freeze period.

Separately, a federal judge in New York issued an order restricting immigration enforcement actions in federal courthouses. US District Judge P. Kevin Castel ruled that ICE agents cannot arrest immigrants in or around three federal courthouses in lower Manhattan except under exceptional circumstances. The ruling came in response to a lawsuit brought by civil liberties organizations including the ACLU and Make the Road NY.

Castel noted that while there is "a strong governmental interest in enforcing immigration laws," there is also a serious interest in allowing individuals to attend removal proceedings and pursue asylum claims "without fear of arrest." The order applies to immigration courts at 26 Federal Plaza and two other Manhattan locations. Castel indicated that a court case before him would likely result in a ruling that it was "arbitrary and capricious" for the Trump administration to withdraw 2021 policies regarding enforcement actions inside courthouses.

The Justice Department has not announced whether it will appeal the ruling on the 39 countries. Immigration advocates have welcomed the decision as a restoration of legal processes for thousands of people whose cases had been frozen.

U.S. stock markets experienced a significant selloff on Friday, with major indices closing sharply lower after a strong employment report sparked concerns about interest rate policy. The S&P 500 fell 2.6 percent in its worst single-day decline of the year, while the Nasdaq dropped 4 percent in its steepest fall since April 2025. The Dow declined 695 points. The losses ended nine consecutive weeks of market gains.

The market downturn was driven by disappointing expectations about Federal Reserve rate cuts. Employers added 172,000 jobs in May while the unemployment rate remained at 4.3 percent, demonstrating continued resilience in the labor market despite economic headwinds. This robust hiring data convinced investors that the Fed would maintain elevated interest rates for longer than previously anticipated, triggering a sharp rise in Treasury yields with the 10-year yield climbing above 4.53 percent.

Higher interest rates for an extended period could slow economic growth and reduce corporate profitability, especially for growth-focused companies that depend on lower rates to justify their valuations. Technology stocks bore the heaviest losses, with semiconductor companies hit particularly hard. Chipmakers and other AI-related stocks tumbled across the board, with shares in companies like Nvidia and other data analytics firms experiencing significant declines despite some reporting improved revenue outlasts.

The market weakness extended beyond the United States. Asian markets followed with their sharpest slides in seven months, as indices in Japan and South Korea dropped more than 5 percent from recent record highs. European markets in the UK, France and Germany also declined slightly.

Several major bank executives warned of potential market corrections ahead. The chief executives of Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs joined JPMorgan Chase's Jamie Dimon in cautioning about market vulnerability. Analysts noted growing concerns about technology company valuations, particularly in the artificial intelligence sector. Many investors have highlighted that the vast majority of AI investment has been concentrated in a small group of companies, yet meaningful returns on these investments remain elusive.

The strong jobs report revealed underlying economic tensions. While headline employment figures appeared robust, wage growth has not kept pace with inflation, and consumer sentiment remains pessimistic. Young workers entering the job market face particular challenges starting their careers, a situation researchers indicate can have lasting effects on lifetime earnings and career advancement.

The market volatility also extended to riskier assets. Bitcoin briefly dipped below $100,000 for the first time since June as investors withdrew money from speculative investments amid broader economic concerns. The cryptocurrency had reached record highs above $126,000 in early October but has since faced significant downward pressure.

12:14 PM ET

NASA directed five astronauts aboard the International Space Station to shelter in their docked Dragon spacecraft and prepare for a possible evacuation while Russian space officials attempted to repair air leaks in the station's Russian segment.

The four crew members of NASA's SpaceX Crew-12 mission and a fifth American astronaut, Chris Williams, who had been aboard the station for 190 days, received the shelter-in-place order. The astronauts included American astronauts Jessica Meir and Jack Hathaway, French astronaut Sophie Adenot of the European Space Agency, and Russian cosmonaut Andrey Fedyaev. Two other Russian cosmonauts, Sergey Kud-Sverchkov and Sergey Mikayev, remained aboard the station.

The air leaks were discovered in the transfer tunnel of the Zvezda Service Module during pressurization of the module's transfer chamber. Roscosmos specialists identified two leak sites. They quickly sealed one leak using a two-component sealant called Germetal-1. The second potential leak was located on the conical portion of the transfer chamber. Russian officials stated there was no immediate threat to crew safety or station systems.

After approximately two hours of sheltering, NASA instructed the five astronauts to end safe-haven procedures and return to normal operations aboard the station. The decision came as Roscosmos paused structural repair efforts to assess additional measurements and data from the leak sites.

"Given this development, NASA has instructed the crew members inside the Dragon spacecraft to end the safe haven procedures and return to planned operations aboard the International Space Station. We look forward to working with Roscosmos on a collaborative approach to address the leaks," said Bethany Stevens, a NASA spokesperson.

The Zvezda Service Module, which measures approximately 43 feet long, serves as a critical component of the station. It contains living quarters, life support systems, communications equipment, electrical power distribution, data processing systems, flight control systems, and propulsion systems. The module was the first fully Russian contribution to the ISS and includes docking ports for Russian Soyuz and Progress spacecraft.

Air leaks have presented a persistent challenge for the Russian segment of the space station. Roscosmos has dealt with cracks and leaks in its station compartments for more than five years, with NASA's Office of Inspector General identifying them as a top safety risk. In June, a chartered spaceflight carrying astronauts from India, Poland, and Hungary was postponed due to similar leak concerns.

The International Space Station operates as a collaborative effort between NASA, the Canadian Space Agency, the European Space Agency, Japan's space agency, and Roscosmos. The station has maintained continuous human occupation for 25 years, orbiting approximately 250 miles above Earth.

The permanent ineligibility marks one of the most severe penalties the NCAA can impose and represents a significant point-shaving scandal in recent college basketball. An NCAA investigation uncovered evidence supporting the arrangement, leading to the permanent ban from college athletics.

The decision is particularly notable because one of the banned players was instrumental in Alabama State's breakthrough NCAA tournament win, a milestone moment for the Southwestern Athletic Conference program. The investigation's findings overshadowed what had been celebrated as a historic achievement for the university's basketball program.

Permanent ineligibility means the four players can never compete in NCAA-sanctioned athletics again. This represents the most severe penalty available to the NCAA for violations involving gambling and game manipulation. The investigation specifically examined the players' conduct during the 2024-25 season, when they were active roster members.

The NCAA announcement did not provide specific details about which game was allegedly fixed or the amount of money involved in the scheme. Point-shaving scandals, where players intentionally affect the margin of victory rather than the game's outcome, have surfaced periodically throughout college basketball history. However, cases resulting in permanent bans for multiple players from the same program remain relatively uncommon.

The ruling underscores growing concerns about gambling's influence on college sports. These concerns have intensified as legal sports betting has expanded significantly across the United States in recent years, creating new opportunities for potential manipulation and corruption. The NCAA has increasingly focused on protecting the integrity of collegiate athletics against gambling-related violations.

The case reflects broader challenges facing college sports as the landscape of legal gambling continues to evolve. Athletic programs and governing bodies must balance the expansion of legal betting with the need to protect players and maintain competitive integrity. The permanent bans signal the NCAA's commitment to enforcing strict penalties for gambling violations, particularly when multiple players from the same team are involved in coordinated schemes.

British actor Anthony Head has died at age 72 from complications of pneumonia. His daughters Emily and Daisy confirmed the news, stating he "passed away peacefully of complications due to pneumonia, surrounded by his family."

Head became an international fixture on television through his role as Rupert Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer. He played the school librarian and mentor to Sarah Michelle Gellar's character throughout the show's seven-season run from 1997 to 2003. His portrayal of the tweedy father figure made him one of the series' most recognizable cast members.

Before Buffy brought him global recognition, Head had built a substantial career in British entertainment. He began on stage, appearing in the 1978 West End revival of Godspell alongside Su Pollard and later playing Freddie Trumper in the original West End production of the Abba musical Chess in 1988. He also performed in the 1990 West End production of The Rocky Horror Show as Dr. Frank-N-Furter.

Head gained significant public attention in Britain during the 1980s for his role in Nescafe Gold Blend television commercials, which featured a slow-burn courtship between two neighbors. Beyond those ads, his film work included roles in the 1981 adaptation of Lady Chatterley's Lover and 1987's A Prayer for the Dying alongside Bob Hoskins and Liam Neeson. He also sang backing vocals for the British pop group Red Box, appearing on their 1986 debut album.

After securing the Buffy role, Head relocated to Los Angeles for five years while his partner Sarah and two daughters remained in England. He made frequent trips home, traveling every three to four weeks whenever the production schedule allowed.

Following Buffy's conclusion, Head continued working across various projects. He took a recurring role in the BBC sketch show Little Britain, playing a British prime minister fawned over by a flirtatious assistant played by David Walliams. He appeared in the BBC production of Merlin and the film The Inbetweeners Movie. More recently, he played Rupert Mannion, a former football club owner, in the Emmy-winning Apple TV Plus series Ted Lasso, which concluded in 2023.

His Buffy co-stars have paid tribute to his memory. Sarah Michelle Gellar began her Instagram tribute with a Buffy quote: "Tell Giles I figured it out and I'm ok." She then wrote, "Well I don't have it figured out and I'm not ok. But I know I'm the lucky one because I knew you."

Head's daughters honored their father's legacy, noting that "he loved his job very much and he always considered himself incredibly lucky to have been able to work alongside such exceptionally talented people, in such wonderful productions, across a career that spanned several decades."
