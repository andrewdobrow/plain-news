A few minutes ago

A federal judge has invalidated Donald Trump's $100,000 annual fee on H-1B visa applications, ruling that the policy violates federal administrative law and the Constitution.

US District Judge Leo Sorokin in Boston issued the 42-page ruling in a lawsuit brought by 20 Democratic state attorneys general who challenged the fee Trump announced in September. The decision vacates the controversial charge, which represented a 20-to-50 fold increase over existing H-1B application fees.

The H-1B visa program allows US companies to temporarily employ foreign workers in specialty occupations requiring technical or theoretical expertise. The program has been widely used by technology companies, healthcare providers, and other industries to fill positions demanding specialized skills.

Judge Sorokin found that the Trump administration lacked legal authority to impose such a substantial fee. The ruling represents a significant setback for the policy, which supporters had argued would generate revenue and incentivize companies to hire American workers instead.

The $100,000 charge would have applied to each H-1B visa petition filed by an employer. This dwarfed the existing application and processing fees, which typically total several thousand dollars. Business groups and immigration advocates had challenged the policy before it took effect, contending it would harm American competitiveness and make it difficult for companies to access needed talent from abroad.

The Trump administration is widely expected to appeal the decision. The outcome of that appeal remains uncertain as the case moves through the court system.

The ruling does not affect other aspects of H-1B visa processing or eligibility requirements, which continue under existing regulations. Employers can continue to apply for H-1B visas under the previous fee structure during the appeals process.

Immigration policy has remained a central focus of the Trump administration, with various proposals and changes implemented since taking office. The H-1B program itself has been subject to ongoing debate, with critics arguing it can depress wages for American workers and supporters maintaining it addresses genuine skills shortages in critical industries.

The fee proposal had generated substantial opposition from business communities that depend on accessing international talent. Technology companies, in particular, had raised concerns about how the increased cost would affect their ability to recruit specialists for positions they argued could not be filled with American workers.

The decision comes as courts continue to review other Trump administration policies implemented through executive orders and regulatory changes. Legal challenges to administration actions have resulted in mixed outcomes across different federal courts.

OpenAI has filed confidentially for an initial public offering on the US stock market, the company announced on Monday. The filing comes just over a week after rival Anthropic submitted its own confidential S-1 form to securities regulators, intensifying the competition between the two artificial intelligence giants.

OpenAI's announcement was characteristically blunt. "We recently submitted a confidential S-1. We expect it to leak so we're just announcing it," the company said in a blogpost. The filing represents a significant milestone for the ChatGPT maker, which is expected to be valued at more than $850 billion, making it one of the most highly valued listings in market history.

The company emphasized that it has not yet decided on timing for the public offering. "It may be a while because there are things we want to do that are likely easier as a private company," OpenAI stated. "But it's a complicated set of tradeoffs and this gives us the option to go public sooner if that ends up being best."

The confidential filing process allows companies to submit financial disclosures to the Securities and Exchange Commission privately while regulators review the materials. This approach has become standard practice for major companies seeking to go public. Both OpenAI and Anthropic are using this method, as is SpaceX, which has filed for a stock market float at a valuation of around $1.75 trillion.

Anthropic, which makes the Claude chatbot, announced its confidential filing on June 1st. The company recently raised $65 billion in funding, valuing it at $965 billion post-money. This latest valuation means Anthropic has leapfrogged OpenAI to become the world's most valuable startup, up significantly from a $380 billion valuation in February. The company suggested it could go public as early as fall of this year.

Both companies have grown dramatically throughout the year as the race for AI dominance intensifies. OpenAI has been backed by Microsoft, while Anthropic has received major investment from Google and other technology firms. Anthropic has positioned itself as a safety-focused alternative, emphasizing responsible AI development.

Going public will subject both companies to new disclosure requirements and shareholder scrutiny. They will need to demonstrate sustainable business models beyond venture capital funding. The companies will also face questions from public market investors about profitability timelines and regulatory risks in the AI sector.

The timing of these filings reflects both companies' desire to capitalize on investor interest in artificial intelligence technology. SpaceX, OpenAI, and Anthropic are all slated to pursue public offerings this year, underlining the rising financial stakes of the competitive AI race.

OpenAI has filed confidentially for an initial public offering, the company announced Monday through a blog post. The artificial intelligence giant is expected to become one of the most highly valued listings in market history, with a valuation exceeding $850 billion.

"We recently submitted a confidential S-1. We expect it to leak so we're just announcing it," OpenAI stated in the announcement. The company added that it has not yet decided on timing for the public debut. "It may be a while because there are things we want to do that are likely easier as a private company. But it's a complicated set of tradeoffs and this gives us the option to go public sooner if that ends up being best."

An S-1 is a registration statement submitted to the Securities and Exchange Commission in advance of an initial public offering. The confidential filing process allows regulators to review financial disclosures before making them public, giving companies flexibility to prepare for market conditions and adjust their plans without immediate public scrutiny.

The move comes slightly more than a week after rival Anthropic, which produces the Claude chatbot, filed to go public. Both companies are now competing to access public capital markets as artificial intelligence investment continues to attract investor attention.

OpenAI's potential IPO represents a significant milestone for the company, which was founded as a non-profit research lab in 2015 under the leadership of CEO Sam Altman. The company later converted to a for-profit structure and publicly released ChatGPT in 2022, which rapidly accumulated hundreds of millions of users and transformed numerous industries.

The company achieved its $852 billion valuation in March when it closed a funding round worth $122 billion, according to reporting on the financing. This valuation came despite OpenAI's struggles to achieve profitability and reports of the company missing key revenue and user growth targets.

The IPO filing arrives as OpenAI navigates several challenges. The company recently won a lawsuit brought by Elon Musk that sought to force OpenAI to revert to non-profit status. A jury ruled that the case fell outside the statute of limitations. OpenAI also faces more than a dozen lawsuits in which individuals allege that ChatGPT has exacerbated mental health crises and contributed to violent incidents.

Despite these obstacles, OpenAI has continued securing major partnerships with technology companies including Microsoft, Google, and Nvidia, as well as with the Trump administration. The public offering represents a potentially significant moment for the AI sector, with multiple companies pursuing listings as investor interest in artificial intelligence remains strong.

6:52 PM ET

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu ordered military strikes against Iran, defying President Donald Trump's calls for an immediate halt to hostilities. The action marks a notable breach between the two longtime allies over Middle East strategy and military operations.

The strikes came after Iran launched approximately 10 ballistic missiles at northern Israel in response to Israeli bombing in southern Beirut. Iranian state media reported explosions across Tehran, Isfahan, Karaj and Tabriz. The exchange represents the first direct military confrontation between Israel and Iran since a ceasefire paused the conflict in April.

Trump responded swiftly, posting on social media that "Israel and Iran must immediately stop 'shooting.'" In a BBC interview, Trump stated that Netanyahu did not defy him over the strikes, though sources indicate tension between the two leaders over military decision-making in the region.

The confrontation highlights a strategic divide. Trump has emphasized his desire to de-escalate tensions and broker peace, declaring in recent interviews that he "calls all the shots." Netanyahu, however, proceeded with military operations despite the president's publicly stated objections, signaling his assessment that Israel's security interests require continued action against Iranian threats.

Historically, Trump and Netanyahu have coordinated closely on major military operations against Iran, with the president providing substantial backing for Israeli objectives. The current disagreement represents a departure from this pattern of alignment. Netanyahu's limited political options in Washington have become apparent as Trump maintains strong influence over the Republican Party, constraining the Israeli leader's traditional ability to seek congressional support during tensions with a sitting president.

The regional impact has been immediate and significant. Saudi Arabia sounded missile alert sirens in an area near Prince Sultan airbase, which hosts U.S. forces. Yemen's Houthi rebels, who joined the Middle East conflict in March supporting Iran, reportedly launched missiles that the Israeli army said it was working to intercept.

Economic consequences rippled through global markets. Brent crude oil jumped 3.50 dollars to 96.59 dollars per barrel on Monday, while stock markets across Asia, heavily dependent on oil imports, declined sharply in early trading. The volatility reflects broader regional instability and investor concerns about further escalation.

Both Israel and Iran announced they would pause military strikes while warning of retaliation if the ceasefire faced additional breaches. These statements suggest a fragile situation where miscalculation could easily reignite wider conflict.

The public nature of the disagreement between Washington and Jerusalem marks a significant shift in U.S.-Israel relations. The episode raises fundamental questions about American influence over Israeli military decisions and whether the two countries can maintain strategic alignment when their security assessments diverge on key issues.

The confidential filing process allows regulators to review financial disclosures before making them public, giving companies flexibility to prepare for market conditions and adjust their plans without immediate public scrutiny.
