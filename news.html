A few minutes ago

President Trump announced Thursday that he is using wartime presidential authority to allocate $700 million to coal-fired power plants across the United States. The move represents the administration's latest effort to revive the struggling coal industry.

"Today, we're taking historic action to bring down the price of energy and the cost of living for all Americans with the power of clean, beautiful coal," Trump said at a press conference.

The funding announcement came during a White House ceremony where Trump was presented with a trophy by the Washington Coal Club, an advocacy group with financial ties to the coal industry. James Grech, CEO of Peabody Energy, the largest coal company in the US, presented the award to Trump while expressing gratitude for his support of the coal sector. More than a dozen coal executives and miners attended the event, along with several Republican lawmakers and cabinet members including EPA administrator Lee Zeldin and Interior Secretary Doug Burgess.

The administration is using the Defense Production Act, a wartime authority that grants expanded executive powers, to direct the Department of Defense to purchase power from coal plants. Trump stated at the ceremony that the military would be "buying a lot of coal" through long-term power purchase agreements with coal plants serving military installations and other mission-critical facilities.

The Department of Energy will allocate $175 million of the total funding to six projects aimed at modernizing, retrofitting and extending the operational life of coal-fired power plants in West Virginia, Ohio, North Carolina and Kentucky. Additionally, the announcement mentioned plans for two new coal plants in Alaska and West Virginia, though specific details were limited.

This coal initiative follows other recent administration actions. In September, the White House announced it would open 13.1 million acres of public land to coal mining and provide $625 million for coal-fired power plants.

The coal industry has experienced significant decline over recent decades. According to the Energy Information Administration, US coal production in 2023 was less than half of 2008 levels. Coal plants generated only about 15 percent of US electricity in 2024, compared to roughly 50 percent in 2000. Coal remains the most polluting fossil fuel.

The administration's investment priorities appear focused on energy security and economic support for coal-dependent regions rather than climate considerations. The use of wartime powers to direct resources toward coal represents a significant policy shift, allowing the administration to bypass standard legislative and regulatory processes typically required for major energy sector investments.

Hezbollah has rejected a U.S.-brokered ceasefire agreement reached between Israel and the Lebanese government, casting doubt on whether any truce can take hold. The Iran-backed group was excluded from negotiations but holds significant military power in the regions where fighting has occurred.

Hezbollah's leader Naim Qassem characterized the deal as a "roadmap to annihilate part of the Lebanese people." He called for a complete Israeli withdrawal from Lebanon and said the resistance would continue as long as the occupation persists. "We call upon the officials to put an end to this farce and humiliation called direct negotiations," Qassem stated in his rejection.

The ceasefire was announced following direct talks between Lebanese and Israeli diplomats in Washington. Under the agreement, Hezbollah would cease fire completely and withdraw its fighters south of the Litani River, while Israeli forces would gradually leave Lebanese territory. The Lebanese government announced the deal would take effect within 24 hours.

Lebanese Prime Minister Nawaf Salam warned that those rejecting or delaying the ceasefire would bear responsibility for the consequences. He described the negotiation track as "the fastest and least costly road for Lebanon, the Lebanese people, the south and its residents."

However, Hezbollah's rejection highlights a fundamental challenge: the Lebanese government negotiated without the armed group that actually controls southern Lebanon and fights Israel. The Lebanese army has not been a party to the conflict, and the government has been attempting to reassert control and disarm Hezbollah through diplomacy.

Despite the agreement, fighting continued. Israel carried out airstrikes in southern Lebanon and the Bekaa valley on Thursday, killing four people. Hezbollah claimed it targeted Israeli soldiers in the village of Qantara. Israel's Defense Minister Israel Katz said the country retained "freedom of action" to strike in response to attacks on Israeli communities and would maintain a buffer zone in southern Lebanon.

The ceasefire rejection also reflects pressure from Iran. Hours after the agreement was announced, Tehran said Israel should withdraw to its prewar positions. Iran's Quds Force commander stated that supporting Lebanese resistance and removing Israel from the region remained the duty of Muslims. Iran has previously insisted that any ceasefire agreement must include a halt to fighting in Lebanon.

The two countries have also agreed to establish "pilot zones" where Lebanese armed forces would take exclusive control while excluding non-state actors like Hezbollah. Israeli media reported that Israeli troops had begun withdrawing from some southern villages and being replaced by Lebanese forces.

This ceasefire represents the fourth round of direct talks between Lebanese and Israeli diplomats since fighting began in March. The previous ceasefire in 2024 saw Hezbollah agree to withdraw south of the Litani River, but full disarmament never occurred, and Israel carried out more than 10,000 strikes in violation of that agreement.

7:04 PM ET

The Cleveland Browns traded star edge rusher Myles Garrett to the Los Angeles Rams in a major transaction, with the Rams sending linebacker Jared Verse and three draft picks to Cleveland. The move reunites Garrett with quarterback Matthew Stafford, creating one of the most significant defensive acquisitions in recent NFL history.

Garrett, 30, is a two-time NFL Defensive Player of the Year and seven-time Pro Bowl selection. Since the Browns selected him with the first overall pick in 2017, he has established himself as one of the league's premier pass rushers. He holds the most sacks (125.5), pressures (413) and tackles for loss (149) of any player since his draft year. In 2025, Garrett set the single-season sacks record with 23.

Garrett had requested a trade from the Browns in 2024 after the team finished with a 3-14 record, stating he wanted to play for a championship contender. The Browns subsequently signed him to a four-year, $160 million extension, making him the highest-paid non-quarterback in league history at the time. The deal included $122.8 million in guaranteed money and averaged $40 million annually.

The Browns finished the 2025 season 5-12 and missed the playoffs for a second consecutive year. Despite the team's struggles, Garrett earned All-Pro recognition. The franchise then made significant changes, firing head coach Kevin Stefanski and hiring former Baltimore Ravens offensive coordinator Todd Monken.

General manager Andrew Berry facilitated the trade after the Browns and Garrett agreed to modify his contract in March, deferring option payments over the 2026-28 seasons. "As discussions intensified we were stuck at a legitimate crossroads: do we hold on to a truly generational player who has become the identity of our team, or do we make the difficult decision that we think is best for the organization over the long run?" Berry said.

The Browns received a 2027 first-round selection, a 2028 second-round pick and a 2029 third-round selection along with Verse. The 25-year-old linebacker was a first-round draft pick by the Rams in 2024 and earned Defensive Rookie of the Year honors. He has 12 career sacks and 22 tackles for loss.

The Rams lost to the Seattle Seahawks in last season's NFC championship game. This trade continues the franchise's pattern of major acquisitions, similar to their 2021 trade that brought quarterback Matthew Stafford from Detroit. Stafford led the Rams to a Super Bowl victory in his first season.

Garrett's addition marks the first time an AP NFL MVP and Defensive Player of the Year will be teammates, with Stafford winning his first MVP award last season. Super Bowl LXI will be played at the Rams' home stadium, SoFi Stadium, next February.

60 Minutes in Crisis After Scott Pelley and Two Other Correspondents Fired

CBS News appointed Nick Bilton as the new executive producer of 60 Minutes following the dismissal of three prominent correspondents and other senior staff members, marking one of the most significant upheavals in the program's history.

The network fired Scott Pelley, Sharyn Alfonsi, and Cecilia Vega, along with the show's executive producer Tanya Simon and executive editor. The departures have created significant internal turmoil and raised urgent questions about whether 60 Minutes can continue operating as a serious news program.

During a staff meeting with Bilton and CBS News managing editor Charles Forelle, Pelley directly criticized the network's direction. "She's murdering 60 Minutes," Pelley said, referring to Bari Weiss, CBS News' editor-in-chief. "She does not love this place. She was brought in to kill it and is doing exactly that." Staff members gave Pelley a standing ovation in response to his remarks. Bilton told Pelley he would not be "intimidated" by his comments.

Following his firing, Pelley issued a public statement accusing CBS News executives of instructing him "to inject falsehoods and bias" into his reporting. He wrote that 60 Minutes has succeeded for decades because audiences "find integrity, quality, and humanity in our stories."

Alfonsi released a blistering statement suggesting editorial independence had been compromised. "The wall between editorial independence and corporate interest at CBS is being methodically torn down," she said. She noted that journalists willing to challenge authority were being pushed aside, warning that the program would become merely something that "looks like 60 Minutes but lacks the courage and character to produce journalism that matters."

Vega alleged that political bias was being inserted into stories. "Reporting teams have held back on submitting story pitches about important news topics out of fear of the internal repercussions," she stated, characterizing the situation as "censorship, both imposed and self-driven."

The network disputed Vega's characterization but provided no detailed public explanation for the dismissals.

The departures have left 60 Minutes in precarious condition. Of the seven full-time correspondents shown on the network's promotional poster, three have now been fired and Anderson Cooper left the show earlier in the year. CBS News employees are asking critical questions about how the program will fill its broadcast slots and whether it can maintain the editorial standards that made it one of television's most respected news programs.

Bilton pledged to maintain the show's independence during his tenure, but the staff turmoil and allegations of editorial interference have created substantial doubt about 60 Minutes' future direction and capability.

Senate rejects Republican election overhaul as Trump priority stalls

The Senate rejected the SAVE America Act on Thursday, a comprehensive Republican election bill that President Trump had publicly declared a top legislative priority. Senate Majority Leader John Thune said the chamber lacked the 60 votes needed to overcome a Democratic filibus...

7:19 PM ET

The Senate rejected the SAVE America Act on Thursday, a comprehensive Republican election bill that President Trump had publicly declared a top legislative priority. The failure represents a significant setback for the administration's efforts to reshape election law.

Senate Majority Leader John Thune said the chamber lacked the 60 votes needed to overcome a Democratic filibuster and advance the legislation. "We don't have the votes, either to proceed to a talking filibuster nor to sustain one if we got one," Thune said. "That's just a function of math. There isn't anything I can do about that." He pledged to bring the bill to a vote anyway, guaranteeing debate but not passage.

Trump pushed back against Thune's position, telling reporters that the Senate leader had "got to be a leader" and instructed him to "get them" the votes needed. Trump also said he would not sign any other legislation until the SAVE America Act reached his desk. The president demanded additional measures not in the House-passed version, including a ban on mail voting, restrictions on gender-affirming surgeries for minors, and prohibitions on transgender women competing in women's sports.

The bill would require documented proof of U.S. citizenship to register and vote, mandate voter identification, and allow private parties to sue election officials over voter registration procedures. Voting rights advocates warned the legislation would prevent millions of Americans from voting, noting that only about half hold valid passports and that other documents like birth certificates may not match current names, particularly affecting married women.

Some Republicans balked at specific provisions. Senator Thom Tillis of North Carolina stated he opposed banning mail-in voting, saying "There's nothing wrong with mail-in voting if you have the right standards in place."

The rejection highlights divisions within the Republican coalition on election policy and reveals limits to Trump's influence over the legislative process. While Trump made election law changes central to his second-term agenda, unified Republican control of government has not guaranteed the votes needed for major policy shifts.

Trump championed the legislation on Truth Social, writing that the "SAVE America Act is by far the most popular Bill of its kind ever put before Congress." However, the bill's provisions and Trump's additional demands proved controversial among some members of his party.

The measure's collapse comes as Congress addresses multiple priorities, and suggests unresolved questions about whether Trump will continue pressing Congress on election policy or redirect his focus toward other legislative goals. Senate Republican leaders have not indicated plans to revive the legislation or pursue a modified version.
