The Justice Department told a federal court that the Trump administration's compensation fund for people who claimed they were targets of government weaponization will not continue. Two DOJ attorneys filed the notice in the Eastern District of Virginia, formally ending the nearly $1.8 billion program.

The fund was created by the Trump administration to compensate individuals who believed they were wrongly investigated or prosecuted for political reasons. The Justice Department's filing asked the court to reject a legal challenge brought by a former federal prosecutor and several other individuals related to the program.

The compensation fund represented one of the Trump administration's initiatives to address what it characterized as political targeting by federal law enforcement. The program's total budget allocation reached $1.8 billion before the Justice Department moved to discontinue it.

The court filing provides official confirmation that the program has been terminated. The former federal prosecutor and other plaintiffs had brought their challenge in Virginia's Eastern District, one of the busiest federal court districts in the country. The Justice Department's request for dismissal of that challenge coincided with its announcement that the fund would cease operations.

The end of the anti-weaponization fund marks a significant policy reversal. No information was provided about whether any compensation had been distributed before the program's termination or what will happen to the funds that had been set aside for the initiative.

The United States and Iran engaged in a series of military strikes on Saturday, marking the latest escalation in a conflict that has tested a fragile ceasefire established in April. The exchange underscores deepening tensions between the two powers and threatens ongoing diplomatic efforts to extend the truce.

The confrontation began when the US military shot down four Iranian attack drones headed toward the Strait of Hormuz, a vital shipping corridor through which roughly a fifth of global oil supplies transit. In response, American forces struck coastal surveillance radar sites on Sirik and Qeshm Island that officials said were involved in the attacks. US Central Command stated the Iranian drones "posed an immediate threat to regional maritime traffic" and the radar strikes were necessary to "defend against further attacks."

Hours later, Iran's Revolutionary Guards retaliated by launching missiles and drones at what they described as "enemy bases" in the Gulf region. The strikes targeted US military facilities in Kuwait and Bahrain, both of which activated air raid alerts. According to Central Command, Iran fired seven ballistic missiles toward Kuwait and Bahrain, with six successfully intercepted and a seventh failing to reach its target. The military reported no harm to US personnel and denied Iranian claims of damaging the US Fifth Fleet headquarters in Bahrain.

Kuwait's military also reported responding to "hostile" missile and drone attacks. The strikes came days after a previous attack on Kuwait's international airport that killed one person and wounded dozens.

The latest escalation reflects broader complications undermining ceasefire negotiations. Both the United States and Iran have been working for weeks to extend the ceasefire by 60 days and initiate new discussions on Iran's nuclear program. However, both sides continue demanding changes to any potential agreement, with neither showing willingness to compromise.

President Donald Trump told reporters on Friday that the situation "seems to be going quite well," though he acknowledged the negotiations faced challenges. "It's a very hard thing" for Iran, Trump said, noting that Tehran faces demands it never expected to accept.

The fighting also extends to Lebanon, where Israeli forces have intensified bombing campaigns despite a ceasefire announced this week through US-brokered talks. However, Iranian-backed Hezbollah rejected the agreement, and both sides have launched new attacks. Iran has demanded that any lasting truce include provisions addressing the conflict in Lebanon, further complicating peace efforts.

The escalations highlight the precarious state of the region. The Strait of Hormuz remains a flashpoint in US-Iran relations, with Tehran previously threatening to restrict shipping during periods of heightened tension. As both sides continue military operations while pursuing negotiations, the balance between conflict and peace remains increasingly unstable.

The U.S. Forest Service has announced plans to close research facilities as part of a cost-cutting effort, but an analysis of the agency's financial data reveals internal inconsistencies. The agency stated it needs to reduce expenses to operate within its budget constraints, yet the proposed closures appear to target lower-cost operations while maintaining facilities with significantly higher operating costs.

According to the agency's figures, some of the research hubs slated for closure cost less than one dollar annually to rent. Meanwhile, the Forest Service plans to keep open at least one facility that carries an annual rental cost of one million dollars. The disparity has raised questions about the rationale behind the closure decisions and whether the moves will achieve the stated financial goals.

The Forest Service oversees research operations across the country that support forest management, wildlife conservation, and environmental monitoring. Research hubs typically house scientists and support staff who conduct studies on forest health, fire ecology, invasive species, and climate impacts. Closures would affect ongoing research projects and could result in staff relocations or job losses.

The agency has not provided detailed explanations for how it selected which facilities to close or how the closures align with its broader budget strategy. Critics have pointed to the cost analysis as evidence that the decision-making process may be driven by factors other than pure financial efficiency. Some observers suggest that real estate considerations, regional politics, or administrative preferences could be influencing which locations remain open.

The proposed closures come as federal agencies face pressure to reduce spending and demonstrate fiscal responsibility. The Forest Service, part of the Department of Agriculture, manages 193 million acres of national forests and grasslands. Research facilities play a role in informing management decisions across these lands, and their closure could have long-term implications for how the agency addresses challenges such as wildfire risk and ecosystem health.

Yesterday, 5:21 PM ET

SpaceX has reached a $30 billion agreement to provide computing capacity to Google, marking one of the largest cloud computing contracts announced this year. Under the terms of the deal, Google will pay SpaceX approximately $920 million each month for access to computing power hosted at data centers operated by xAI, the artificial intelligence company founded by Elon Musk.

A Google representative attributed the agreement to unexpected demand for the company's recently launched AI products. The arrangement reflects the growing need for computing infrastructure to support artificial intelligence development across the technology industry.

The deal comes as SpaceX prepares for a potential initial public offering. The substantial recurring revenue from Google's monthly payments provides SpaceX with predictable income tied to the expanding demand for AI computing resources. This positions SpaceX to demonstrate consistent revenue streams to potential IPO investors.

The Google-SpaceX agreement follows similar patterns emerging across the industry. Other major technology firms have begun securing computing capacity through long-term contracts to support their AI development efforts. These arrangements signal a shift in how companies are approaching their infrastructure needs as artificial intelligence applications become more computationally demanding.

The partnership also comes amid broader developments regarding AI companies and government relationships. Google recently signed a separate agreement with the US Pentagon to make its artificial intelligence models available for classified government work. That contract allows the Pentagon to use Google's AI for "any lawful government purpose," according to reports. The Pentagon has signed agreements worth up to $200 million each with major AI labs including Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google.

However, Google's Pentagon agreement has generated significant internal controversy. More than 600 Google employees signed an open letter to CEO Sundar Pichai expressing concerns about the classified work arrangement. The employees stated they felt responsible for preventing unethical and dangerous uses of their technology, and urged the company to refuse making AI systems available for classified workloads.

The employees' concerns reflect longstanding tensions within Google over the company's involvement with military and surveillance applications. Last year, Alphabet, Google's parent company, lifted a ban on using AI for weapons and surveillance tools, removing language from its ethical guidelines that previously promised the company would not pursue "technologies that cause or are likely to cause overall harm."

Google's AI lead, Demis Hassabis, defended the shift by arguing that AI has become essential for protecting national security. The company has emphasized that it remains committed to consensus positions against domestic mass surveillance and autonomous weapons without human oversight.

The SpaceX computing deal represents Google's effort to meet surging demand for AI infrastructure, while the Pentagon agreement signals the technology company's deepening involvement in national security applications.

Microsoft's AI leadership stated the company has been liberated from its partnership with OpenAI and can now independently pursue advanced artificial intelligence development. The remarks emerged during Microsoft Build 2026, where the company introduced fresh AI models aimed at reducing dependency on OpenAI technology and cutting expenses for developers.

The declaration represents a major realignment in Microsoft and OpenAI's relationship, which had been defined by substantial Microsoft investments in the startup. Microsoft has constructed its own AI models to compete with OpenAI's offerings, expanding developer choices and potentially reducing licensing costs. At the conference, Microsoft positioned Windows as a trustworthy foundation for AI application development.

The divergence follows months of deteriorating relations between the two organizations. Microsoft served as OpenAI's primary cloud infrastructure supplier and major financial backer, but the partnership has frayed as each company pursues distinct strategies for advancing artificial intelligence. The Microsoft AI chief's assertion that the company had been "set free" hints that the collaboration had grown limiting rather than mutually beneficial.

During Build 2026, Microsoft focused on democratizing AI development and making it more economical for developers working with Windows. The company displayed infrastructure and tools supporting AI application creation independent of OpenAI's technology. Microsoft underscored security and trustworthiness capabilities positioned to make Windows the leading platform for constructing AI applications.

The separation enables Microsoft to engage in more direct competition with OpenAI in pursuing superintelligent systems. By advancing its own research agenda, Microsoft can establish independent technical priorities and development schedules without requiring coordination with OpenAI. This independence also allows Microsoft to retain greater value from AI systems built using its own infrastructure and resources.

The timing reflects broader industry dynamics. Anthropic, creator of the Claude chatbot, has surged ahead with a confidential IPO filing and recently surpassed OpenAI in valuation at 965 billion dollars following a 65 billion dollar funding round. Anthropic's Claude Code product has gained considerable traction with business clients, while its cybersecurity AI tool generated international attention for identifying bugs in widely used software. OpenAI's comparable products have struggled to achieve comparable market reception.

OpenAI is expected to file for its own public offering imminently, but the company appears to be losing ground in investor confidence and market perception. The reversal represents a significant loss of competitive advantage for ChatGPT's creator as the AI industry matures and valuations increasingly depend on demonstrated business performance rather than early-market positioning.

Meanwhile, Reid Hoffman has departed Microsoft's board after a profitable decade to focus on Manus, his AI drug discovery startup, signaling how prominent technology figures are reallocating attention across the rapidly evolving AI landscape.

Scottie Scheffler acknowledged the tightly bunched nature of the competition. "I've never seen anything like this," he said. "I've never seen a leaderboard this bunched up. It's quite literally anybody's tournament." Scheffler found himself five shots back after struggling with his putting on Saturday.

Rory McIlroy bounced back impressively from a difficult opening round. After shooting a poor first round where he made four consecutive bogeys and sat tied for 105th place, he carded a 66 on Saturday to move back into contention. "I've climbed my way out of that hole and I'm proud of myself for doing that," McIlroy said. "There's one more day left, and I feel like I'm close enough to the lead that I've still got a good chance."

McIlroy had been vocal about the difficulty of the course setup through the first two days. On Friday, both he and Scheffler complained about the pin placements. "When you have a set of greens like this, you can start to frustrate people pretty easily," McIlroy explained. He noted that while the challenging setup frustrated players, it created compelling entertainment for spectators. "If I wasn't playing this tournament, I'd love what's going on this week, but watching and playing are two different things," he said. "No profanity today," he added as he arrived to speak with the media.

The tournament committee appeared to adjust the difficulty on Saturday, moving pins to more accessible locations and benefiting from favorable weather conditions. The wind shifted and sunshine emerged, making some of the shorter par-fours reachable off the tee.

Smalley, making his first 54-hole lead in a professional tournament, had recovered from a rough start. He made four bogeys in his first eight holes before shooting five under par on his final ten holes. "I don't like being in the spotlight a whole lot," Smalley said. "I'm still trying to get used to playing in front of large groups of people like there are at tournaments like this one."

Jon Rahm, Xander Schauffele and Patrick Reed all sat just one shot behind Smalley. With such a congested leaderboard, Schauffele summed up Sunday's outlook: "It is going to be an absolute free-for-all."

Anthony Head, the British actor best known for playing Rupert Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, has died at age 72. He passed away peacefully from complications due to pneumonia, surrounded by his family, according to a statement from his daughters Emily and Daisy Head.

"It has been, and forever will be, an honour and a privilege to be his daughters, and to have witnessed first-hand the impact both he and his work have had on so many," his daughters said.

Head's most iconic role was as the mentor and librarian Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, which ran from 1997 to 2003. In the show, he served as a father figure to Sarah Michelle Gellar's title character throughout its seven seasons. More recently, he appeared in the Emmy-winning comedy series Ted Lasso, where he played former football club owner Rupert Mannion and worked alongside co-creator Jason Sudeikis, receiving praise for their on-screen chemistry.

Beyond these major roles, Head's career was remarkably diverse. He appeared in the BBC production of Merlin and had a recurring role in the sketch comedy show Little Britain, where he played a British prime minister. He also appeared in films including The Iron Lady and The Inbetweeners Movie.

Before his television success, Head had an extensive stage career. He starred in the 1978 West End revival of Godspell and later performed in the original West End production of the Abba musical Chess in 1988. He also played Dr Frank-N-Furter in the 1990 West End production of The Rocky Horror Show.

In the 1980s, Head became well-known in the UK and US for his role in Nescafe Gold Blend television advertisements, which featured a slow-burn courtship between a British couple. During that same period, he sang backing vocals for the British pop group Red Box and was featured on their 1986 debut album.

Head was born in Camden, London, to artistic parents. His father was a documentary filmmaker who founded Verity Films, while his mother was an actor who appeared in the 1960s BBC crime drama Maigret. His brother was also an actor, known for his lead role in the 1971 film Sunday Bloody Sunday.

Following news of his death, cast members from Buffy the Vampire Slayer paid tribute to their former colleague. Sarah Michelle Gellar shared a tribute on Instagram, beginning with a quote from her Buffy character before reflecting on her loss. His daughters noted that their father loved his work and considered himself fortunate to have worked with talented people throughout his career spanning several decades.

"We know his legacy will live on in the shows he was a part of and in the audiences that love them," his daughters said.
