6:06 PM ET

The FBI has informed multiple intelligence analysts they are being fired for their work on a memo that linked Catholic ideology to potential domestic extremism. The terminations mark the latest fallout from a document that drew widespread criticism from religious groups and lawmakers when it surfaced.

The memo, which originated from the FBI's Richmond field office, suggested that some Catholics who prefer traditional Latin Mass might be susceptible to radicalization. The document proposed developing sources within Catholic parishes and using church events for investigative purposes. After the memo became public, FBI Director Christopher Wray ordered it withdrawn and apologized, calling it a product that did not meet bureau standards.

The analysts being terminated had worked on the memo's creation and distribution. FBI officials have characterized the document as poorly researched and lacking proper oversight before it was circulated internally. The memo drew sharp rebukes from Catholic organizations and members of Congress, who argued it improperly targeted religious Americans based on their faith practices.

The firings represent a significant personnel action in response to the controversy. Federal employee terminations typically follow internal review processes, and the analysts involved may have appeal rights under civil service protections. The FBI has not publicly disclosed how many analysts are being let go or provided details about the internal investigation that preceded the terminations.

The episode has raised broader questions about FBI training and oversight of intelligence products, particularly those touching on constitutionally protected activities like religious worship. Some lawmakers have called for additional reforms to prevent similar incidents. The bureau has said it is reviewing its processes for vetting intelligence assessments before they are shared internally or with law enforcement partners.

5:00 PM ET

Security researchers have discovered a critical vulnerability in Creative's Sound Blaster Katana V2X speaker that allows attackers to compromise connected computers through Bluetooth without requiring any user action. The exploit, named Pwnd Blaster, demonstrates how USB-connected speakers can serve as an attack vector for PC infections.

The attack leverages the speaker's dual connectivity, exploiting both its Bluetooth interface and USB connection to the host computer. When the speaker is connected to a PC via USB, an attacker can use Bluetooth to transmit malicious commands that compromise the machine. Notably, the victim does not need to interact with the device in any way, click links, or download files for the attack to succeed.

Creative has declined to treat this as a vulnerability. The company has made no announcement regarding plans to patch the issue or offer product recalls. The Sound Blaster Katana V2X is a widely used PC speaker priced at approximately $300.

Security professionals have raised significant concerns about the implications of this discovery. The ability to compromise a computer through a peripheral device without any user interaction represents a substantial security risk, particularly in corporate environments where multiple connected devices are attached to individual workstations. The vulnerability highlights how seemingly innocuous hardware can become a dangerous attack vector for malicious actors.

The revelation has prompted reassessment of connected peripherals among some users. Security researchers recommend that concerned users unplug microphones and speakers when they are not actively using them. For devices that support both USB and wireless connectivity, disabling Bluetooth functionality is another protective measure worth considering.

This discovery adds to mounting concerns about the security of Internet of Things devices and computer peripherals that combine multiple connectivity methods. As technology increasingly integrates wireless and wired connections, researchers warn that similar vulnerabilities may exist in other household and office devices.

The Pwnd Blaster exploit underscores a fundamental challenge in modern computing: the expanding attack surface created by interconnected devices. While individual peripherals may seem low-risk compared to other components, their privileged access to computer systems can make them attractive targets for sophisticated attackers.

Industry observers suggest that manufacturers need to prioritize security in device design rather than treating vulnerabilities as acceptable trade-offs. The situation with Creative's Sound Blaster Katana V2X demonstrates how a manufacturer's reluctance to acknowledge and address security flaws can leave users exposed to unnecessary risks.

For users who own this speaker model, the absence of an official patch means security depends on defensive measures like disconnecting the device or limiting its Bluetooth range until Creative chooses to address the issue.

Anthony Head, the acclaimed British actor best known for playing Rupert Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, has died at age 72. He passed away peacefully from complications related to pneumonia, surrounded by his family, according to a statement from his daughters Emily and Daisy Head.

"It has been, and forever will be, an honour and a privilege to be his daughters, and to have witnessed first-hand the impact both he and his work have had on so many," they said.

Head's career spanned multiple decades and crossed both British and American entertainment industries. He achieved international recognition playing the mentor figure Giles, the school librarian and "watcher" to Sarah Michelle Gellar's character, throughout Buffy the Vampire Slayer's seven-season run from 1997 to 2003. The role made him a beloved figure among fans and helped define the mentor archetype in modern television fantasy.

Before becoming known to global audiences, Head had an extensive career in British entertainment. He began his professional work on the London stage, including a lead role in the 1988 West End production of the Abba musical Chess. He also starred as Dr Frank-N-Furter in the 1990 West End production of The Rocky Horror Show. Beyond theater, Head became widely recognized in the UK and US for appearing in Nescafé Gold Blend television advertisements throughout the 1980s.

His television work extended across multiple genres and productions. He took a recurring role in the BBC sketch comedy series Little Britain, playing a British prime minister constantly fawned over by a flirtatious assistant. He appeared in the BBC fantasy series Merlin and took on film roles in The Iron Lady and The Inbetweeners Movie. More recently, he joined the cast of Ted Lasso, the Apple TV+ comedy series, where he played former football club owner Rupert Mannion and developed on-screen chemistry with co-creator Jason Sudeikis.

Head was born in Camden, London, to artistic parents. His father founded Verity Films, a documentary company, while his mother was an actor who appeared in the 1960s BBC crime drama Maigret. His brother also pursued acting, earning a lead role in the 1971 film Sunday Bloody Sunday.

In an earlier interview with the Guardian, Head reflected on his childhood immersion in performance. "My Christmas present was always a new dressing-up outfit that my mother would make herself," he recalled. "At playgroup the woman who ran it took my parents aside one day and said, we love it when Anthony comes in dressing-up clothes, but it would be really nice to meet Anthony himself one day."

Head's daughters paid tribute to their father's legacy, noting that his grief is substantial but that his contributions will continue through the shows he appeared in and the audiences who love them. "How lucky we are to know we are able to watch him doing what he loved, even when he is no longer with us," they said.
