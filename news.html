The Bank of Japan raised its benchmark interest rate to 1% on Tuesday, the highest level in 31 years, defying pressure from Prime Minister Takaichi and marking a significant shift in the country's monetary policy. The decision comes amid mounting concerns over inflation driven by energy disruptions and a weakening yen, as well as pressure from the United States.

The rate increase represents the central bank's continued move away from the near-zero interest rate policy it has maintained since the mid-1990s. The Bank of Japan has been gradually raising rates since 2024, but this latest hike signals a more aggressive stance in response to mounting economic pressures. The bank also announced plans to stop reducing its bond purchases, a key component of its previous stimulus efforts.

The decision to raise rates came despite opposition from Prime Minister Takaichi, highlighting tensions between the government and the central bank over monetary policy direction. The move was influenced by multiple factors, including pressure from U.S. officials, concerns about the yen's sharp decline against other major currencies, and rising inflation stemming from global energy market disruptions.

Japan's currency has been under significant pressure in recent months, making imports more expensive and contributing to domestic inflation. Energy costs have surged amid global supply disruptions, adding to the inflationary pressures facing Japanese households and businesses. The central bank appears to have concluded that maintaining ultra-low rates was no longer tenable given these economic conditions.

The rate hike marks a turning point for Japan's economy, which has relied on accommodative monetary policy for decades to combat deflation and stimulate growth. The move to 1% represents the highest borrowing costs Japanese businesses and consumers have faced in more than three decades, potentially affecting everything from corporate investment decisions to household spending patterns. Financial markets will be closely watching how the economy responds to this shift in monetary policy direction.

The European Union has formally launched membership negotiations with Ukraine and Moldova, starting substantive talks that represent a crucial milestone for both countries seeking to join the bloc. The negotiations began in Luxembourg on Monday with senior EU officials and ministers from both nations.

This moment carries significant symbolic weight for Ukraine and Moldova, both former Soviet republics that applied for EU membership days after Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022. The countries were quickly accepted as candidates in 2022, but substantive negotiations faced delays due to opposition from Hungary's government under Prime Minister Viktor Orbán. A change in Hungary's leadership in April removed this obstacle, allowing EU member states to unanimously agree last Friday to open talks on the first cluster of negotiation chapters.

The initial cluster covers rule of law and democracy, with discussions expanding to other areas including the single market, environment, and economic and social policy. EU leaders Ursula von der Leyen and António Costa said in a joint statement that they praised "the determination, courage and hard work shown by both countries in advancing reforms, even in the face of immense challenges."

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy responded positively to the development, stating that "Ukraine is doing what is necessary, and it is important that the EU is also keeping its word." He called the opening of the first cluster "significant political and moral support for our state and our people."

The accession process is lengthy and complex. To join the EU, a country must adopt thousands of European laws and decisions, then secure unanimous approval from all existing member states. Officials estimate that with sufficient commitment, Ukraine could complete technical negotiations in approximately four years, though membership ultimately remains a political decision.

EU officials have acknowledged progress in Ukraine's reform efforts, citing high-profile arrests related to corruption investigations as positive signs. However, they have expressed disappointment that Ukraine has completed only about 15 percent of reforms outlined in a ten-point plan agreed last December. This plan includes strengthening anti-corruption agencies, adopting anti-corruption strategies, and reforming judicial appointment processes.

A proposal from German Chancellor Friedrich Merz suggesting "associate membership" for Ukraine, which would grant representation in EU institutions without voting rights, has not gained traction among other EU capitals. Instead, the concept of staged membership appears to be gaining acceptance, with Ukraine already participating in some EU policies.

Security experts have argued that the EU should prioritize integrating Ukraine into security and defense policy, where EU law is less stringent. Ukraine's military experience and modern weapons capabilities make it a significant security asset for Europe, observers have noted.

The revamped Siri, now branded as "Siri AI," has been fundamentally rebuilt with generative AI at its core. Rather than functioning as a traditional question-and-answer tool, the new assistant more closely resembles AI chatbots like ChatGPT and Google Gemini. Mike Rockwell, Apple's vice-president of Siri engineering, demonstrated the tool's enhanced capabilities, including improved conversational abilities and integration across Apple's native app ecosystem.

The updated Siri will operate as a dedicated app available on both mobile devices and laptops. It can now assist with daily planning, information retrieval, directions, proofreading, shopping, calendar management, and image-based tasks like extracting nutrition information from photos. During a demonstration, Rockwell showed how Siri could identify a beach location from a photo, retrieve a friend's address, and provide directions with a planned stop, all within a single interface rather than jumping between multiple apps.

Notably, Siri's transformation is powered by Google's Gemini AI model through a billion-dollar partnership between the two companies. This collaboration reflects Apple's broader strategy shift as it works to catch up with competitors who have aggressively pursued AI development over the past decade. The initial release will be available only in English.

According to Dipanjan Chatterjee, a principal analyst at Forrester, "A fundamentally re-architected Siri inside iOS 27 with a little help from Google's Gemini would represent more than an update – it would signal Apple's readiness to make AI the primary interface layer across its ecosystem."

The update arrives as Apple addresses longstanding criticisms of Siri's capabilities. The company has repeatedly postponed previous versions of the assistant, first announced in 2024. In May, Apple settled a 250 million dollar class-action lawsuit alleging the company falsely advertised Siri's AI capabilities, with some consumers receiving payouts up to 95 dollars.

Craig Federighi, Apple's senior vice-president of software engineering, acknowledged the company's previous hesitation regarding AI, stating that some competitors appear "to be racing forward, seemingly pursuing AI for the sake of AI, without clear regard for the people that it ultimately meant to serve."

The launch of Siri AI represents a critical moment for Apple as it positions itself in the increasingly competitive artificial intelligence market while maintaining its emphasis on privacy protections and on-device processing for many requests.

Supreme Court declines to hear gun industry challenge to New York liability law

The Supreme Court declined on Monday to hear a challenge from gun manufacturers and industry groups to a New York law that permits lawsuits against firearms makers and dealers for harms resulting from the criminal or unlawful misuse of their products. The National Shooting Sports Foundation, along with Smith & Wesson and Ruger, sought to appeal a lower court decision that uphe...

Yesterday, 11:19 PM ET

The Supreme Court declined on Monday to hear a challenge from gun manufacturers and industry groups to a New York law that permits lawsuits against firearms makers and dealers for harms resulting from the criminal or unlawful misuse of their products.

The National Shooting Sports Foundation, along with Smith & Wesson and Ruger, sought to appeal a lower court decision that upheld the state law. The Supreme Court's decision to reject the case leaves that ruling in place and allows New York to continue enforcing the liability measure.

Gun rights groups had argued the statute unfairly exposes manufacturers to liability for actions they cannot control. Supporters of the law contend it provides an important avenue for victims of gun violence to seek recourse from an industry they believe has failed to prevent firearms from reaching criminals.

The decision does not establish a national precedent but rather preserves New York's authority to enforce its own law. The choice to decline the appeal comes as states continue to grapple with gun violence and debate how much responsibility manufacturers and dealers should bear for how their products are used after sale.

New York enacted the law as part of broader efforts to address gun violence through both criminal and civil enforcement mechanisms. Advocates argue that holding the firearms industry accountable encourages manufacturers to take additional steps to prevent their products from being used in crimes.

Gun industry groups contend that such laws unfairly shift responsibility from those who actually commit crimes to manufacturers and sellers who have no control over how consumers ultimately use firearms. They argue that existing federal law shields gun makers from liability in certain circumstances, and that state laws attempting to circumvent this protection overreach their authority.

The Supreme Court's rejection of the case does not include any written explanation or comment, which is typical when the court declines to hear a case. This procedural decision means the justices did not find sufficient reason to review the lower court's judgment.

Other states have watched this legal battle closely as they consider their own approaches to holding the gun industry accountable. The outcome in New York may influence whether additional states attempt to pass similar legislation allowing civil litigation against firearms manufacturers and dealers.

Gun industry groups are likely to continue challenging such measures in lower courts and may pursue other legal avenues to block state laws they view as overreaching. Meanwhile, advocates for gun violence prevention point to the Supreme Court's decision as a victory for the ability of states to craft their own policies addressing firearms liability.

The ruling reflects ongoing national divisions over gun rights and gun control, with Americans holding sharply different views on whether manufacturers bear responsibility for how their products are misused and what role courts should play in holding them accountable.
