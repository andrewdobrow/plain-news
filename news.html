Oil prices have spiked, prompting industry observers to discuss the possibility of demand destruction, a term describing a sustained drop in demand for a commodity caused by high prices. The concept, which has been used in energy markets for decades, signals a shift in consumption patterns when prices reach levels that force consumers and businesses to reduce their usage or seek alternatives.

Recent price increases have been driven by geopolitical tensions in the Middle East. A US-Iran ceasefire initially sent crude oil prices down, but renewed military escalation between Israel and Lebanon has cast doubt on whether supplies will resume flowing normally. Market uncertainty over the potential closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical shipping route, has pushed prices back up as traders worry about future supply constraints.

The impact on fuel costs has been immediate and severe. In Australia, diesel prices have climbed to record highs of nearly 330 cents per litre in some areas, with prices rising as much as 20 cents per litre in just two days. Petrol prices have also faced upward pressure, though they fell slightly in some capitals this week. These wholesale price increases have outpaced government fuel tax relief measures.

The Australian government has taken action to address supply concerns by helping domestic refineries bid for oil shipments on the open market. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese is traveling to Singapore to lock in ongoing fuel supplies from Australia's largest source of refined fuels. Energy Minister Chris Bowen confirmed that contracted shipments remain secure until at least mid-May and indicated that new supplies could come from multiple sources including the United States, Mexico, Singapore, Korea, and Malaysia.

Higher fuel costs create ripple effects throughout the economy. Industries dependent on petroleum products, including transportation and manufacturing, face increased operating costs that can squeeze profit margins or force price increases for consumers. Treasury officials have highlighted how the fuel crisis underscores the need for transitioning to renewable energy sources and developing more resilient supply chains.

The question now facing markets is whether current price levels will trigger demand destruction similar to previous oil shocks. When prices reached record levels in 2008, global oil consumption declined as consumers drove less and businesses reduced energy use. Whether that pattern repeats depends on several factors, including inflation rates, wage growth, energy policy decisions, and the availability of alternative fuels.

Australian officials have warned that even if the Middle East ceasefire holds and the Strait of Hormuz reopens, the effects will persist for months. Disruption to shipping transit and storage facilities in the Gulf means global supply will take considerable time to return to normal levels. Until then, consumers and businesses worldwide face uncertainty about whether they can absorb higher fuel costs or will be forced to reduce consumption.

5:00 AM ET

Bumblebees can solve problems spontaneously despite their tiny brains, according to new research that demonstrates their remarkably advanced cognitive abilities. The study shows the insects performing a task previously documented only in large-brained animals like chimpanzees, elephants, and crows.

Researchers gave bumblebees an adapted version of a classic experiment from a century ago that first showed chimpanzees could retrieve an out-of-reach banana by stacking boxes. In the latest version, bees were tested on their ability to roll a polystyrene ball to a specific location and climb on top of it to access an artificial flower positioned on a low ceiling. The bees had never encountered this type of challenge before and received no training to perform it.

In the basic test, 75 percent of the bees successfully reached the flower. "This is essentially an insect version of the classic 'box-and-banana' problem," said Dr Olli Loukola, a behavioural ecologist at the University of Oulu, Finland, and senior author of the research. "What stands out about the result is that this kind of spontaneous problem solving is now demonstrated in an insect."

The findings challenge the long-held assumption that insects operate purely on instinct and mindless trial-and-error learning. To verify that the bees were genuinely solving the problem rather than simply enjoying rolling objects, scientists created increasingly complex versions of the challenge. In the final setup, bees explored two chambers before the test began, one containing the artificial flower. When scientists illuminated the chamber with red light to hide the blue flower from view and then introduced the ball, 23 out of 30 bees successfully positioned it beneath the flower's location based on memory alone.

"We are not claiming that bees think like humans," Loukola said. "But our findings show that miniature brains can generate flexible solutions to novel problems in ways we are only beginning to understand."

The research contributes to growing evidence that insect intelligence has been significantly underestimated. Previous studies have shown bees can count, recognize faces, and communicate through dance, but spontaneous tool use represents a notable leap in cognitive flexibility.

Prof Lars Chittka, a behavioural ecologist at Queen Mary University of London who was not involved in the research, emphasized the importance of these findings. "There's a general perception that intelligent behaviour requires big brains because we are big-brained and relatively intelligent among animals," Chittka said. "Bees are a model of how much intelligence you can squeeze into a small nervous system."

The study, published in the journal Science, raises fundamental questions about how intelligence evolved across different species and what minimal neural systems can achieve.

Scott Pelley, a veteran journalist who spent decades at CBS News, was terminated by the network following a heated confrontation with management over recent staff cuts at 60 Minutes, according to reporting from the Guardian.

The conflict centered on changes made to the long-running news program last week, when CBS News fired its executive producer, executive editor, and two correspondents, Cecilia Vega and Sharyn Alfonsi, without providing specific reasons for the terminations.

During a Monday morning meeting with Nick Bilton, the newly appointed executive editor, Pelley criticized Bari Weiss, the former opinion commentator who joined CBS News in October as editor-in-chief. According to the Guardian, Pelley said Weiss was "murdering 60 Minutes" and accused her of not caring about the organization. "She was brought in to kill it and is doing exactly that," he told the group.

After the meeting, Weiss informed Pelley that his behavior was inappropriate. Pelley subsequently told staff members he expected to be terminated. On Tuesday evening, Bilton sent Pelley a message stating he had been "terminated for cause effective immediately." In the message, Bilton pointed to Pelley's conduct during their meeting, writing that Pelley had "hijacked" the conversation to "disparage me, my qualifications, and my intentions with remarkable incivility and contempt."

Bilton then notified the 60 Minutes staff of Pelley's departure in an email, stating the two had "parted ways." He noted his attempts to have conversations with Pelley over the weekend and that afternoon, though Pelley "was not" willing to find common ground.

Pelley's firing comes as the program faces significant upheaval. Earlier this year, journalist Anderson Cooper announced he was leaving the show. The departure leaves 60 Minutes with only three full-time correspondents ahead of its 59th season launch: Lesley Stahl, Bill Whitaker, and Jon Wertheim. Norah O'Donnell contributes to the program as well.

In response to his termination, Pelley issued a public statement accusing CBS News executives of silencing employees and instructing him to "inject falsehoods and bias" into his reporting. He emphasized that 60 Minutes has remained America's top program "because our beloved audience finds integrity, quality, and humanity in our stories."

Rome Hartman, a longtime 60 Minutes producer who retired last year, praised Pelley as "among the all-time greats of CBS News." He criticized Weiss and Bilton for firing Pelley for pushing back during a staff meeting, noting the irony given Weiss's company the Free Press positions itself against cancel culture.

Pelley joined CBS News in 1989 and served as anchor and managing editor of the CBS Evening News from 2011 to 2017 before joining 60 Minutes as a correspondent.

Bumblebees can solve problems spontaneously despite their tiny brains, according to new research that demonstrates their remarkably advanced cognitive abilities. The study shows the insects performing a task previously documented only in large-brained animals like chimpanzees, elephants, and crows.

Researchers gave bumblebees an adapted version of a classic experiment from a century ago that first showed chimpanzees could retrieve an out-of-reach banana by stacking boxes. In the latest version, bees were tested on their ability to roll a polystyrene ball to a specific location and climb on top of it to access an artificial flower positioned on a low ceiling. The bees had never encountered this type of challenge before and received no training to perform it.

In the basic test, 75 percent of the bees successfully reached the flower. "This is essentially an insect version of the classic 'box-and-banana' problem," said Dr Olli Loukola, a behavioural ecologist at the University of Oulu, Finland, and senior author of the research. "What stands out about the result is that this kind of spontaneous problem solving is now demonstrated in an insect."

The findings challenge the long-held assumption that insects operate purely on instinct and mindless trial-and-error learning. To verify that the bees were genuinely solving the problem rather than simply enjoying rolling objects, scientists created increasingly complex versions of the challenge. In the final setup, bees explored two chambers before the test began, one containing the artificial flower. When scientists illuminated the chamber with red light to hide the blue flower from view and then introduced the ball, 23 out of 30 bees successfully positioned it beneath the flower's location based on memory alone.

"We are not claiming that bees think like humans," Loukola said. "But our findings show that miniature brains can generate flexible solutions to novel problems in ways we are only beginning to understand."

The research contributes to growing evidence that insect intelligence has been significantly underestimated. Previous studies have shown bees can count, recognize faces, and communicate through dance, but spontaneous tool use represents a notable leap in cognitive flexibility.

Prof Lars Chittka, a behavioural ecologist at Queen Mary University of London who was not involved in the research, emphasized the importance of these findings. "There's a general perception that intelligent behaviour requires big brains because we are big-brained and relatively intelligent among animals," Chittka said. "Bees are a model of how much intelligence you can squeeze into a small nervous system."

The study, published in the journal Science, raises fundamental questions about how intelligence evolved across different species and what minimal neural systems can achieve.

Pope Leo celebrated Mass before huge crowds in Madrid during a visit to Spain marked by a traditional flower-carpeted procession.

6:27 AM ET

More than a million people gathered near Madrid's Plaza de Cibeles for an outdoor Mass celebrated by Pope Leo. Crowds pressed against barriers, waving flags and shouting "Long live the pope" as the pontiff arrived in his white popemobile. Some attendees tossed flower petals during his arrival at the central plaza.

The event marked the Pope's first visit to an EU country outside Italy and was likely the largest gathering of his week-long visit to Spain. The Pope urged world leaders to stop dividing electorates and to respect every human being. The Mass came during Spain's first papal visit in 15 years.

Defense Secretary Hegseth criticizes European migration policy in D-Day speech

Speaking in Normandy on the 82nd anniversary of the allied operation, Hegseth attacked Europe over migration.

6:35 AM ET

U.S. Defense Secretary Hegseth delivered remarks in Normandy on the 82nd anniversary of D-Day, the Allied operation that liberated Nazi-occupied northwestern Europe. He used the occasion to criticize European migration policies, characterizing migration as a "beach invasion."

The speech drew attention for linking the historic military anniversary to contemporary migration debates in Europe.

New telescope survey to catalog millions of unseen solar system objects

A facility preparing to begin operations will search for objects in our solar system that have never been observed.

Yesterday, 5:00 PM ET

A new astronomical facility will begin a decade-long survey within months, aiming to discover millions of objects in the solar system that have never been seen. The project represents a comprehensive effort to catalog bodies orbiting in our cosmic neighborhood.

The survey will track objects ranging from small asteroids to distant bodies in the outer solar system. The facility's advanced capabilities will detect objects too faint or too distant for previous surveys, potentially revealing new classes of solar system bodies and improving understanding of how the planetary system formed and evolved.

A few minutes ago
