Trump orders return to steam catapults on aircraft carriers, raising costs and delays

6:00 AM ET

President Trump has directed the Pentagon to redesign Ford-class aircraft carriers to replace their electromagnetic launch systems with traditional steam catapults, a change he has advocated for since 2017. The shift could significantly increase construction costs and delay delivery schedules for new carriers already under way.

Trump signed a national security memorandum on Thursday instructing Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao to develop a plan for the conversion. The directive specifically targets the USS Doris Miller, a Ford-class carrier that is already two years behind schedule. Officials have 60 days to outline the required measures to replace both the Electromagnetic Aircraft Launch System and Advanced Weapons Elevators with steam and hydraulic alternatives.

The president has made his preference for steam catapults a consistent talking point for years. In a 2017 Time magazine interview, Trump described a conversation with a navy officer about the electromagnetic system. "It sounded bad to me. Digital," Trump said, before declaring that steam was superior and less expensive. He has retold variations of this story annually since then, including during campaign speeches, at CPAC, and in remarks to active-duty personnel after returning to office.

Trump recently restated his position at the Army War College in Pennsylvania, recounting a conversation with someone he called "the catapulter." According to Trump's account, the person said steam catapults were better because "we can fix it with a hammer and a blowtorch. When the electric goes bad, I have to send to MIT."

The proposed change faces significant resistance from industry and military leaders. General Atomics, which manufactures the electromagnetic launch system, warned that switching course would create "significant cost, schedule, and integration risks." The company noted that work on the new ship is nearly 50 percent complete, making a redesign particularly expensive and disruptive.

Senator Mark Kelly, an Arizona Democrat and former astronaut with extensive experience launching from aircraft carriers, criticized the decision. "I've launched off the front of aircraft carriers hundreds of times, have a Master's degree in aeronautical engineering, and am a test pilot and even I wouldn't suggest to the Navy how to engineer specific systems on its ships," Kelly wrote on social media.

The electromagnetic launch systems were originally designed to reduce maintenance requirements and increase sortie rates compared to older steam catapults. However, the technology has encountered technical challenges during initial deployment on newer carriers.

The timing of Trump's order complicates the Navy's broader shipbuilding strategy as the service works to expand its carrier fleet amid strategic competition with China. Defense officials have not yet provided cost estimates or timeline projections for implementing the mandated changes, though industry observers expect substantial impacts on both metrics.

Ukraine conducted one of its largest drone attacks on Moscow, deploying nearly 200 drones against the Russian capital in a strike that demonstrated the country's growing capacity to hit targets deep inside Russia. The primary target was the Kapotnya oil refinery in Moscow's southeastern district, a major energy facility that supplies approximately 40 percent of the capital's petrol and about half of its diesel fuel. Video footage showed a fuel tank detonating at the refinery, with flames engulfing one of Moscow's most important energy infrastructure sites.

The scale of the attack appears to have overwhelmed Russian air defenses, which are normally extensive in the capital. Videos circulating online showed Ukrainian drones penetrating Moscow's layered defense systems largely unchallenged. Ukraine appears to have employed a mix of conventional long-range strike drones alongside faster jet-powered missile drones, including the Bars hybrid drone-cruise missile system that was unveiled publicly in late 2024 and early 2025. These faster weapons pose greater challenges for Russian air defense systems to intercept.

Beyond the refinery, at least one residential building, an industrial facility and several private homes suffered damage, with Russian authorities reporting 17 injuries. The attack created visible impact across Moscow and surrounding areas, with residents in the town of Balashikha describing a "black rain" after the strike, sharing photographs of dark, oily residue covering cars, streets and buildings.

The psychological impact on Moscow residents proved significant. Russian social media was flooded with videos of residents reacting to the strikes. In one widely shared clip, a woman tearfully said, "The war is here. My windows are shaking. The air is dark and smells of smoke." The visible smoke over the capital marked a notable moment, as Moscow has largely been insulated from the conflict's direct consequences until now.

Ukrainian officials hope that targeting key energy infrastructure will bring the war's consequences closer to ordinary Russians. Previous attacks on energy infrastructure have caused fuel shortages in occupied territories, including Crimea where residents waited hours at petrol stations. The impact will depend on the extent of damage to the refinery and Russia's ability to redirect fuel supplies from elsewhere.

Meanwhile, Russian missile attacks killed 10 people in a village in the Kharkiv region of Ukraine. The strikes hit civilian areas, continuing the pattern of attacks on Ukrainian population centers throughout the conflict.

President Vladimir Putin, attending a summit in Kazan, had not publicly commented on the Moscow strike as of reporting time. Russian hardliners quickly demanded retaliation, with some urging consideration of nuclear weapons. The most likely Russian response involves renewed waves of large-scale missile and drone strikes against Ukrainian cities, which historically have resulted in significant civilian casualties.

US states sue Meta over child privacy practices on Instagram and Facebook

5:00 AM ET

More than half of the US states have launched an unprecedented lawsuit against Meta, accusing the company of deliberately designing addictive products that harm young people. The federal jury trial began in Oakland, California and is expected to last between six and eight weeks.

The case represents a coordinated legal effort by 29 states, with attorneys from California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey leading the proceedings. The lawsuit, originally filed in October 2023, claims that Meta knowingly created dangerous products for minors while misleading the public about their risks.

"Meta designed a dangerous product for young users, knew it to be dangerous, and then lied to children, families and the community about how dangerous it was," said California Attorney General Rob Bonta.

The 233-page complaint alleges that Meta collects data on children under 13 without parental consent, violating federal and state laws. State attorneys argue that Meta refuses to remove harmful features and prioritizes profit over child safety. They claim the company developed psychologically manipulative features including infinite scrolling, constant notifications, like buttons and image-altering filters designed to maximize user engagement.

According to internal Meta research presented in court, a 2019 survey of 2,500 teenagers found that "young people are acutely aware that Instagram can be bad for their mental health, yet are compelled to spend time on the app for fear of missing out on cultural and social trends."

The trial will include testimony from Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg, Instagram CEO Adam Mosseri and former Meta employee turned whistleblower Arturo Béjar. If found liable, Meta could face damages as high as 200 billion dollars, equivalent to the company's 2025 annual revenue. Beyond financial penalties, states are seeking structural changes to make the platforms safer for children.

Meta denies all allegations, stating that the claims are unsubstantiated and the financial demands vastly disproportionate. The company argues that the states offer no proof of misleading residents and claim that features like additional accounts somehow caused harm.

The federal trial follows recent legal victories against Meta. In February, Meta and YouTube lost a California case and were ordered to pay 6 million dollars to a young woman. Additionally, a New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay 567 million dollars in a similar child safety case, bringing the state's total recovery to 942 million dollars.

The lawsuit strategy mirrors the successful legal approach used against tobacco companies in the 1990s, which focused on the products' addictive qualities and manufacturers' knowledge of harm. Kentucky Attorney General Russell Coleman said, "We did it with the tobacco settlement in the 1990s. We did it with the companies behind the opioid crisis. We'll do it again with Meta."

US 30-year Treasury yields reach highest level since 2007

3:12 AM ET

Treasury yields on 30-year US government bonds have climbed to their highest level in nearly two decades as escalating tensions between the United States and Iran continue to destabilize global markets. The surge in long-term borrowing costs reflects investor concerns about geopolitical risk and its impact on the economy.

Rising yields mean the US government faces higher costs to finance its long-term debt. When yields increase, bond prices fall, and the government must offer higher interest rates to attract buyers. The 30-year yield has not reached this level since 2007, before the financial crisis.

Energy prices have driven much of the market upheaval. US gas prices this month are the highest ever recorded for August, with the national average at $4.06 per gallon as of Monday, according to AAA data. This represents a $1 increase from a year ago. In California and Hawaii, prices have reached approximately $5.50 per gallon.

The high energy costs stem from the US-Israel war with Iran that began at the end of February. The conflict has disrupted the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway through which a fifth of the world's oil passes. Brent crude, the global oil benchmark, reached $112 per barrel in March, the highest level since 2022. While prices have since declined to around $85 per barrel, they remain about 30 percent higher than a year ago.

Gas prices had dipped briefly after the US and Iran reached initial peace deals, but have climbed again as diplomatic efforts have stalled. On Monday, both countries missed their 60-day diplomatic deadline to end the war after failing to agree on Iran's nuclear program. President Trump has indicated he is not pressuring for quick resolution, stating "I have no time schedule. I'm not in a hurry."

Higher energy costs fuel inflation, which typically pushes bond yields higher as investors demand greater returns to compensate for reduced purchasing power. Treasury yields serve as benchmarks for borrowing costs across the entire economy, affecting mortgages, corporate debt, and consumer loans.

Global bond markets have experienced widespread selling pressure, with yields on government debt in multiple countries reaching multi-decade highs. Stock markets have also declined, reflecting heightened uncertainty about the economic outlook.

Over the past six months of war, Americans have paid an excess of $56.4 billion in elevated gas prices, or approximately $477 per household, according to the congressional Joint Economic Committee. Meanwhile, major oil companies have recorded substantial profits. Eight of the largest oil companies accumulated $90 billion in profits during the first full financial quarter after the war began in March through June, generating roughly $700,000 in profit every minute during that period.

The combination of rising yields and falling stock prices signals growing concern about economic consequences as the geopolitical situation remains unresolved.

Anthropic's Valuation Surge Reflects AI Market Dominance

Anthropic has become the world's most valuable startup following a funding round that valued the artificial intelligence company at $965 billion, surpassing rival OpenAI's $852 billion valuation. The company announced the $65 billion funding raise this week, marking a dramatic shift in the competitive AI landscape.

The valuation increase represents remarkable growth in a short timeframe. Anthropic was valued at just $380 billion in February, meaning the company's worth has more than doubled in less than a year. This surge reflects investor confidence in the company's business performance and market position.

A key driver of Anthropic's rapid ascent is its impressive revenue growth. According to the Wall Street Journal, the company's revenue expansion has been described as "mind-blowing," particularly due to strong demand for its coding tool among business clients. The company is poised to achieve its first profitable quarter in June, an unusual milestone for a young technology firm still scaling operations.

Anthropic's coding product, Claude Code, has proven significantly more popular with enterprise customers than OpenAI's comparable offering, Codex. Additionally, Claude Mythos, the company's cybersecurity AI tool, gained international attention in April after successfully identifying bugs in widely used software. OpenAI released a similar product weeks later but it garnered limited attention by comparison.

The competitive dynamics have shifted notably. Anthropic's CEO Dario Amodei previously worked at OpenAI, and his company's rapid success represents a reversal of fortune for ChatGPT's maker. The first-mover advantage that benefited OpenAI early in the AI boom has not translated into sustained market leadership.

Anthropic announced on Monday that it had confidentially filed for an initial public offering on the US stock market. The company provided minimal details in its announcement, offering no specific timeline for going public or information about share offerings. The filing preempts OpenAI, which is expected to file for its own IPO imminently.

The IPO filing caps what Anthropic has called a banner year for the company. Alongside the funding announcement, the filing represents another significant milestone in the company's expansion from a smaller player in the AI industry to a dominant force competing directly with established competitors.

The broader market context shows continued enthusiasm for AI companies. SpaceX and OpenAI are also expected to debut on the stock market this year, reflecting significant investor appetite for technology firms. The level of hype surrounding each company will likely influence its eventual valuation, creating competition not only in product development but also in investor perception and market positioning.
