6:00 AM ET

A federal judge this week lifted the final legal barrier preventing immigration officials from deporting individuals who lost temporary protected status. The action follows a Supreme Court decision in June that removed judicial review limitations on the program, clearing the way for enforcement actions.

With no remaining legal roadblocks in place, the Department of Homeland Security has begun deportation flights. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin stated this week that the agency is actively pursuing individuals who lost TPS protections. "These individuals can either self-deport or we'll arrest you and send you back. It's that simple," Mullin said.

The Trump administration has terminated TPS designations for multiple countries since taking office, leading the Supreme Court to authorize ending protections for Haiti and Syria despite both nations appearing on the State Department's "do not travel" advisory list. Over 300,000 Haitians have lost TPS protections overnight and face deportation unless they have applied for alternative immigration relief such as asylum or a green card.

TPS, enacted by Congress in 1990, protects individuals already in the United States when their home country experiences natural disasters, epidemics, armed conflict, or other extraordinary circumstances making return unsafe. The program provides work permits and has contributed an estimated 262 billion dollars to the U.S. economy since 2001, according to FWD.us. TPS holders have worked in essential industries including construction and healthcare.

Krish O'Mara Vignarajah, president and CEO of Global Refuge, criticized the contradiction between government travel warnings and deportation policies. "In a stunning contradiction, the administration advises Americans against all travel to Haiti and instructs those who go anyway to draft a will and submit DNA samples so their remains can be identified," Vignarajah said. "To then compel hundreds of thousands of Haitians into such dire circumstances is an unconscionable abdication of our moral responsibility."

Harriett Cecilia Joseph, a former TPS holder, expressed the impossible situation facing many Haitians. "It's not that we don't want to go back to Haiti. It's just the situation doesn't allow us to," Joseph said.

Removal orders for Haitians in immigration courts have increased 96 percent between March and June, according to the Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse. Immigration officials have begun placing ankle monitors on Haitians to track their movements, making apprehension easier.

Thousands of Syrians face similar risks following TPS termination. Additionally, a federal court allowed the government to end employment authorization for active TPS holders from El Salvador, Ukraine, and Sudan, threatening their livelihoods despite ongoing deportation protections.

Josiane Valsaint, a Haitian who held TPS, wrote that immigrants contribute significantly to the American workforce. "We work hard, we pay our taxes, we give back to our communities and we are a big part of the engine that keeps the United States workforce in many essential industries moving. We deserve to stay here, with our families and loved ones," Valsaint said.

Lebanese President Joseph Aoun took office with broad public support for reasserting government control over Hezbollah, the powerful armed organization that operates largely independent of state authority. However, his efforts to limit the militant group's military autonomy have stalled as fighting between Hezbollah and Israel continues to escalate.

The renewed conflict between Israel and Hezbollah erupted on March 2 and has persisted despite a ceasefire that began on April 17. Both sides have maintained near-daily exchanges of fire in the months following the truce. The ongoing violence has intensified calls within Lebanon for the government to constrain Hezbollah's independent military operations, which many blame for dragging the country back into armed conflict with its neighbor.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu announced a major escalation of military operations against Hezbollah. "I have ordered an even greater acceleration of our operations," Netanyahu said in a video statement. "We will intensify our blows, increase our firepower, and we will crush them." Israel accused Hezbollah of breaching the ceasefire and issued evacuation orders for ten villages in southern Lebanon.

In response, Hezbollah claimed responsibility for multiple attacks on Israeli military targets on Monday and Tuesday. The group said it launched at least four drone attacks on Shomera barracks, attacks on two additional barracks, and another strike on a military post in Misgav Am. Hezbollah stated these attacks were carried out "in response to the violation of the ceasefire" by Israel.

The escalating violence has prompted widespread displacement. An Agence France-Presse correspondent observed residents fleeing the southern suburbs of Beirut, a major Hezbollah stronghold. Israeli airstrikes targeted the Bekaa valley in eastern Lebanon and multiple towns in the south, including areas near the ancient city of Tyre.

The humanitarian toll has been severe. According to Lebanese authorities, Israeli strikes since early March have killed more than 3,100 people. The Israeli military reported that one soldier was killed in southern Lebanon, bringing the total number of Israeli soldiers killed since fighting began to 23, with one civilian contractor also dead.

Two far-right Israeli ministers called for expanding the military campaign. Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich urged putting "an end to the threat posed by Hezbollah's explosive drones," while National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir called for a "return to intensive warfare" and "taking control" of further territory.

Aoun's apparent inability to bring Hezbollah under government control suggests the Lebanese government lacks either the political will or practical capacity to challenge the well-armed organization that functions as a state within a state. The group's continued military autonomy and escalating conflict with Israel have undermined the president's central reform agenda.

Yesterday, 11:58 PM ET

The federal government finalized mandatory water cuts for California, Nevada and Arizona from the drought-stricken Colorado River. The new restrictions require the three states to reduce their water consumption from the river, which has faced declining levels due to prolonged drought conditions affecting the southwestern United States.

The cuts represent a significant reduction in water allocations for the three states, which rely heavily on the Colorado River for municipal water supplies, agriculture, and power generation. The river serves roughly 40 million people across seven states and Mexico. Arizona, California and Nevada will face the steepest reductions under the federal plan.

Colorado was spared from mandatory cuts under the new rules, which will govern water distribution for the next two years. The decision comes after years of negotiations among the states that depend on the Colorado River basin. Water levels in key reservoirs along the river have dropped substantially in recent decades, forcing federal officials to impose increasingly stringent conservation measures.

The Colorado River basin has experienced more than two decades of drought conditions, with climate patterns reducing snowpack and rainfall that feed the river system. Major reservoirs like Lake Mead and Lake Powell have seen water levels fall to historic lows, threatening both water supply and hydroelectric power generation for millions of residents across the region.

The new federal rules aim to stabilize reservoir levels and ensure the long-term sustainability of the river system. Officials have warned that without significant conservation efforts, the Colorado River could face a collapse that would devastate communities and economies throughout the Southwest. The mandatory cuts are part of a broader effort to manage declining water resources as the region adapts to changing climate conditions and growing population demands.

Yesterday, 8:08 PM ET

TikTok has agreed to pay $400 million to settle a lawsuit with the US Justice Department over allegations that the company violated the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act. The settlement represents one of the largest penalties ever imposed in a children's privacy case.

The lawsuit, filed in 2024, centered on accusations that TikTok and its parent company ByteDance collected vast amounts of data from millions of users under the age of 13. The Justice Department alleged that the platform gathered this personal information without notifying parents or obtaining their consent, as required by federal law.

Under the settlement terms, TikTok will pay the $400 million penalty to resolve the government's claims. The agreement addresses concerns about how the company manages privacy protections for its youngest users and handles their personal data. The size of the penalty reflects the scale of TikTok's user base and the government's emphasis on protecting children's digital privacy.

This settlement comes as TikTok continues to face regulatory scrutiny in the United States. The platform has more than 150 million American users and has become a cultural phenomenon, particularly among teenagers and young adults. The company has repeatedly emphasized its commitment to user safety and privacy protections but has faced persistent questions from lawmakers and regulators about its data security practices.

The $400 million figure places this settlement among the highest financial penalties ever levied in cases involving children's online privacy. Previous major settlements in this area have typically resulted in significantly smaller fines, making this agreement a notable enforcement action by federal authorities.

Meanwhile, regulatory concerns about TikTok's age verification practices have extended beyond the United States. The UK's online regulator, Ofcom, announced it is conducting a formal investigation into whether TikTok has adequately protected children from harmful content. The watchdog expressed particular concern about TikTok's method of inferring children's ages, suggesting the approach may have failed to correctly identify a significant proportion of young users.

Ofcom said compliance failures could result in fines of up to 18 million pounds or 10 percent of qualifying worldwide revenue, whichever is larger. Harmful content includes material about disordered eating, self-harm, suicide and pornography.

TikTok has said it requires users to enter a date of birth when creating an account and uses technology to check for indicators that someone may not meet minimum age requirements. The company stated it strictly enforces age-appropriate experiences and is confident it meets its obligations under the UK's Online Safety Act.

Ofcom's investigation reflects broader global concerns about how social media platforms verify user ages and protect minors from inappropriate content.

SpaceX launched its 100th orbital mission of 2026, marking a new milestone in commercial spaceflight operations. The company deployed 29 Starlink satellites and successfully landed its rocket on a ship at sea. The achievement represents a significant increase in launch cadence for the private aerospace manufacturer.

The milestone comes as SpaceX continues expanding its Starlink satellite internet constellation. As of August 19, the company pushed its Starlink network past 11,000 satellites in orbit. This single network now accounts for roughly two-thirds of all active satellites currently circling Earth.

SpaceX experienced at least one launch delay during recent operations, halting a Starlink launch countdown with just 28 seconds remaining before liftoff. The company routinely performs last-minute countdown holds as part of its safety protocols before clearing rockets for launch.

The rapid expansion of SpaceX's satellite network has drawn attention to the growing concentration of orbital infrastructure under single operators. Elon Musk highlighted the disparity between SpaceX and competitors, noting that Amazon has not yet reached 1,000 satellites in orbit despite its own planned constellation.

The 100 orbital missions milestone demonstrates SpaceX's dominant position in the commercial launch market. The company maintains this pace through reusable rocket technology and frequent launches from facilities in Florida and California. Each successful landing of a rocket booster allows SpaceX to refurbish and refly hardware, reducing costs and enabling higher launch frequencies than traditional expendable rocket systems.

Following Heroes, she appeared in Scream 4 as Kirby Reed, a high school senior and horror film enthusiast who becomes targeted by a serial killer. She later returned to the franchise in Scream VI, released in 2023, playing an FBI agent investigating new murders.

Her other prominent role came in Nashville, where she portrayed Juliette Barnes, an ambitious country singer navigating fame and personal struggles. Panettiere later revealed that she related to her character's battle with addiction and mental health issues. In 2022, she told People magazine that she had spent eight months in rehab. She explained that her troubles began at age 15 when she was given medication to appear energetic during red carpet interviews, saying "things kept getting out of control and, as I got older, the drugs and alcohol became something I almost couldn't live without."

Panettiere had previously discussed experiencing depression following the birth of her daughter in 2014 with Ukrainian heavyweight boxing champion Wladimir Klitschko. She later gave full custody of her daughter to him after becoming dependent on prescription pills. In her 2026 memoir, This Is Me: A Reckoning, she wrote about the shame of her addiction and her struggle with life during that period.

Born in Rockland County, New York, Panettiere began her career at just 11 months old in a commercial. She made her acting debut at age four in the daytime soap One Life to Live, later appearing in The Guiding Light. She also voiced a character in the Disney-Pixar film A Bug's Life and appeared in Remember the Titans and Ally McBeal.

Panettiere had begun reviving her career in 2023 after addressing her personal challenges. Her death has prompted reflection within the entertainment community about the pressures faced by child actors and the difficulties many have experienced throughout their careers in the public eye.
