The United States and Iran engaged in direct military strikes on Thursday in the most serious challenge yet to their month-long ceasefire agreement. The exchange raised questions about whether the fragile truce could survive further escalations.

The US military said it targeted Iranian military facilities after Iranian forces launched multiple missiles, drones and small boats at three American destroyers transiting the Strait of Hormuz. The Pentagon stated that no US assets were struck and that its forces eliminated the inbound threats while targeting Iranian missile and drone launch sites, command centers, and surveillance nodes.

Iran disputed the American account, accusing the US of violating the ceasefire by targeting two ships at the strait and conducting airstrikes on civilian areas along the Iranian coast, including the island of Qeshm, which is home to approximately 150,000 people. Iran's military said it responded by attacking US military vessels, claiming to have inflicted significant damage on them.

The United Arab Emirates reported intercepting Iranian missiles and drones hours after the strikes. Brent crude oil prices rose to around $101 per barrel following news of the attacks.

President Trump described the incident as a minor affair, calling it a "love tap" during an interview with ABC News. "They trifled with us today," Trump told reporters. "We blew them away." He insisted the ceasefire remained intact despite the strikes.

When asked about prospects for a broader negotiated settlement, Trump said a deal "might not happen, but it could happen any day." He claimed Iran wanted an agreement more than the United States did.

The confrontation comes as reports suggested the two countries may have been close to a temporary agreement to halt the conflict. A one-page memorandum was reportedly shared between Washington and Tehran through Pakistan. However, senior Iranian officials have recently rejected concessions, with some preferring to delay negotiations until closer to the November midterm elections in the US, when the Trump administration might face pressure to reach a settlement.

Regional diplomats warned that Iran could miscalculate. They suggested the current moment offered an opportunity to end the conflict and claim victory, which might become harder if large-scale fighting resumes. They also noted that if negotiations failed, Washington could unilaterally end the conflict and leave Iran facing severe economic sanctions.

The strikes occurred in and around the strategically vital Strait of Hormuz, through which a significant portion of global oil shipments pass. The US has been pressuring Iran to reopen the strait and has enforced a naval blockade of Iranian ports. Earlier this week, Trump sent warships to guide stranded tankers through the waterway in an operation he called "Project Freedom."

Neither government has announced casualties from the strikes.

Canada bans Texas cattle imports after screwworm outbreak

12:22 AM ET

Canada has banned imports of cattle from Texas following the discovery of a flesh-eating parasite in the state. The action came after a second infected calf was identified, prompting Texas to declare a state of disaster.

The New World screwworm fly, which had not been confirmed in the United States since 1966, was found in a calf in south Texas, roughly 50 miles from the Mexico border. The parasite's larvae feed on the blood and flesh of warm-blooded animals, causing severe damage and death if untreated. Female screwworm flies lay eggs in open wounds on livestock, and the larvae quickly consume blood and tissue once they hatch.

For more than a year, the US Department of Agriculture and Texas officials have warned livestock owners about the parasite advancing through Mexico. Despite efforts to contain it, including the release of billions of sterile flies, the screwworm has spread over 1,100 miles from southern Mexico into Texas.

Texas Agriculture Commissioner Sid Miller criticized the federal response in a statement, saying the USDA had missed an important component of its containment strategy. He called for the Trump administration to increase deployment of the screwworm adult suppression system, or Swass, which uses attractants, bait, and targeted insecticides to reduce adult screwworm populations before sterile fly releases complete the eradication effort.

Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins defended her agency's response, stating there is no threat of mass infestation and no reason to believe the parasite will become established in the country. She noted that the Texas case was the only confirmed detection in the state so far.

The screwworm was eradicated from the United States in the 1970s after causing tens of millions of dollars in losses. Officials are concerned about a resurgence at a time when beef prices have reached record highs. While the flies pose no direct threat to food safety, officials fear potential shortages if the parasite spreads among cattle and poultry populations.

In response to the outbreak, the USDA has partnered with the Army Corps of Engineers and private construction company Mortenson to build a new sterile fly production facility at Moore Airbase in Edinburg, Texas. This facility will help combat the spread of the parasite.

Texas is a major cattle-producing state, and the livestock industry represents a significant portion of its agricultural economy. The trade restrictions imposed by Canada and the outbreak itself pose potential economic losses for Texas ranchers. The state's disaster declaration allows for faster deployment of resources and personnel to affected areas as officials work to contain the infestation and determine how the parasite reentered the United States.

A few minutes ago

The NBA has interviewed Los Angeles Clippers star Kawhi Leonard and his business adviser Dennis Robertson as part of an investigation into allegations the team violated salary cap rules to compensate Leonard.

The probe was initiated following claims made by journalist Pablo Torre, who alleged that Clippers owner Steve Ballmer employed Leonard through a non-existent role at one of his companies to circumvent NBA salary cap restrictions. Torre's reporting, which cited legal documents, suggested the arrangement was designed to provide Leonard with additional compensation beyond his standard contract.

According to Torre's investigation, Ballmer partially funded a tree-planting company called Aspiration. The company then entered into a $28 million agreement with KL2 Aspire, LLC, a business owned by Leonard. Torre reported finding no evidence that Leonard performed any work for Aspiration. The contract between the two entities contained a clause that would allow Leonard to receive payment even if he did not work, and another clause voiding the deal if Leonard left the Clippers.

A former Aspiration employee told Torre the arrangement was designed to "circumvent the salary cap."

The Clippers and Ballmer have firmly denied the allegations. In a statement released to Torre, they said: "Neither Mr Ballmer nor the Clippers circumvented the salary cap or engaged in any misconduct related to Aspiration. Any contrary assertion is provably false."

This is not the first time investigators have examined Leonard's compensation with the Clippers. The NBA previously investigated Robertson in 2019 and determined at that time the team had not granted Leonard any impermissible benefits during the free agency period when he joined the club. The league indicated it would reopen that investigation if new evidence emerged.

Leonard, a six-time All-Star, signed with the Clippers in July 2019 after winning the NBA championship with the Toronto Raptors the previous season.

The NBA has not yet publicly commented on Torre's reporting. However, the league has established penalties for salary cap violations. Under the NBA collective bargaining agreement, the Clippers could face a fine of up to $4.5 million for a first offense if found to have attempted to circumvent the salary cap. The team could also lose a first-round draft pick as punishment.

The investigation represents a significant matter for the Clippers organization as the NBA examines whether the team engaged in competitive balance violations through improper compensation methods.

A few minutes ago

Anthony Head, the British actor best known for playing Rupert Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, has died at age 72. He passed away peacefully from complications related to pneumonia, surrounded by his family, according to a statement from his daughters Emily and Daisy Head.

"It has been, and forever will be, an honour and a privilege to be his daughters, and to have witnessed first-hand the impact both he and his work have had on so many," they said.

Head achieved international recognition for his role as the mentor and librarian to Sarah Michelle Gellar's character in Buffy the Vampire Slayer, which ran for seven seasons. Beyond this iconic part, his career encompassed a wide range of television, film, and stage work. He appeared in the BBC fantasy series Merlin and played a recurring character in the sketch comedy show Little Britain alongside David Walliams and Matt Lucas. More recently, he joined the cast of Ted Lasso as Rupert Mannion, a former football club owner, where he developed strong on-screen chemistry with co-creator Jason Sudeikis.

His film roles included appearances in The Iron Lady and The Inbetweeners Movie. Before achieving fame through television, Head built a respected career on the stage, performing in West End productions including the original 1988 production of Chess, where he played Freddie Trumper. He also starred as Dr. Frank-N-Furter in the 1990 West End production of The Rocky Horror Show.

Born in Camden, London, Head came from an artistic family. His father founded Verity Films as a documentary filmmaker, while his mother was an actor who appeared in the 1960s BBC crime drama Maigret. His brother also worked as an actor. Head initially trained for a career in performance, beginning his professional work on stage before transitioning to screen roles.

In the early 1980s, Head also sang backing vocals for the British pop group Red Box and was featured on their 1986 debut album The Circle and the Square. He became well-known in the UK and US for his appearances in Nescafé Gold Blend television advertisements, which depicted a slow-developing romance between two neighbours.

Following the end of Buffy the Vampire Slayer in 2003, Head continued working steadily across various productions. During his time on Buffy, he lived in Los Angeles while his partner Sarah and two young daughters remained in England, travelling home every three or four weeks when possible.

Sarah Michelle Gellar paid tribute to Head through Instagram, beginning with a quote from her Buffy character before writing, "Well I don't have it figured out and I'm not ok. But I know I'm the lucky one because I knew you."

His daughters emphasized that his legacy would continue through the shows he appeared in and the audiences who loved them.

Justice Department ends Trump anti-weaponization compensation fund

The announcement marks the formal end of a controversial program that faced swift legal and political opposition since its announcement last month. Two DOJ attorneys stated in their filing to the Eastern Dist...

Yesterday, 9:57 PM ET

The Trump administration's nearly $1.8 billion anti-weaponization fund is effectively dead, according to a Justice Department filing submitted to federal court on Friday. The announcement marks the formal end of a controversial program that faced swift legal and political opposition since its announcement last month.

Two DOJ attorneys stated in their filing to the Eastern District of Virginia that the compensation fund "will not continue." The department made the statement while asking the court to reject legal challenges to the program, including a lawsuit filed by Democracy Forward.

The fund was created as part of a settlement in a Trump lawsuit against the Internal Revenue Service over the leak of his tax returns. The administration established it to compensate individuals who claimed they were targeted by federal investigations or prosecutions for political reasons. Officials indicated that potential recipients could include pardoned January 6 rioters.

The program faced immediate and intense backlash. US District Judge Leonie Brinkema in Virginia temporarily blocked the administration from transferring money from the fund last Friday, before the DOJ's announcement of its discontinuation.

Even Republicans expressed serious concerns about the initiative. Senate Majority Leader John Thune told reporters, "I do think the best way to handle it is if the administration decides to shut the fund down themselves." Mike Pence, Trump's first-term vice president, called the possibility of compensating January 6 rioters "deeply offensive" and said the concern was "broadly held by most Republicans and most Americans."

Democrats promised aggressive opposition. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer called it "corruption in broad daylight" and a "$1.8bn Maga slush fund." He vowed that Democrats would force Senate Republicans to vote on measures to block payments and demand preserved records and hearings.

Several state governors proposed retaliatory measures. California's Gavin Newsom proposed a 100 percent income tax on any fund distributions to California residents. Similar proposals emerged from Illinois, New York, and Connecticut.

The fund drew criticism for its lack of transparency and oversight. The terms did not require disclosure of how much money would be paid to whom, raising concerns about potential misuse. Legal experts questioned the criteria for determining legitimate claims of political persecution versus standard law enforcement actions.

The compensation fund represented a signature Trump administration response to claims of political bias in federal law enforcement. Its termination suggests a shift in approach following the legal challenges and political pressure. The Justice Department has not announced any replacement program or alternative mechanism for addressing the concerns the fund was designed to address.

The Eastern District of Virginia has not yet ruled on the DOJ's request to dismiss the case, though the administration's statement that the fund will not continue likely moots the remaining legal claims for compensation under this specific program.
