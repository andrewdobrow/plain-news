A glacial collapse in the Himalayas triggered devastating floods across the Nepal-Tibet border region, killing at least 273 people with nearly 1,400 still missing. The disaster swept away entire villages, destroyed bridges and roads, and left communities on both sides of the border searching through debris for survivors.

Scientists have determined that a glacier collapse, rather than an earthquake as initially reported, caused the catastrophic flooding. The U.S. Geological Survey confirmed the seismic event registered as a magnitude 5.2 on the Richter scale and was actually a glacial collapse and debris flow. Satellite analysis showed a 0.2 square kilometer chunk of ice collapsed vertically about 1.2 kilometers, breaking off from a glacier at approximately 5,200 meters elevation and crashing onto the valley floor 1,200 meters below.

The collapse released a torrent of mud, water and ice into the Trishuli river, also known as the Bhotekoshi, which flows between China and Nepal. Water levels in the river rose as much as nine meters in just half an hour, according to experts at the Kathmandu-based International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development. The Lhende Khola river, a tributary of the Bhotekoshi, was particularly affected by the surge.

Officials reported that 403 people are missing in Nepal, including 341 foreign nationals from countries including India, the US, UK, Australia, South Korea and Malaysia. In Tibet, China's state broadcaster said at least three people were killed with 265 missing. Many of the missing are tourists and pilgrims traveling through the mountainous region popular with trekkers.

Satellite imagery revealed that unusually warm weather preceded the disaster. A geomorphologist at the University of Calgary noted that some glaciers in the valley had been covered in snow the day before but were mostly bare ice by the time of the collapse, suggesting significant melting occurred in the 24 hours prior to the event.

While a glacial collapse clearly triggered the flooding, an international group of glacier researchers said it was "unlikely to be the sole cause." Scientists are investigating whether construction in the area may have been a contributing factor, and whether the collapse involved the whole mountainside or was isolated to the glacier itself.

The disaster highlights growing concerns about glacier stability in the Himalayas, where the region is warming twice as fast as the global average. The United Nations reported in 2023 that Nepal's mountains lost close to one-third of their ice in just over 30 years. Between 2011 and 2020, Hindu Kush Himalayan glaciers disappeared 65 percent faster than in the previous decade.

Rescue operations have been complicated by difficult mountain terrain and extensive infrastructure damage. Search teams continue working through valleys and debris fields as authorities in both Nepal and China mobilize emergency response efforts.

5:41 AM ET

Major British retailers including Currys, B&Q, Amazon, Asda, Screwfix and Wickes are preparing to sell plug-in solar panels following government discussions about bringing the technology to UK consumers. The move aims to make renewable energy accessible to renters and people without roof space, helping households reduce energy bills.

Executives from these retailers met with Martin McCluskey, the minister for energy consumers, to discuss guidelines for selling what are known as "balcony solar panels." The panels represent a simpler alternative to traditional rooftop installations, allowing people living in flats or rented homes to generate their own electricity.

The panels work by sitting on balconies, terraces, sheds or other outdoor spaces. They plug directly into a standard three-prong power socket in a home's electrical circuit. When an appliance is switched on, it uses the solar power first before drawing electricity from the grid, thereby reducing overall energy consumption.

These systems typically have a capacity of around 800 watts, roughly one-fifth of the size of a conventional residential rooftop solar installation. While they cannot power a house independently, government research suggests a household could save between 70 and 110 pounds annually. Plug-in systems are expected to cost around 400 pounds, meaning households would recover their initial investment within four to seven years.

The technology is not new to Europe. About 1.5 million balconies in Germany already have DIY solar panels, known as Balkonkraftwerk or "balcony power plants." Similar uptake has occurred in Spain. The UK government first announced plans to introduce these panels last summer, expecting them to become available in shops within months.

McCluskey said plug-in panels could be "transformative for renters or those on lower incomes" and welcomed retailers' support for getting the technology into homes. He noted that the "easy-to-install tech can cut people's bills and help make the UK less reliant on global fossil fuel markets."

Graham Bell, chief executive of B&Q, stated the retailer was "working closely with government and suppliers to understand and help shape the guidance, ensuring any products we offer are safe, compliant and straightforward to install." B&Q already sells portable solar devices that charge batteries and aims to offer plug-in systems "as soon as possible."

The government's push for plug-in solar forms part of a broader renewable energy strategy. Plans include increasing UK solar capacity from 18 gigawatts currently to between 45 and 47 gigawatts by decade's end, enough to power roughly 12 million typical UK homes. Ministers are also exploring solar installations on warehouse rooftops, industrial buildings and large outdoor car park canopies to help achieve these targets.

Nvidia has agreed to buy Hugging Face, an open source artificial intelligence platform, for $12.9 billion, according to a report from The Information. The deal marks one of the largest acquisitions in the AI sector and would give Nvidia control over a popular platform used by developers to share and build AI models.

Hugging Face has become a central hub for the AI development community, hosting thousands of open source models and datasets. The platform allows researchers and companies to collaborate on machine learning projects and has gained significant traction as AI applications have expanded across industries. Multiple reports indicate the company has been fielding acquisition interest for deals worth at least $13 billion.

The acquisition comes as Nvidia reported strong financial results for the second quarter of fiscal 2027, with the company's stock jumping 7 percent following the earnings announcement. Nvidia has positioned itself as the dominant supplier of chips and infrastructure for AI training and deployment, with CEO Jensen Huang overseeing rapid growth in the sector. The purchase of Hugging Face would extend Nvidia's reach beyond hardware into the software and platform layer of AI development.

Industry analysts view the deal as Nvidia's effort to build a more complete ecosystem around its chips. By owning the platform where many developers share and test AI models, Nvidia could more tightly integrate its hardware offerings with the software tools that run on them. The acquisition would also give Nvidia access to Hugging Face's community of developers and researchers, potentially steering future AI development toward Nvidia's technology stack.

The transaction still requires regulatory approval and the completion of due diligence. Hugging Face was founded as an open source project and has maintained a reputation for making AI technology accessible to a broad range of users. How Nvidia manages the platform's open source culture while integrating it into a publicly traded hardware company will likely face scrutiny from the developer community. The deal is expected to close in the coming months, subject to standard closing conditions.

The company has become a central hub for open-source machine learning, hosting thousands of pre-trained models and datasets that developers use to build AI applications. The platform has attracted a large community of researchers and companies working on natural language processing and other AI technologies.

The deal would allow Nvidia to deepen its position in the AI industry beyond selling graphics processing units. The move would also represent a return to the cloud business for Nvidia, which has previously focused primarily on hardware sales to cloud providers and enterprises.

However, as competition intensifies in the chip market, the acquisition would give Nvidia a stronger foothold in the software and services layer of the AI stack.

The $12.9 billion price tag reflects the growing value investors place on AI infrastructure companies. Hugging Face had previously raised funding at lower valuations, but the explosion of interest in generative AI over the past two years has driven up the worth of companies positioned at key points in the AI development pipeline. Dolly Parton, one of the greatest singer-songwriters in country music history, has died at age 80 following a cancer diagnosis. Her nephew Brian Seaver announced her death on Instagram, saying "It is an honour, an honour that is mixed with absolute pride and great sadness."

Parton rose from poverty in rural Tennessee to become a global entertainment icon whose influence extended far beyond music. Born in Pittman Center and raised in the isolated Smoky Mountains hamlet of Locust Ridge, she transformed her humble beginnings into the foundation of her artistic vision. Songs like "Coat of Many Colors" and "My Tennessee Mountain Home" drew directly from her early years and resonated with audiences across generations.

Her career spanned more than six decades and encompassed music, film, business, and philanthropy. As a soprano with a distinctive vibrato and east Tennessee twang, her voice became one of the most recognizable in music. She achieved remarkable commercial success with hits including "Jolene," "I Will Always Love You," and "9 to 5." The last of these was written for the 1980 film in which she also starred.

Parton's business acumen matched her creative talents. When Elvis Presley wanted to record "I Will Always Love You" but demanded 50 percent of the publishing rights, she refused. That decision proved wise when Whitney Houston's rendition for "The Bodyguard" soundtrack in 1992 became one of the best-selling singles of all time, earning Parton substantial royalties.

Known for her wit and self-deprecating humor, Parton crafted a distinctive persona that balanced authenticity with showmanship. She famously said, "It takes a lot of money to look this cheap," capturing her approach to her signature glamorous style. At Glastonbury in 2014, she attracted what was reportedly the largest crowd in the festival's history, performing in a crystal-studded white suit.

Beyond entertainment, Parton became celebrated for her charitable work. She established her Imagination Library, a book-reading program launched in 1995 that distributed millions of free books to children internationally. She also invested in medical research, supported communities affected by wildfires and hurricanes, and contributed to COVID-19 vaccine development.

Her awards reflected her broad impact. She earned 10 Grammy Awards, 11 from the Academy of Country Music, and numerous other honors. She was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame and played a major role in bringing country music into the mainstream.

Tributes poured in from across music, film, and politics. Her goddaughter Miley Cyrus described her as an angel, while others acknowledged the depth of her influence on popular culture and her lasting legacy as both an artist and humanitarian.
