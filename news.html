Federal judge strikes down Trump administration's $100,000 H-1B visa fee

A federal judge has invalidated Donald Trump's $100,000 annual fee on H-1B visa applications, determining the charge constitutes an unlawful tax that violates federal administrative law and the Constitution. US District Judge Leo Sorokin in Boston issued the 42-page ruling in a lawsuit filed by 20 Democratic state attorneys general who challenged the fee Trump announced in Sep...

10:47 PM ET

A federal judge has invalidated Donald Trump's $100,000 annual fee on H-1B visa applications, determining the charge constitutes an unlawful tax that violates federal administrative law and the Constitution.

US District Judge Leo Sorokin in Boston issued the 42-page ruling in a lawsuit filed by 20 Democratic state attorneys general who challenged the fee Trump announced in September. The decision vacates the sweeping charge, which represented a 20-to-50 fold increase from existing application rates.

The H-1B visa program enables US companies to hire foreign workers for specialty occupations, particularly in technology, engineering, and healthcare sectors. The Trump administration had positioned the fee as a mechanism to prioritize American workers and generate revenue, but opponents argued it would severely restrict companies' ability to recruit international talent.

The judge's ruling determined that the administration lacked legal authority to implement such a substantial fee without congressional approval. Industry groups and technology companies that had challenged the fee welcomed the decision, noting it would have harmed innovation and competitiveness in sectors dependent on specialized international expertise.

Indian diaspora groups also welcomed the court's decision, as workers from India represent a significant portion of H-1B visa recipients. The ruling preserves the existing H-1B visa application process and fee structure. Companies that had been preparing for higher costs or reconsidering hiring plans can now proceed under the previous system.

The decision does not address other aspects of immigration policy or separate visa categories. The Trump administration is widely expected to appeal the ruling.

The H-1B program operates under an annual cap of 85,000 visas: 65,000 available for workers holding bachelor's degrees and 20,000 for those with advanced degrees from US institutions. Demand consistently exceeds available slots, with the government employing a lottery system to allocate visas among eligible applicants.

Previously, Trump had announced the fee through an executive order aimed at raising the cost of obtaining H-1B visas. The proposed charge would have applied to each H-1B visa application filed by employers, significantly increasing what had been several thousand dollars in existing application costs.

The ruling represents a setback for the administration's efforts to restrict access to temporary work visas for foreign nationals. Companies seeking to maintain their international workforce recruitment strategies can now resume operations without facing the proposed fee increase.

The case reflects ongoing debate about immigration policy and the balance between protecting American workers and maintaining the ability of US businesses to access specialized talent from abroad. The Trump administration has not announced a timeline for any potential appeal.

However, Netanyahu vowed to respond "with force" to any future Iranian attacks. "If that terrorist regime makes the mistake of attacking us again, we will respond with force," he said.

Iran's military leadership also declared it had halted operations but issued its own warnings. Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, Iran's parliamentary speaker and chief negotiator, said Tehran would not tolerate what it called "repeated violation." "So long as you lack a genuine willingness to build trust, Iran's response will remain the same," he posted on social media.

The ceasefire came after a weekend of intense escalation. Explosions were reported across multiple Iranian cities including Isfahan, Karaj, Tabriz, and Tehran. An Iranian missile fragment caused damage to homes in a West Bank settlement, though no injuries were reported.

Trump claimed credit for the pause, posting on social media that both countries wanted "an immediate CEASEFIRE" and that "final negotiations on 'Peace' are proceeding." He also said a US blockade of Iranian ports would remain until a final deal was reached. According to reports, Trump had warned Netanyahu that continued strikes would leave Israel acting without American support, allegedly telling the prime minister: "You better be careful, or you will be on your own very soon."

Analysts described the ceasefire as extremely fragile, with multiple flashpoints that could trigger fresh exchanges at any moment. Israel has rejected Iranian attempts to link a permanent ceasefire to Israel stopping its military operations against Hezbollah in Lebanon. Israel's defense minister said the country would continue operations against the militant group and strike Beirut if necessary. The standoff reflects Netanyahu's domestic political pressure to continue degrading Hezbollah's capabilities ahead of elections later this year.

The violence sent oil prices up five percent and shook financial markets, though stocks rose when the ceasefire was announced. The escalation also complicated Trump's broader diplomatic efforts to end the wider regional conflict and negotiate on Iran's nuclear program.

Both nations maintained military readiness despite the announced halt. Iranian officials said they were exchanging messages with Washington in an atmosphere of "extreme suspicion," while accusing Israel of attempting to sabotage diplomacy through its Lebanon operations.

The Pentagon has added three major Chinese companies to its official list of firms with alleged ties to China's military. The designation includes electric vehicle manufacturer BYD, e-commerce and cloud services platform Alibaba, and internet search and artificial intelligence company Baidu.

The updated list was released by the Department of Defense and serves as a cautionary signal to American businesses about potential risks in working with the flagged Chinese companies. While inclusion on the list does not automatically trigger sanctions or immediate penalties, it can create obstacles for these firms seeking to establish commercial partnerships or secure government contracts in the United States.

The Trump administration released this updated version of the list four months ago before unexpectedly removing it without providing public explanation.

The three companies represent some of China's most prominent technology and business enterprises with significant global operations. BYD has surpassed Tesla to become the world's largest electric vehicle manufacturer by sales volume. Alibaba operates one of the world's largest e-commerce platforms alongside major cloud computing services. Baidu dominates Chinese internet search and has made substantial investments in artificial intelligence development and autonomous vehicle technology.

The Pentagon's decision to add these consumer-facing companies marks a significant expansion of its scrutiny beyond traditional defense contractors. Previously listed companies have primarily included telecommunications equipment manufacturers and semiconductor firms. The inclusion of a major automaker and popular internet platforms reflects a broader interpretation of which Chinese commercial enterprises might support China's military modernization efforts.

US officials have expressed ongoing concerns that Chinese law may require commercial companies to share technology and data with China's military regardless of their stated operational independence. This intersection between civilian technology companies and military capabilities has become a central focus in US-China technology and trade competition.

The designation reflects growing American apprehension about the dual-use nature of advanced technology. Companies developing electric vehicles, cloud computing infrastructure, search algorithms, and artificial intelligence systems potentially possess capabilities relevant to military applications. The Pentagon's expanded list suggests officials view the boundary between civilian and military technology sectors as increasingly blurred in China.

The three companies have not yet publicly responded to their inclusion on the Pentagon list. Industry analysts note that the designation complicates these firms' international business prospects at a time when US-China relations remain strained across multiple domains including trade, technology, and security.

The listing comes as the United States continues assessing which Chinese technology companies pose strategic risks to American interests. The Pentagon maintains and regularly updates this list as part of its broader effort to monitor potential national security implications of Chinese technological advancement.

OpenAI has filed confidentially to go public on the US stock market, marking a major milestone for the artificial intelligence company. The filing comes just over a week after its primary competitor Anthropic made a similar announcement, intensifying the competition between the two firms.

The company submitted a confidential Form S-1 to the Securities and Exchange Commission, which allows OpenAI to keep its financial details private during the initial review process. In a blog post announcing the move, OpenAI stated: "We recently submitted a confidential S-1. We expect it to leak so we're just announcing it." The company added that it has not yet decided on a timeline for going public, noting that "it may be a while because there are things we want to do that are likely easier as a private company."

OpenAI's expected valuation exceeds $850 billion, positioning it as one of the most highly valued listings in market history. The company achieved this valuation in a funding round completed in March, even while facing challenges in profitability and missing certain revenue and user growth targets.

The path to this moment reflects OpenAI's rapid transformation since its founding in 2015 as a non-profit research organization. The company gained widespread attention after releasing ChatGPT in 2022 and subsequently converted to a for-profit structure. The chatbot quickly accumulated hundreds of millions of users and fundamentally changed how people work across sectors including entertainment, healthcare, education and employment.

OpenAI's pursuit of an IPO comes as the AI sector experiences a major funding wave. Beyond Anthropic's competing filing, SpaceX, which owns Elon Musk's AI company xAI, is also preparing to go public at an expected valuation of $1.75 trillion.

The filing follows a significant legal victory for OpenAI. Musk had sued the company, alleging it illegally converted from a non-profit to for-profit status and unjustly enriched itself and its founders. He sought to force the company to revert to non-profit status, which could have complicated its IPO plans. A jury panel ruled that the case fell outside the statute of limitations and found OpenAI not liable.

Despite the positive momentum, OpenAI faces ongoing legal challenges. The company has been sued in more than a dozen cases in which individuals claim that ChatGPT has worsened mental health crises and contributed to violent acts. These lawsuits could present obstacles as the company moves forward with its Wall Street debut.

Nevertheless, OpenAI has continued to strengthen its market position through major partnerships with companies including Microsoft, Google and Nvidia, as well as agreements with the Trump administration.

Actor Idris Elba has dismissed speculation about him playing James Bond, clarifying that he was never seriously in contention for the role after Daniel Craig's departure in 2021. The 53-year-old star, currently promoting his film Masters of the Universe, told British GQ that the conversation linking him to the iconic character was "never legit."

Elba explained his perspective on why a Black actor in the role would face challenges. "I've always felt that it's not a realistic thing," he said. "James Bond was written how he was written for a reason. But I was complimented by it. And also, I think, in realistic terms, some markets just don't go for that. Bond is big all over the world. And [audiences] won't [all] go for a black male, an African male, playing Bond. That's not what they like in their culture. Period."

The actor emphasized that he believes the character should remain faithful to Ian Fleming's original creation rather than evolving significantly. "Bond is so unrealistic, so a hint of reality is good, but let's not try and make it woke," he said. According to Elba, the franchise should prioritize its core appeal. "I think you've got to be pure to what it is: escapism. Don't try and answer the world's taste. Just be Bond."

These comments come as the James Bond franchise undergoes significant changes following Daniel Craig's final appearance as 007. Amazon MGM Studios is rebooting the series with director Denis Villeneuve and screenwriter Steven Knight. Veteran casting director Nina Gold recently joined the production team to oversee the search for the next Bond actor.

Several candidates are reportedly being considered for the role. Sources indicate that 26-year-old Tom Francis has already auditioned for the position, while Aaron Taylor-Johnson, Jacob Elordi, and Callum Turner are also apparently in contention.

The debate over Bond's casting reflects broader discussions within Hollywood about representation and diversity in major film franchises. Elba's comments acknowledge the genuine commercial considerations studios face when making casting decisions, particularly for a character with such a long-established cultural identity. The franchise has produced 25 films since 1962, making it one of cinema's most successful and enduring series.

While many have advocated for a more diverse interpretation of the character, others argue that Bond should remain consistent with how he was originally conceived in Fleming's novels. Elba's remarks suggest these competing perspectives will likely continue to shape the conversation as producers move forward with their casting process for what will be a pivotal moment in the franchise's history.

Congressional Republicans are urging President Trump to reconsider his nomination for intelligence director as lawmakers attempt to break a stalemate over renewing a critical surveillance law. The president has begun interviewing alternative candidates for the position, signaling the administration may be open to changing course.

The push to withdraw the current nominee stems from Republican concerns that the nomination has become an obstacle to advancing legislation that renews expired intelligence authorities. According to reporting, Republicans hope that swapping out the intelligence director nominee could create the political opening needed to secure votes for the spy law reauthorization.

The intelligence surveillance law at stake governs crucial intelligence gathering operations, and its lapse poses significant implications for national security programs. Republicans who lead relevant congressional committees have indicated they view the nominee withdrawal as a potential path forward to resolve both the personnel confirmation fight and the legislative stalemate.

Trump's initial choice for intelligence director has faced resistance within his own party, complicating efforts to advance both the nomination and the related legislation. The resistance appears substantial enough that some Republicans believe withdrawing the nominee represents the most practical way forward.

The intelligence community has stressed the importance of renewing the surveillance authority without interruption. Congressional leaders are working to find a resolution that satisfies concerns about the current nominee while ensuring the spy law does not lapse.

The exact timeline for resolving both issues remains unclear. However, lawmakers believe that installing a new intelligence director nominee could clear the way for securing bipartisan support on the spy law reauthorization. Such legislation typically requires broad backing due to its sensitive nature and significant civil liberties implications.

The situation reflects the delicate balance Republicans must maintain while managing multiple political priorities simultaneously. Securing confirmations for key positions while advancing major legislation requires careful navigation, particularly when the two goals become intertwined.

Republicans who chair the armed services committees have expressed concerns about other Trump administration moves, including potential troop withdrawals from Europe, demonstrating broader tensions over defense and foreign policy priorities. However, the immediate focus in Congress centers on finding a resolution to the intelligence director nomination that preserves support for the surveillance law reauthorization.

The stakes for the intelligence community are substantial, as the absence of renewed surveillance authorities would disrupt ongoing national security operations. Whether a nominee withdrawal ultimately produces the votes needed to advance the spy law remains to be seen, but Republican leaders appear confident that swapping out the intelligence director choice could break the current deadlock.
