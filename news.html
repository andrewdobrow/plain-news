2:46 PM ET

A flesh-eating parasite that infests livestock has spread beyond its initial Texas detection site, with authorities now confirming cases in both Texas and New Mexico. The New World screwworm fly, which had been eradicated from the United States decades ago, has reemerged after more than 50 years and now threatens the nation's cattle industry.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture confirmed three additional screwworm infections on Monday, including two more cases in Texas and one in New Mexico. The Texas cases involved a calf in La Salle county and a goat in Gillespie county. The initial cases were discovered in Zavala county, located southwest of San Antonio, where two infections were found approximately five miles apart.

The screwworm fly lays eggs in open wounds on animals, and the hatching larvae then feed on living tissue, causing a painful condition called screwworm myiasis. If left untreated, the infection can kill the host animal. The parasite affects cattle, livestock, and wildlife, though federal officials have confirmed that the U.S. food supply remains safe, as screwworms do not infest meat, fruits, vegetables or other food sources.

Texas Governor Greg Abbott issued a statewide disaster declaration on Friday, warning that it is likely to be "an extraordinarily challenging summer" for infected areas. Abbott said state officials were working with the federal government to slow the spread of the fly.

Authorities have launched an aggressive containment response. Officials are dispersing millions of sterile screwworm flies in the vicinity of the initial infections. These sterile male flies prevent wild female flies from reproducing, a method that has proven effective for controlling and eradicating the pest when combined with enhanced surveillance, movement restrictions, and prompt treatment of wounds.

The Texas Animal Health Commission noted that it had been actively preparing for a potential resurgence of the parasite for over two years. The director urged all animal owners and caretakers to remain vigilant for the presence of larvae in animal wounds and to immediately report any suspicions.

Animal movement restrictions are now in effect to prevent further spread of the pest. The USDA discovered the second infestation after testing a number of suspect cases, according to the agency's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service.

The parasite's arrival poses a significant economic threat to U.S. cattle ranchers. A widespread resurgence could lead to animal deaths as well as higher labor and treatment costs. The situation has already impacted border trade. The U.S. had closed its southern border to live cattle imports from Mexico for over a year as the disease moved northward, contributing to a contraction in the beef industry to a 75-year low. Additionally, Canada announced a temporary ban on livestock from Texas following the confirmed infections.

Airlines are already taking concrete action to manage the...

10:29 AM ET

Global airline profits are projected to drop by half this year as jet fuel costs have surged due to Middle East conflicts disrupting oil supplies. The International Air Transport Association reports that fuel expenses have climbed significantly over the past year, placing severe financial pressure on carriers worldwide.

Airlines are already taking concrete action to manage the crisis. Two million airline seats have been cut from May schedules as carriers redraw their operations in response to soaring jet fuel prices. About 13,000 fewer flights will operate globally next month, according to aviation analytics company Cirium, representing less than 2 percent of global aviation capacity. Istanbul and Munich have recorded the biggest drops in flights, with Turkish Airlines and Lufthansa making substantial cuts. Lufthansa has eliminated 20,000 short-haul flights operated by its CityLine subsidiary.

Budget airlines face particularly acute challenges from the fuel price spike. These carriers operate on thin profit margins and have less flexibility to absorb cost increases compared to larger legacy airlines. However, some budget operators have committed to maintaining full summer schedules. EasyJet and Wizz Air have pledged to operate their summer schedules despite pressure on the unhedged portion of their fuel bills. Most major short-haul airlines operating from the UK are well hedged on jet fuel, meaning they do not expect immediate cost increases.

The industry has limited options to hedge against sharp price movements, and the increases arrive at a time when many carriers were still recovering from previous economic disruptions. Fuel typically represents one of the largest operating expenses for airlines, often accounting for a quarter or more of total costs.

The UK government has introduced contingency measures to manage potential summer disruptions. The transport secretary agreed with the aviation industry on unusual measures including consolidating schedules on routes with multiple daily flights to the same destination. Airlines can now cancel flights with fewer seats sold without losing valuable operating rights for the following season. UK refineries have been asked to maximise jet fuel production under contingency planning, though ministers have resisted industry requests to cut taxes or reduce environmental rules.

International agencies have predicted that Europe faces jet fuel shortages if Middle East disruptions continue. Analysts at Goldman Sachs warned that the UK is particularly vulnerable as Europe's largest net importer of jet fuel, with low inventory levels and reduced domestic refining capacity. UK stocks could fall to critically low levels, potentially requiring rationing measures.

The projected profit decline will be felt across the airline industry, though impacts will vary based on individual carriers' fuel hedging strategies, route structures, and ability to pass costs to consumers through higher ticket prices. The combination of rising fuel costs and operational constraints raises questions about whether some carriers, particularly smaller operators, will sustain operations if fuel prices remain elevated.

A few minutes ago

House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries said he will not vote to renew the nation's warrantless surveillance powers unless President Trump removes Bill Pulte as acting Director of National Intelligence. Jeffries made the statement as Congress faces a deadline to reauthorize Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, which allows intelligence agencies to monitor communications of foreign targets without a warrant.

"Reversing the Bill Pulte appointment is a starting point, not an ending point," Jeffries said of the battle to renew Section 702. The Democratic leader's position adds a significant political obstacle to the reauthorization process, as the minority party's cooperation is typically needed for major intelligence legislation to pass.

The Section 702 program has been a source of ongoing debate between privacy advocates and national security officials. Intelligence agencies argue the tool is critical for monitoring foreign threats, while critics have raised concerns about its potential use to collect information on Americans without proper oversight. The program requires periodic reauthorization by Congress.

Pulte's appointment as acting DNI has drawn scrutiny from Democrats who question his qualifications for overseeing the nation's intelligence apparatus. The acting director position gives Pulte authority over multiple intelligence agencies and access to the nation's most sensitive classified information. Democratic concerns about his leadership appear to have crystallized around the FISA renewal debate.

The standoff sets up a potential clash between the White House and congressional Democrats over both personnel and surveillance policy. Without Democratic support, passage of FISA reauthorization could face significant hurdles in the House, where the minority party has leverage on national security matters that typically require bipartisan backing. The outcome will test whether Trump is willing to make changes to his intelligence leadership team to secure the surveillance authorities his administration seeks.
