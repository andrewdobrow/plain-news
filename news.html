The United States military launched retaliatory strikes against Iran on Tuesday evening following Iran's downing of an American Apache helicopter near the Strait of Hormuz. US Central Command forces began the strikes at 5 p.m. Eastern Time at President Trump's direction, describing them as a proportional response.

The Apache helicopter crashed off the coast of Oman early Tuesday morning while on patrol. Both crew members were rescued in an unprecedented operation using an unmanned drone boat called a Corsair, a 24-foot vessel. The military confirmed both aviators were safe and uninjured after spending roughly two hours in the water.

President Trump announced the incident on Truth Social, stating that Iran had shot down the helicopter. "There were two pilots involved, both are safe and uninjured. Nevertheless, the United States must, of necessity, respond to the attack," Trump wrote. The actual cause of the crash remained under investigation, though Trump attributed it to Iranian action.

The retaliatory strikes came as tensions escalated in the Middle East following recent exchanges between Iran and Israel. Iranian state television reported that Israeli attacks killed at least two members of the country's air defense units. The region has remained unstable since the US and Israel began striking Iran on February 28, which has disrupted global energy prices and raised the cost of food and other essentials worldwide.

Apache helicopters have been critical assets for American military operations in the region, particularly for enforcing a blockade on Iranian crude oil shipments designed to pressure Tehran into making a deal. The helicopters have also been used by the United Arab Emirates to shoot down Iranian drones.

Despite the military action, Trump indicated he remains optimistic about negotiations with Iran. Earlier Tuesday, before the helicopter incident, Trump expressed confidence that a deal could be reached within "two or three days," though he provided no details supporting this assessment.

Trump previously stated that while the US could conduct extensive bombing campaigns, doing so would be costly in both lives and time. "If we go and bomb, which we could do very easily if we want, and we spend another two or three weeks bombing, they'll have nothing left whatsoever. But you won't have the strait open for months," he said. "If we do the bombing, you know, a lot of people are going to be killed. Who wants to do that? I don't."

Iranian state media reported explosions in Hormozgan province, which borders the Strait of Hormuz, following the announcement of US strikes. Central Command has not disclosed the specific targets of the military action.

The situation highlights the delicate balance the Trump administration is attempting to maintain: responding forcefully enough to the loss of military assets while preserving space for continued diplomatic negotiations with Iran.

The House of Representatives passed legislation providing $70 billion in funding for immigration enforcement agencies over the next three years, ensuring that the Department of Homeland Security, Immigration and Customs Enforcement, and Customs and Border Protection remain funded through September 2029.

The bill, known as the Secure America Act, allocates $38 billion to ICE, $26 billion to Customs and Border Protection, and $5 billion to the Department of Homeland Security. House Republicans advanced the measure on Tuesday by a narrow margin, with the final vote coming down to a single lawmaker's decision.

Republican Tim Walberg of Michigan initially voted against the legislation, which would have resulted in a 213-213 tie and killed the measure. After being surrounded by House Republican leaders, Walberg changed his vote, allowing the bill to pass. The measure now heads to President Trump's desk for his signature.

The Senate had already passed the bill the previous week. In the House, debate over the measure advanced on a party-line vote of 213-211, reflecting the slim Republican majority. One Republican member, Tom Kean Jr., has been absent for months due to a health crisis, further narrowing the margin for passage.

Republicans have characterized the funding as essential support for Trump's immigration enforcement agenda. The extended funding period eliminates the need for annual congressional reauthorization of these agencies, giving immigration enforcement operations sustained resources without requiring regular legislative review.

Democrats have criticized the approach, arguing that Congress has surrendered a crucial oversight mechanism. By approving long-term funding without built-in review mechanisms, lawmakers have removed a traditional tool used to maintain accountability over executive branch agencies and their operations.

The bill sparked particular concern among Democrats regarding provisions related to compensation for January 6 rioters. Representative Bennie Thompson, the Mississippi Democrat who chaired the 2022 select committee investigation of the Capitol attack, noted that the bill does not include a provision that would have prevented the Department of Justice from paying Trump supporters convicted of attacking police officers during the riot up to $1.776 billion.

The legislation focuses exclusively on enforcement operations and does not include funding for immigration courts, legal services, or other components of the immigration system. Immigration advocacy groups have expressed concern about the scale of enforcement funding without corresponding investment in due process infrastructure.

The passage comes after weeks of debate among Republican lawmakers about the bill's scope and structure. The measure represents one of the largest single appropriations for immigration enforcement in recent history, cementing Trump's immigration priorities into law through multiple years of guaranteed funding.

The Social Security retirement trust fund will be depleted by late 2032 unless Congress takes action, according to new projections that moved the insolvency date forward by several years. The program, which supports roughly 68 million Americans, faces an accelerated shortfall that could result in automatic benefit cuts averaging 22 percent for all recipients.

The updated timeline represents a significant shift from previous estimates and reflects changing economic conditions and demographic trends. Social Security relies on payroll taxes to fund monthly benefits for retirees, disabled workers, and survivors of deceased workers. When the trust fund runs dry, the program will only be able to pay out what it collects in current taxes, forcing across-the-board reductions unless lawmakers intervene.

The 2032 deadline puts pressure on Congress to address the funding gap through some combination of tax increases, benefit adjustments, or other reforms. Options under consideration have included raising the retirement age, increasing the payroll tax cap, or adjusting cost-of-living calculations. Each approach faces political resistance, and previous attempts at reform have stalled despite bipartisan acknowledgment of the problem.

The accelerated timeline reflects broader concerns about the program's long-term sustainability as the baby boomer generation continues to retire and draw benefits. The worker-to-beneficiary ratio has declined as the population ages, putting additional strain on the system. Economic factors, including wage growth patterns and labor force participation rates, also influence the trust fund's trajectory.

The announcement underscores the urgency of legislative action to preserve benefits for current and future retirees. Financial planners have begun advising clients to consider the possibility of reduced benefits when making retirement plans. Advocacy groups representing seniors have called on Congress to prioritize a solution before the 2032 deadline, warning that waiting until the last moment could result in more drastic cuts or tax increases than would be necessary with earlier intervention.

NASA announced the four-person crew selected for its Artemis III mission, representing the next phase in the agency's effort to return astronauts to the lunar surface. The crew will launch in 2027 for a two-week mission to low Earth orbit designed to test critical systems before planned lunar landings.

The selected crew includes NASA astronaut and commander Randy Bresnik, European Space Agency pilot Luca Parmitano, and NASA mission specialists Frank Rubio and Andre Douglas. Parmitano, an Italian astronaut, will serve as pilot for the mission. One crew member is a University of Michigan alumnus.

It is important to note that the Artemis III crew will not land on the moon or travel to the lunar surface. Instead, they will remain in Earth orbit while testing lunar landers developed by private companies SpaceX and Blue Origin. This mission serves as a preparatory step for future moon landings rather than the landing mission itself.

NASA administrator Jared Isaacman described the crew selection as part of creating "Earth's first starfleet" and emphasized the significance of the next stages in NASA's moon return program. The Artemis III mission represents a continuation of the broader Artemis program, which began with the uncrewed Artemis I test flight and continued with the crewed Artemis II lunar flyby that recently completed a record-breaking journey around the moon.

The space agency has positioned Artemis as its pathway back to the lunar surface after a gap of more than 50 years since the final Apollo mission. NASA aims to land astronauts on the moon through the Artemis IV mission planned for 2028. When humans do return to the lunar surface, the missions will mark a historic milestone by including the first woman and first person of color to walk on the moon.

The Artemis program faces ongoing scrutiny regarding its ambitious timeline. Technical challenges and development delays have affected various components of the program, and questions persist about whether NASA can meet its target dates. Some reports suggest that crew members may face disappointment if the program experiences further delays that prevent them from achieving their mission objectives.

Beyond lunar exploration, NASA has positioned the Artemis program as a stepping stone toward eventual human missions to Mars. The program represents a significant expansion of human spaceflight ambitions and international collaboration, with participation from the European Space Agency alongside American space agencies and private companies.

The crew selection drew from NASA's experienced astronaut corps, with considerations given to training, experience, and specific mission requirements. The announcement reflects NASA's commitment to advancing human space exploration in the coming years.

Serena Williams made a successful competitive return to professional tennis at age 44, winning her first match in 1,375 days. Playing alongside Canadian teenager Victoria Mboko, Williams defeated third seeds Nicole Melichar-Martinez and Erin Routliffe 7-6 (7-2), 6-2 in the opening round of doubles at Queen's Club in London.

Williams last competed professionally in September 2022 when she stepped away from tennis following the US Open. The 23-time Grand Slam singles champion has described her return as focused on enjoyment rather than achievement. "I don't need to win. I've won more than most people have in their whole lives," Williams said. "For me it's not important. I don't have anything to prove, I don't have anything to lose and everything here is just a gain."

The match itself proved competitive, particularly in the opening set. With the score balanced at 5-5, 30-30, Williams fired down a 120mph service winner to help secure the hold. The unseeded pair controlled the tiebreak, winning 7-2, then dominated the second set to close out the match decisively.

Williams selected Mboko as her partner after being impressed by the world number 9 in singles. "She reminded me a lot of myself," Williams explained. "I remember seeing her play in Montreal. I was impressed with her attitude, and what I liked the most about her was that the next time she played she still kept winning."

For Mboko, who was only 10 years old when Williams won her last Grand Slam title, the partnership held special significance. "Growing up, Serena has always been my idol," said the Canadian. "Seeing someone on TV versus actually talking to them in person and having conversations, of course, it's very different. I think she's really nice and very personable, very relatable."

Williams has committed to competing in doubles in Berlin following the Queen's tournament, though her plans beyond that remain uncertain. When asked about returning to singles competition, she said: "I can't say yeah, I can't say no. Right now, no." She added that she would likely need additional training if she decides to play singles events.

The 44-year-old emphasized that her primary motivation for competing is personal rather than professional. "It's really about my kids getting to see me play," she said, referencing her daughters Olympia, aged eight, and two-year-old Adira. "It's summer, the kids aren't in school, so it's a perfect time to get out there, have fun and see what happens."

Williams and Mboko will advance to the second round of the grass-court event, which serves as key preparation for Wimbledon later this month.

4:18 PM ET

James Blood Ulmer, the innovative guitarist who merged avant-garde jazz with blues and funk into a distinctive sound, has died at 86. He died on June 3, according to a statement from his family, who described him as fearless in both music and spirit.

Born Willie James Ulmer in South Carolina in 1940, he began his music career playing in funk bands across Pittsburgh, Columbus, and Detroit, backing musicians including Jewel Bryner and Hank Marr. After settling in New York in the early 1970s, Ulmer fundamentally changed his approach to music. "I ain't never thought nobody could make no money playing free music," he later reflected. "So I always played structured blues, rhythm playing, dance music, or something like that. And I abandoned it! When I came to New York, it was like I just went totally another way."

In New York, Ulmer performed with Art Blakey, Joe Henderson, and Rashied Ali while studying under avant-garde pioneer Ornette Coleman. Coleman taught him his harmolodic theory, which rejected traditional keys and harmonics in favor of a freer approach to sound. This philosophy would shape Ulmer's entire career, influencing his instinctive, unbounded playing style even as he began writing songs.

Coleman co-produced Ulmer's debut album "Tales of Captain Black." His next release, "Are You Glad to Be in America?" on the British Rough Trade label, featured a spirited social commentary title track that became his signature song. Ulmer went on to support punk and rock bands including Public Image Ltd and Captain Beefheart, bringing an aggressive energy to these collaborations. When addressing crowds at these performances, he was direct: "I'd stand at the microphone and tell them to shut the fuck up. They had five minutes to get into it or get the fuck out!"

Collaborations with saxophonist Arthur Blythe on albums like "Lenox Avenue Breakdown" and "Illusions" led to Ulmer securing a record deal with Columbia. Between 1981 and 1983, he released three progressive albums: "Free Lancing," "Black Rock," and "Odyssey." Rolling Stone hailed him in "Free Lancing" as "the most original electric guitarist to emerge since the late Jimi Hendrix." These records paired virtuosic bluesy guitar work with tight funk arrangements and soulful vocals.

Ulmer also formed the Music Revelation Ensemble with saxophonist David Murray, bassist Amin Ali, and drummer Ronald Shannon Jackson, debuting with "No Wave" in 1980. He later founded another group, Phalanx, reconnecting with Ali.

Following his Columbia years, Ulmer continued recording prolifically through subsequent decades, gradually focusing more on blues than jazz. His 2001 album "Memphis Blood: The Sun Sessions" earned his only Grammy nomination, for best traditional blues album. He also appeared on recordings by Ry Cooder and hip-hop group the Roots, among others. Ulmer retired in 2024 after performing his final concert at Detroit's jazz festival.

Born Willie James Ulmer in South Carolina in 1940, he began his music career playing in funk bands across Pittsburgh, Columbus, and Detroit, backing musicians including Jewel Bryner and Hank Marr. After settling in New York in the early 1970s, Ulmer fundamentally changed his approach to music. "I ain't never thought nobody could make no money playing free music," he later reflected. "So I always played structured blues, rhythm playing, dance music, or something like that. And I abandoned it! When I came to New York, it was like I just went totally another way."

In New York, Ulmer performed with Art Blakey, Joe Henderson, and Rashied Ali while studying under avant-garde pioneer Ornette Coleman. Coleman taught him his harmolodic theory, which rejected traditional keys and harmonics in favor of a freer approach to sound. This philosophy would shape Ulmer's entire career, influencing his instinctive, unbounded playing style even as he began writing songs.

Coleman co-produced Ulmer's debut album "Tales of Captain Black." His next release, "Are You Glad to Be in America?" on the British Rough Trade label, featured a spirited social commentary title track that became his signature song. Ulmer went on to support punk and rock bands including Public Image Ltd and Captain Beefheart, bringing an aggressive energy to these collaborations. When addressing crowds at these performances, he was direct: "I'd stand at the microphone and tell them to shut the fuck up. They had five minutes to get into it or get the fuck out!"

Collaborations with saxophonist Arthur Blythe on albums like "Lenox Avenue Breakdown" and "Illusions" led to Ulmer securing a record deal with Columbia. Between 1981 and 1983, he released three progressive albums: "Free Lancing," "Black Rock," and "Odyssey." Rolling Stone hailed him in "Free Lancing" as "the most original electric guitarist to emerge since the late Jimi Hendrix." These records paired virtuosic bluesy guitar work with tight funk arrangements and soulful vocals.

Ulmer also formed the Music Revelation Ensemble with saxophonist David Murray, bassist Amin Ali, and drummer Ronald Shannon Jackson, debuting with "No Wave" in 1980. He later founded another group, Phalanx, reconnecting with Ali.

Following his Columbia years, Ulmer continued recording prolifically through subsequent decades, gradually focusing more on blues than jazz. His 2001 album "Memphis Blood: The Sun Sessions" earned his only Grammy nomination, for best traditional blues album. He also appeared on recordings by Ry Cooder and hip-hop group the Roots, among others. Ulmer retired in 2024 after performing his final concert at Detroit's jazz festival.

During a pivotal moment at 5-5, 30-30, Williams responded to fan support from the stands with a composed performance, firing down a 120mph service winner to secure the hold. The strong return demonstrated her enduring competitive edge on the grass courts where she has achieved considerable success throughout her career.
