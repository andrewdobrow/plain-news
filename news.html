House rejects extension of surveillance law set to expire

The controversy centers on President Trump's appointment of Bill Pulte as acting director of national intellige...

6:47 PM ET

The House of Representatives voted against extending a powerful surveillance law on Thursday, with the measure failing 218-198. The failed vote means Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act will expire Friday unless Congress acts before the deadline.

The controversy centers on President Trump's appointment of Bill Pulte as acting director of national intelligence. Democrats announced they would block the extension in protest of the appointment, arguing that Pulte lacks relevant national security experience. The position by law requires someone with "extensive" national security experience. Pulte previously headed the Federal Housing Finance Agency and is an heir to a home construction company fortune.

House Democratic leaders, including Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, released a statement criticizing the appointment. They noted that "the apparent motivation for his elevation is the demonstrated willingness of Bill Pulte to search government databases for alleged dirt on President Trump's chosen political enemies." Democrats said there could be a path forward for reauthorizing the law, but only "with meaningful reforms" rather than extending it without changes.

Section 702 allows intelligence agencies to monitor communications of foreign targets outside the United States without obtaining individual warrants. The program has been a key tool for national security operations since its creation following 9/11, but it has drawn criticism from privacy advocates and lawmakers concerned about potential abuse and the incidental collection of American communications.

Republicans attempted to pass the extension using a fast-track process requiring a two-thirds majority. They fell short of success with 19 Republican members joining most Democrats in opposition. Seven conservative Republicans who had voiced civil liberties concerns also voted against the bill.

The Senate previously failed to pass a three-year extension, with all Democrats except John Fetterman of Pennsylvania voting no over Pulte's appointment. Seven conservative Republicans with civil liberties concerns also voted against that measure.

However, the expiration will not completely halt surveillance operations. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court issued a year-long certification allowing Section 702 collection to continue through approximately March 2027. The statute contains a provision permitting collection to continue under that court order even if the law itself lapses.

The failed vote reflects ongoing tensions in Congress over balancing surveillance powers with civil liberties protections. Both progressive Democrats and libertarian-leaning Republicans have raised concerns about government surveillance scope and potential abuse.

Republican leaders on the Senate intelligence and judiciary committees sent a letter to Secretary of State Marco Rubio, asking the administration to prepare for a "potential significant gap in foreign intelligence collection" if the law is not reauthorized.

Trump suggests executive order could extend surveillance program after Congress fails to act

President Trump indicated Thursday that he may use executive power to extend Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act if Congress continues to fail in passing a legislative reauthorization of the surveillance program. "Congress wants me to do it," Trump said in the Oval Office when asked by The Hill whether an executive order could serve as a mechanism to exten...

5:37 PM ET

President Trump indicated Thursday that he may use executive power to extend Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act if Congress continues to fail in passing a legislative reauthorization of the surveillance program.

"Congress wants me to do it," Trump said in the Oval Office when asked by The Hill whether an executive order could serve as a mechanism to extend the program.

Section 702 grants intelligence agencies the authority to monitor communications of non-U.S. citizens located outside American borders without obtaining a warrant. The provision has generated ongoing debate between those who view it as essential national security infrastructure and civil liberties advocates concerned about privacy violations and potential for abuse. The law requires periodic congressional reauthorization to remain in effect.

Trump's suggestion that he might bypass the legislative process through executive action represents a significant escalation in the surveillance debate. Intelligence officials have consistently defended Section 702 as a critical tool for tracking foreign threats and protecting national security. However, civil rights organizations have long objected to the program's scope and the minimal oversight it receives.

The feasibility of using an executive order to extend the program remains unclear legally. Historically, Section 702's authority has derived from congressional legislation rather than presidential action. Legal experts have suggested that an executive order attempting to maintain the surveillance authority would likely face court challenges, as the Constitution typically grants Congress primary power over intelligence programs and their funding.

The impasse reflects a broader struggle within Congress over how to balance security interests with privacy protections. Lawmakers have been unable to reach consensus on whether to renew the program, modify its scope, or allow it to lapse. Trump's willingness to consider unilateral action suggests the administration views maintaining these surveillance capabilities as a priority.

The Trump administration has signaled concerns about national security across multiple fronts. Beyond the surveillance question, the White House has been engaged in various negotiations and policy decisions related to intelligence matters and foreign threats.

The situation presents Trump with a choice between two paths forward. He could pursue executive action to maintain the surveillance authority unilaterally, or he could continue efforts to encourage Congress toward a legislative compromise. The outcome could reshape the balance between executive and legislative power over intelligence operations.

Civil liberties groups, which have worked for years to restrict Section 702's scope, have warned against executive branch overreach on the program. Their position suggests that any executive order would generate significant opposition beyond Capitol Hill.

The coming weeks will reveal whether Congress can resolve its differences on the program's future or whether Trump will move forward with the executive order approach he indicated was under consideration.
