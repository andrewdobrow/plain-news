The House of Representatives failed to pass a short-term extension of a powerful surveillance law, clearing the way for a key intelligence authority to lapse. The measure fell short in a 198-218 vote on Thursday, with Democrats blocking the renewal of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) in protest of a controversial Trump administration appointment.

The dispute centers on Donald Trump's decision to name Bill Pulte as acting director of national intelligence. Pulte, a major Republican donor and head of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, has no extensive national security experience, according to House Democrats. The law explicitly requires the intelligence director to have "extensive" national security experience.

"Bill Pulte has no relevant national security experience. Consequently, his appointment is in defiance of the law," said House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries and other senior Democrats in a statement. They argued the appointment demonstrates Pulte's willingness to misuse government databases to target Trump's political opponents.

Section 702 of FISA, enacted after 9/11, allows U.S. intelligence agencies to intercept foreign communications without a court warrant. Republican leaders had been negotiating a compromise with Democrats and civil liberties-minded members of their own party to extend the law for three years. Those negotiations collapsed after the Pulte announcement.

"There is a path to reauthorizing FISA, but it will require enacting meaningful reforms," Democrats said, explaining their opposition to what they called "kicking the can further down the road."

Republicans attempted to pass the extension through a fast-track process requiring a two-thirds majority. They fell significantly short, with 19 Republican members joining most Democrats in opposition. The Senate had already rejected a similar extension attempt the previous week, with all Democrats except John Fetterman of Pennsylvania voting no, alongside seven conservative Republicans concerned about civil liberties implications.

However, the surveillance program will not completely shut down. The FISA court issued a year-long certification allowing Section 702 collection to continue through approximately March 2027, even if the law itself lapses. The statute contains provisions permitting collection to continue under that court order despite the law's expiration.

Still, the failure to extend FISA creates uncertainty about intelligence operations. Republican leaders of the Senate intelligence and judiciary committees sent a letter to Secretary of State Marco Rubio asking the administration to prepare for "a potential significant gap in foreign intelligence collection."

Lawmakers will need to resolve the situation when they return, either through retroactive authorization or new legislation that can gain bipartisan support. The outcome will determine how intelligence agencies conduct surveillance operations and their access to key investigative tools.

A few minutes ago

The European Central Bank raised its main deposit rate from 2% to 2.25%, marking its first rate increase since 2023. Financial markets anticipate two additional rises by next spring as the central bank confronts surging inflation linked to the ongoing war in Iran.

Eurozone consumer price inflation climbed to 3.2% in May 2026, up from 3% in April, well above the ECB's 2% target. The conflict in the Middle East has disrupted energy supplies and pushed oil prices above $90 per barrel, compared with roughly $70 before the war began. Energy costs are now feeding through to broader price pressures as manufacturers and retailers seek to maintain profit margins.

The ECB also raised its main refinancing rate to 2.4% from 2.15%. The decision represents a shift in strategy after the central bank held rates steady in hopes that the United States and Iran would reach a peace agreement. That outcome has not materialized, forcing policymakers to act.

ECB President Christine Lagarde acknowledged the uncertainty surrounding the conflict's economic impact. "The full implication of the war for medium-term inflation and growth will depend on the intensity and duration of the energy price shock, as well as the scale of its indirect and second-round effects," she said.

The move comes after criticism that the ECB delayed rate increases in 2022 following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, allowing inflation to become more entrenched. Officials had considered "looking through" the initial energy price spike from the Middle East conflict, but mounting evidence that higher oil and gas prices were already accelerating inflation prompted the change in approach.

The central bank trimmed its growth forecasts for the eurozone to 0.8% in 2026 and 1.2% in 2027, down from previous projections of 0.9% and 1.3%. "The risks to the growth outlook are to the downside, mainly owing to the war in the Middle East, which has added to the volatile global policy environment," Lagarde stated, warning that prolonged energy supply disruptions could push prices higher for longer than currently expected.

Mark Wall, chief European economist at Deutsche Bank, called this a significant moment. "Not only is this the first ECB hike since 2023, it is also the first hike by one of the major global central banks in response to the energy shock," he said. However, Wall cautioned that markets may be overestimating the rate-hiking cycle. He predicted one additional increase in September but not the multiple hikes markets were pricing in, given weakness in employment and growth.

The Bank of England is expected to hold rates at 3.75% at its next meeting, while the Federal Reserve is also anticipated to pause despite the highest inflation in the G7 at 4.2%.

The research provides concrete evidence of how climate change-induced weather events pose immediate threats to species already facing habitat loss and fragmentation....

11:02 AM ET

Four days of intense rainfall and landslides killed 7% of the world's rarest orangutan population, according to a new study examining the impact of extreme weather on the critically endangered Tapanuli orangutan. The research provides concrete evidence of how climate change-induced weather events pose immediate threats to species already facing habitat loss and fragmentation.

The study focused on the Tapanuli orangutan, of which only about 800 individuals remain. Researchers found that more than 1,000 millimeters of rain fell over four days in North Sumatra province in November 2025, killing an estimated 58 orangutans. This represents 11% of the local population and 7% of the entire species. Scientists used satellite imagery overlaid with ape density estimates to determine how Cyclone Senyar affected orangutans living in the West Block of the Batang Toru ecosystem.

The rainfall triggered catastrophic landslides that destroyed approximately 8,300 hectares, or 11.7%, of this critical forest habitat. According to the research, human-induced climate change caused by fossil fuel burning increased the rainfall intensity by up to 50%.

Tapanuli orangutans face multiple threats beyond extreme weather. The Batang Toru ecosystem where they live is already threatened by mining operations, palm oil plantations, and a large hydropower project. These pressures, combined with the sudden demographic shock from the extreme weather event, raise serious concerns about the species' survival.

"It is tragic to lose so many apes in this way. In landscapes where populations are small and fragmented, this type of weather or climate event can have population-level consequences. It is extremely worrying for the future of this ape," said Serge Wich, a primatologist at Liverpool John Moores University and study co-author.

Previous research suggests that annual losses of just 1% of the Tapanuli orangutan population would eventually lead to extinction. The loss of 58 individuals in a single event represents a devastating demographic shock that conservation programs struggle to address.

Conservation biologist Jatna Supriatna from Universitas Indonesia emphasized the urgency of action: "To prevent the first modern extinction of a great ape species, Indonesia must permanently protect the Batang Toru ecosystem, but our international partners must also meet their global commitments by providing immediate biodiversity-recovery financing."

In response, the Indonesian government has temporarily paused all major industrial activity in the Batang Toru area to allow scientists to investigate how best to secure the long-term survival of the species while assessing risks to human lives. Researchers involved in the study recommended an immediate moratorium on land-use activities that degrade remaining orangutan habitat and the expansion of protected areas to stabilize the population.

These pressures, combined with the sudden demographic shock from the extreme weather event, raise serious concerns about the species' survival.

Conservation biologist Jatna Supriatna from Universitas Indonesia emphasized the urgency of action: "To prevent the first modern extinction of a great ape species, Indonesia must permanently protect the Batang Toru ecosystem, but our international partners must also meet their global commitments by providing immediate biodiversity-recovery financing."

Speaker Mike Johnson said Thursday he would not take President Trump's threat to seize Kharg Island, Iran's main oil export hub, at face value. Johnson's comments framed the threat as strategic messaging rather than a concrete military plan, suggesting Trump was using it to communicate with regional adversaries.

The threat came after intensified strikes between the U.S. and Iran, escalating tensions in the Middle East. Trump had vowed to hit Iran "very hard" and later take control of its oil and gas sectors in response to the downing of an Army helicopter.

Extreme Rainfall Killed 7% of World's Rarest Orangutans

Four days of heavy rain linked to climate change caused significant deaths among critically endangered orangutan populations, according to a new study.

11:02 AM ET

Extreme rainfall and landslides in Indonesia's North Sumatra province killed 58 Tapanuli orangutans in November 2025, wiping out 7% of the entire species. More than 1,000mm of rain fell over four days, devastating the critically endangered population of roughly 800 individuals.

The disaster underscores how climate change-driven extreme weather threatens species already vulnerable to habitat loss. Scientists warn that such events push the world's rarest great ape closer to extinction.
