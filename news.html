Yesterday, 9:20 PM ET

The United States and Iran have signed a memorandum of understanding to end their conflict and reopen the Strait of Hormuz. President Trump signed the document remotely, with the agreement now in effect. The signing happened ahead of a scheduled ceremony in Switzerland on Friday, after discussions among mediating countries about implementing the deal sooner.

The 14-paragraph memo includes an end to fighting, a commitment that Iran will never develop nuclear weapons, and a $300 billion redevelopment package for Iran. The deal aims to stabilize global energy markets after two months of military action that severely disrupted energy production and transportation across the Middle East.

Trump defended the agreement during an hour-long press conference, though he appeared to lower expectations for success. He warned he could authorize military strikes against Iran again if nuclear negotiations fail. Critics argue that making concessions simply to return to the pre-war situation demonstrates the conflict itself was a costly mistake. Before the war and as it began, Trump had outlined highly ambitious goals including Iran's total surrender and complete dismantling of its nuclear program.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has sharply criticized the deal, calling it a strategic and political disaster. Netanyahu had promised the Israeli public total victory but now faces Trump's diplomatic settlement and repeated public criticism from the president, occurring four months before an Israeli election. Netanyahu stands alone internationally in his opposition. Even the United Arab Emirates, previously the most hawkish Gulf state, supports the diplomatic resolution. Republican allies in Washington and the media have also been reluctant to fully reject a deal carrying Trump's approval.

The immediate reopening of the Strait of Hormuz faces significant delays. An estimated 5,000 mines that Iran placed in the waterway during the conflict must be swept before normal shipping traffic can resume. Trump said the U.S. military had already begun searching for mines, but the operation will require considerable time.

The conflict has created widespread economic damage globally. About 20,000 seafarers and 2,000 ships have been stranded in the Gulf. Germany halved its 2026 growth forecast to 0.5 percent on Wednesday, while Greece announced 500 million euros in extra aid for households and farmers. Countries in Asia dependent on Gulf oil have suffered particularly severe shortages of fuel, fertilizer, and other raw materials.

Peace negotiations remain fragile. Iran's parliamentary speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf stated that reopening the strait would be impossible while the U.S. and Israel committed breaches of the ceasefire, including the U.S. naval blockade. He accused them of "hostage-taking of the world's economy." Iran's military seized two ships attempting to cross the strait on Wednesday, marking the first vessel seizures since the war began on February 28.

Yesterday, 11:09 PM ET

Eight people died when a B-52 Stratofortress bomber crashed at Edwards Air Force Base in the Mojave Desert, about 100 miles northeast of Los Angeles. The aircraft went down during a test flight on Monday with no survivors.

The bomber took off from the California base and made a sharp right turn followed by a near 180-degree turn before plummeting to another runway at a rate of about 5,056 feet per minute, nearly 10 times faster than a typical landing descent. The crash resulted in a fiery explosion that sent thick plumes of smoke across the base.

Those killed included Boeing employees, government workers, military personnel and civilian contractors. Their identities have been released by the Air Force. Among the victims was an engineer marking his wedding anniversary and an Iowa native. One victim was described by his wife as "a leader, he's a hero."

The aircraft was supporting a radar modernization program. In 2025, Boeing delivered a B-52 to Edwards equipped with a modernized radar system designed to keep the bomber operational through at least 2050, nearly a century after it first entered service.

Investigators believe a flight control malfunction likely caused the crash. According to aviation safety expert Jeff Guzzetti, who previously worked for the Federal Aviation Administration and National Transportation Safety Board, "I think it was definitely a controllability issue. Now, whether that was tied to an engine failure, a flight control failure, or some new testing device failure, I'm not sure."

Guzzetti suggested the controls could have been rigged incorrectly after maintenance, or the crash could have resulted from a catastrophic engine problem or failure of equipment being tested. The investigation into the accident could take up to six months to complete, officials said.

Crews worked to make the crash site safe for search and recovery teams after fires flared up overnight. The airfield remained closed on Tuesday.

Edwards Air Force Base has served as a testing and development facility for military aircraft since World War II. The 412th Test Wing runs the base and conducts developmental testing of all Air Force aircraft, weapons systems, software and components before the service purchases them and throughout their operational lifespans.

The crash marks one of several recent fatal military aviation accidents. In 2024, an instructor pilot died when an ejection seat activated while the aircraft remained on the ground in Texas. Two Air Force pilots were killed when a trainer jet crashed near an Alabama airport in 2021. An Air Force ROTC cadet died in a 2022 accident involving a Humvee during a training exercise in Idaho.

Federal Reserve holds rates steady as new chairman signals possible hikes ahead

The decision received unanimous support from the voting committee, but signaled a notable shift in the central bank's outlook on future policy. Stock markets initially declined following th...

12:55 AM ET

The Federal Reserve kept interest rates unchanged at its first meeting under new chairman Kevin Warsh, maintaining rates in a range of 3.5% to 3.75% where they have remained since December. The decision received unanimous support from the voting committee, but signaled a notable shift in the central bank's outlook on future policy.

Stock markets initially declined following the announcement, with the Dow dropping 500 points and the S&P 500 and Nasdaq each falling over 1.2%. The reaction reflected investor concerns about the Fed's new direction under Warsh's leadership.

The policy statement highlighted economic concerns, noting that "economic activity is expanding at a solid pace despite elevated uncertainty that owes, in part, to the conflict in the Middle East." Committee members acknowledged that inflation remains elevated at 4.2 percent, well above the Fed's 2 percent target, driven partly by sharp increases in energy prices from the Middle East conflict.

A significant change in expectations emerged from the Fed's latest projections. Nine of the voting members now predict at least one rate increase by year's end, a dramatic reversal from March when 12 of 19 officials projected rate cuts. Warsh notably did not contribute to these projections, making him the sole board member to abstain.

The policy statement itself was notably shorter than previous versions, reflecting Warsh's stated desire to tighten the Fed's public communications. In his first press conference as chairman, Warsh outlined plans for five new taskforces to overhaul the central bank's approach. These groups will examine monetary policy conduct, communications, the balance sheet, data, and productivity and jobs. One taskforce specifically will reassess how the Fed communicates with the public, including news conferences, projections, and meeting transcripts.

Warsh struck a different tone on inflation compared to his earlier reputation as an inflation hawk. He stated that the Fed's monetary policy "cannot have a very significant effect on particularly prices," noting that oil, eggs, and beef prices "do not have first-order consequences to what we're doing." However, he emphasized the Fed's role in preventing such price increases from spreading throughout the broader economy.

The situation presents challenges for the new chairman. Inflation has reached its highest level since 2023, while hourly earnings have dropped to 0.7 percent, suggesting wage gains have been eroded by price increases. Core inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy prices, has risen more modestly to 2.9 percent. The labor market remains relatively strong with unemployment at 4.3 percent.

A ceasefire announcement between the United States and Iran sent oil prices to three-month lows, though analysts expect energy prices will take months to fully normalize. Warsh begins his four-year term navigating these economic pressures while establishing the Fed's independence under his leadership.

Apple to raise iPhone prices due to memory chip shortage

Apple's chief executive Tim Cook has announced that the company plans to raise prices across its product lineup in response to escalating memory chip costs driven by artificial intelligence development. In recent remarks, Cook indicated that price increases are now unavoidable. He explained that the company has been attempting to absorb the substantial cost increases being pas...

Yesterday, 5:00 PM ET

Apple's chief executive Tim Cook has announced that the company plans to raise prices across its product lineup in response to escalating memory chip costs driven by artificial intelligence development.

In recent remarks, Cook indicated that price increases are now unavoidable. He explained that the company has been attempting to absorb the substantial cost increases being passed down by component suppliers, but the situation has become unsustainable. Rather than continuing to shield customers from these expenses by reducing profit margins, Apple has decided that price rises are necessary.

The memory chip shortage stems from unprecedented demand for high-performance computing components as technology companies race to incorporate artificial intelligence features into their devices. Apple's strategy to expand AI capabilities within its products, particularly in Siri, requires significantly more memory than previous generations. The upcoming iPhone 18 is expected to include 12 gigabytes of RAM to support these enhanced features, roughly double the amount found in many current models.

The shortage has created intense competition among technology manufacturers for limited supplies from major producers. Samsung and SK Hynix control much of the global memory chip production, and their output cannot meet current demand. Multiple smartphone makers are simultaneously rushing to add generative AI functionality to their devices, further straining available inventory.

Cook did not specify which products would be affected by the price increases or provide details about how much prices would rise. He also declined to announce when the increases would take effect. This marks a notable departure from Apple's historical approach. The company has traditionally maintained stable pricing even during periods of component shortages by absorbing the additional costs internally or adjusting profit margins downward.

Industry analysts have observed that memory chip supply constraints have deteriorated significantly over the past year as AI applications have driven unprecedented demand for high-performance components. The timing of Apple's announcement is significant as the company prepares to introduce new iPhone models later this year that will heavily feature AI capabilities to differentiate themselves from previous versions.

The price increases could affect Apple's competitive standing in the smartphone market, where the company already charges premium prices compared to most Android manufacturers. Consumers have grown accustomed to Apple's flagship devices commanding high prices, but further increases could push some buyers toward alternative options, particularly if competing manufacturers manage to offer advanced AI features without proportional price hikes.

Cook's comments represent one of the first major admissions from a technology leader that the artificial intelligence boom is creating genuine financial pressures on device manufacturers. The announcement signals that the cost of developing and implementing AI features will likely be passed on to consumers through higher prices rather than absorbed by companies seeking to maintain market share.
