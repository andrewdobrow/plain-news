A few minutes ago

The House of Representatives voted against extending a powerful surveillance law set to expire Friday, rejecting a short-term renewal measure in a 198-218 vote. The failed extension ensures that Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act will lapse, marking a significant development in ongoing debates about government surveillance powers.

The rejection came after Democrats announced they would block renewal of the law in protest of Trump's appointment of Bill Pulte as acting director of national intelligence. Pulte, a major Republican donor and head of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, has faced criticism for lacking relevant national security experience. House Democratic leaders including minority leader Hakeem Jeffries stated that Pulte's appointment violated legal requirements for the position and expressed concern about potential misuse of government databases.

Section 702 of FISA, enacted following the September 11 attacks, permits U.S. intelligence agencies to intercept foreign communications without court warrants. Republican leaders had attempted to extend the law for three years and sought bipartisan support for the measure. However, Democrats withdrew from those negotiations after Pulte's nomination was announced.

The extension attempt used a fast-track process requiring a two-thirds majority to pass. Republicans fell significantly short of success, with 19 members of their own party joining most Democrats in opposition. Some Republican opponents cited civil liberties concerns about the law's scope and impact on privacy rights. A Senate vote on extending FISA for three years similarly failed last week, with Democrats except John Fetterman of Pennsylvania voting against the measure.

Despite the law's expiration, surveillance operations will not immediately cease. The FISA court previously issued a year-long certification authorizing Section 702 collection through approximately March 2027. The statute contains a provision allowing collection to continue under that court order even after the law lapses. This means intelligence agencies can maintain certain surveillance activities during any gap in statutory authorization.

The development reflects broader tension between national security priorities and civil liberties protections. Lawmakers on both sides have expressed concerns about balancing government surveillance needs with individual privacy rights. Republican chairs of the Senate intelligence and judiciary committees sent a letter to Secretary of State Marco Rubio asking the administration to prepare for a potential significant gap in foreign intelligence collection.

The lapse will require intelligence agencies to adjust their operations and potentially rely on alternative legal frameworks for activities previously conducted under Section 702. The House rejection sets up potential negotiations for a longer-term solution, though the immediate expiration appears unavoidable given the current timeline and voting patterns in Congress.

NASA's Deep Space Network successfully supported the Artemis II mission despite operating near capacity limits, according to agency officials. The network, which provides communications and tracking for deep space missions, performed well during the crewed lunar flyby that took place in April.

The strain on the network stems from growing demand across multiple missions. Officials noted that some missions are consuming more bandwidth and resources than their initial documentation indicated. This discrepancy has put pressure on the aging infrastructure, which must support an expanding portfolio of lunar, Mars, and deep space exploration programs.

The Deep Space Network consists of three antenna complexes positioned around the globe in California, Spain, and Australia. This arrangement allows continuous communication with spacecraft as Earth rotates. The network has been a backbone of space exploration since the 1960s, but faces increasing demands from both NASA missions and international partners.

The Artemis II mission marked a significant achievement for the space program. The spacecraft carried astronauts around the moon in a test flight for NASA's Space Launch System rocket and Orion crew capsule. Reliable communication throughout the mission was essential for crew safety and mission success.

Looking ahead, NASA faces even greater communication demands. The agency has announced the Artemis III crew, scheduled to launch in 2027. This all-male crew of four includes Italian astronaut Luca Parmitano of the European Space Agency as pilot, along with American astronauts Randy Bresnik, Frank Rubio, and Andre Douglas. Bresnik serves as mission commander and is a former commander of the International Space Station.

Unlike Artemis II, the Artemis III mission will remain in lower Earth orbit rather than traveling to the moon. The crew will test docking procedures and life support systems for lunar landers from Blue Origin and SpaceX. These tests will prepare for Artemis IV in 2028, which is scheduled to achieve the first crewed lunar landing since 1972.

NASA has been working to upgrade the Deep Space Network to handle these future demands. The agency plans to add new antennas and improve existing equipment to support the Artemis program's long-term goals, including establishing a sustained presence on the Moon and eventual human missions to Mars.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman described the growing constellation of spacecraft needed for upcoming missions as "Earth's first starfleet." Multiple vehicles including Dragon, Soyuz, Starliner, Starship, and Blue Origin landers will eventually operate simultaneously in orbit.

The successful performance during Artemis II, despite the network operating near its limits, demonstrates both the capability of existing infrastructure and the urgent need for planned expansions to support NASA's ambitious exploration agenda.
