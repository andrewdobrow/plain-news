Oil prices rose sharply after the United States and Iran exchanged fire for a second consecutive day, raising concerns that the two nations could enter a prolonged military conflict. The price increases reflected market anxiety over potential disruptions to global energy supplies as tensions between Washington and Tehran intensified.

The military exchanges marked an escalation in hostilities between the two countries, with fears growing that further confrontation could threaten oil production and shipping routes in the Persian Gulf region. Energy markets responded quickly to the developments, with traders pricing in the risk of supply disruptions that could affect global oil availability.

The conflict has already begun affecting energy costs for American consumers and businesses. Higher oil prices typically translate into increased costs for gasoline, heating, and transportation, which can ripple through the broader economy. Companies dependent on energy-intensive operations may face pressure on profit margins if oil prices remain elevated.

The timing of the price surge comes as inflation concerns have already mounted in recent months. Rising energy costs could compound existing economic pressures, potentially affecting everything from manufacturing to consumer spending. Industries ranging from airlines to shipping companies closely monitor oil price movements, as fuel represents a significant portion of their operating expenses.

Market analysts are watching for signs of whether the conflict will expand or de-escalate in coming days. The duration and intensity of any military engagement will likely determine how sustained the impact on oil prices proves to be. Past conflicts in the Middle East have demonstrated that even temporary disruptions can cause lasting effects on energy markets, particularly when major oil-producing regions are involved or key shipping channels are threatened.

A federal judge has reopened Donald Trump's $10 billion lawsuit against the Internal Revenue Service, ruling that the settlement lacks sufficient detail and may represent "collusion" and "fraud on the court."

Miami Judge Kathleen Williams made the decision after receiving a motion from a bipartisan group of 35 former federal judges who questioned whether the settlement was legitimate. Trump had dropped the lawsuit last week, and acting Attorney General Todd Blanche announced the government was "forever barred" from auditing Trump family tax returns in exchange.

The case centers on a leak of Trump and his sons' personal and business tax returns by a former contractor. The settlement also created a controversial $1.8 billion fund to compensate people claiming they were victims of federal government targeting, commonly referred to as the "anti-weaponization" fund.

The former judges argued that Trump had used the IRS lawsuit to obtain "unlawful private benefits" for himself and his family while creating a fund that would distribute taxpayer money "without constitutional or congressional authority." They contended Trump had rushed the settlement to shield the deal from judicial oversight and "short-circuit" the court's ability to review its terms.

In her order, Williams said she wanted to investigate the circumstances of the settlement and determine who would benefit from it. She noted that she is "empowered to investigate serious misconduct" and ordered Trump's attorneys to respond by June 12 about whether the parties truly opposed each other in court and whether the case should be reopened because the court was "the victim of a fraud."

Williams also questioned the "forever" provision shielding the Trumps from IRS scrutiny, noting that only Blanche signed it.

The settlement has drawn widespread criticism. Many fear the anti-weaponization fund will primarily benefit Trump supporters convicted of crimes related to the January 6, 2021 Capitol riot, though the fund is not explicitly limited by political affiliation. Supporters and critics alike, including former Trump attorney Michael Cohen, have indicated they intend to apply for compensation.

Charles Littlejohn, an IRS consultant employee who leaked the Trump tax information, was sentenced to five years in prison in 2024. The leaked data showed Trump paid little or no income tax in several years after earning millions from his television show The Apprentice.

The anti-weaponization fund is facing multiple legal challenges. A federal judge in Virginia's eastern district has already issued a temporary block on its establishment and any disbursements. The controversy continues as courts examine whether the settlement represents legitimate judicial process or an improper use of executive power.

6:52 PM ET

Apple announced a major overhaul to Siri at its Worldwide Developers Conference, introducing a fundamentally redesigned voice assistant powered by generative AI technology. The new Siri, set for broad release in the fall, represents a significant departure from the question-and-answer tool users have known for years, instead resembling conversational AI chatbots like ChatGPT and Google Gemini.

Mike Rockwell, Apple's vice-president of Siri engineering, demonstrated the revamped assistant's capabilities on stage. The new Siri features its own dedicated app and can pull information from Apple's native app ecosystem across devices to assist with daily planning, finding information, and navigation. In a live demo, Rockwell showed how Siri could identify a beach location from a photo, retrieve a friend's address, and provide directions with a planned stop, all within the Siri interface rather than switching between multiple applications.

The updated assistant will handle tasks including proofreading, shopping, calendar management, and using the camera to extract information like nutrition details from food. Initially, the tool will only be available in English.

Apple's redesigned Siri is powered by a billion-dollar partnership with Google, utilizing Google's Gemini AI model at its core. This collaboration marks a significant shift for Apple's AI strategy after the company lagged behind competitors in rolling out artificial intelligence features. Craig Federighi, Apple's senior vice-president of software engineering, acknowledged this positioning during the conference, noting that "some appear to be racing forward, seemingly pursuing AI for the sake of AI, without clear regard for the people that it's ultimately meant to serve."

The company has repeatedly delayed Siri updates initially announced in 2024. In May, Apple agreed to pay 250 million dollars to settle a class-action lawsuit alleging it falsely advertised Siri's capabilities, with some consumers receiving payouts up to 95 dollars.

Beyond Siri, Apple announced extensive child safety features designed to give parents greater control over their children's device usage. New safeguards limit content visibility, restrict communication, and control app access. An easy-to-use setup assistant enables parents to customize what their child can view and gradually expand access as they age. Parents can also require permission before their child browses new websites and set daily time limits for specific apps based on their own preferences or recommendations from the American Academy of Pediatrics.

The company is integrating AI capabilities across its ecosystem, including Safari, messaging, the Home app, camera, and photos. These features were developed in collaboration with Google. According to Dipanjan Chatterjee, a principal analyst at Forrester, a "fundamentally re-architected Siri inside iOS 27 with a little help from Google's Gemini would signal Apple's readiness to make AI the primary interface layer across its ecosystem."

While member dues represent just one revenue stream for the DCCC, they constitute a significant source of funding for the Democratic congressional campaign organization. The dues system typically requires House Democrats in safe districts to contribute money to help fund campaigns in competitive races where the party hopes to gain seats.

The dues structure reflects a long-standing practice within both parties, where members from heavily partisan districts are expected to help finance competitive races in districts that could swing either way. This allows campaign committees to concentrate resources on the most winnable opportunities without having to rely entirely on outside fundraising.

The timing of the dues shortfall coincides with the broader debate over the DCCC's primary involvement. Progressive members have objected to what they view as the committee backing certain candidates over others in contested primaries. This conflict has created friction between the party establishment and more liberal members, adding another layer of complexity to the committee's fundraising efforts.

The 47 percent average payment rate suggests widespread reluctance or inability to pay among the Democratic membership. The fact that nearly 30 members have paid nothing indicates either a coordinated effort to withhold funds or a general breakdown in the party's ability to collect dues.

The DCCC faces a delicate situation. It needs the revenue from member dues to fund its operations and competitive races heading into the midterm elections. Yet pushing too hard to collect could further inflame tensions with members already upset about the committee's primary interference.

As House Democrats navigate these internal disputes, the fundraising challenges underscore broader organizational questions within the party.
