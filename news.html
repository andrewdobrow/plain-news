Airlines Face $100 Billion Fuel Cost Increase as Iran Conflict Drives Oil Prices Higher

The International Air Transport Association estimates that jet fuel prices will be 70 percent higher throughout 2026, forcing carriers to raise ticket prices to offset the financial...

3:27 PM ET

Global airlines are confronting an additional $100 billion in jet fuel costs this year as the ongoing conflict involving Iran continues to disrupt energy markets and drive oil prices sharply higher. The International Air Transport Association estimates that jet fuel prices will be 70 percent higher throughout 2026, forcing carriers to raise ticket prices to offset the financial impact.

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz in March has effectively cut off a critical global oil shipping route, stranding vast quantities of crude and creating severe supply constraints. This disruption has pushed fuel costs to levels not seen in years and represents a significant burden for an industry already operating on thin profit margins.

The fuel crisis has dramatically altered industry forecasts. Iata projects that collective airline profits worldwide will drop roughly in half to $23 billion, down from earlier projections. Some carriers may struggle to survive the shock caused by soaring fuel expenses. Willie Walsh, Iata's director general, stated that "high oil prices will inevitably mean higher ticket prices" and that "there's just no way to avoid that."

Airlines have already begun adjusting fares to recover costs. British Airways chief executive Sean Doyle explained that long-haul and business passengers will likely face the bulk of fare increases, while price-sensitive short-haul holiday flights may be among the last to rise. "If fuel goes up, fares have to go up," Doyle said.

Despite the financial pressure, industry research suggests that roughly half of passengers are prepared to spend substantially more on fares if prices track oil costs. Walsh noted this "bodes well" for a strong northern summer season. Industry data shows that more British and European travelers will fly within the continent than usual, with fewer venturing farther afield given continued uncertainty around Gulf hubs.

Walsh emphasized that while the situation is challenging and margins remain "wafer-thin," concerns about fuel shortages have subsided. He compared the current situation favorably to the Covid crisis, noting that the industry remains profitable and is still forecasting growth, with traffic up 2 percent. However, Walsh acknowledged the predicament facing many carriers, saying the fuel bill increase represents an "existential" threat for some airlines.

Airlines are attempting to manage the crisis through various measures, including optimizing flight routes, adjusting capacity, and negotiating fuel hedging contracts. However, these steps provide only partial relief from the scale of the increase.

The situation remains dependent on developments in the Middle East conflict and whether access to oil shipping routes can be restored. Until the underlying supply disruptions are resolved, the aviation industry expects elevated fuel costs to persist, continuing to pressure both airline profitability and consumer travel expenses.

NASA is collaborating with luxury fashion house Prada to develop specialized undergarments for astronauts on the Artemis IV moon mission scheduled for 2028. The partnership focuses on creating a Liquid Cooling and Ventilation Garment (LCVG) that astronauts will wear as a base layer beneath their spacesuits during lunar surface operations.

The LCVG represents Prada's contribution to the Axiom Extravehicular Mobility Unit (AxEMU) spacesuit project. This undergarment serves a critical function in managing astronaut body temperature during moon exploration, where surface conditions present extreme thermal challenges. The lunar environment experiences dramatic temperature swings, from intense heat in direct sunlight to severe cold in shadowed areas, requiring advanced thermal management systems.

Prada's involvement demonstrates the luxury brand's expansion into the space industry through technical engineering applications. The company has applied its materials science expertise to develop specialized fabrics and thermal regulation technology for the LCVG. This collaboration reflects a broader trend of luxury brands pursuing innovation beyond traditional fashion into specialized technical fields.

NASA has increasingly pursued partnerships with private sector companies to accelerate development of next-generation space equipment. The Artemis program represents a significant part of this strategy, with the agency leveraging commercial innovation to support its lunar exploration goals. The Artemis IV mission is part of the broader effort to establish sustained human presence on the moon.

Previous Apollo-era spacesuits used simpler thermal control systems that constrained the duration and scope of moonwalk activities. The new Prada-designed LCVG addresses these historical limitations by providing both cooling and insulation capabilities needed for extended surface operations. The garment's sophisticated temperature management system will allow astronauts greater flexibility during their time on the lunar surface.

The Prada-designed components will undergo extensive testing before deployment on Artemis IV. NASA continues to develop multiple aspects of its lunar exploration equipment through commercial partnerships, reflecting a strategic approach to space exploration that combines government expertise with private sector innovation.

The Artemis IV mission builds on earlier Artemis flights. Artemis II, currently facing scheduling adjustments due to technical issues with the Space Launch System rocket, will conduct a crewed lunar flyby in 2025. That mission will carry four astronauts, including Americans Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, and Christina Koch, along with Canadian astronaut Jeremy Hansen, on a 10-day trip around the moon that will set new distance records for human spaceflight.

Artemis III, planned for 2028, will achieve the first human moon landing since the Apollo program's final flight in 1972. The Artemis IV mission will follow, continuing NASA's goal of establishing long-term lunar operations. Through partnerships like the one with Prada, NASA aims to ensure that astronauts have access to cutting-edge equipment designed for the demands of deep-space exploration.

Alexander Zverev claimed his first Grand Slam title on Sunday, defeating Flavio Cobolli 6-1, 4-6, 6-4, 6-7 (5), 6-1 in a tense five-set final at the French Open. The second-seeded German dropped to his back and began crying after Cobolli missed an overhead on the second championship point.

The victory comes after three previous Grand Slam final losses. Zverev had won at nearly every other level of professional tennis, including Masters 1000 events, two ATP Finals titles, and an Olympic gold medal in Tokyo in 2020, but the sport's biggest prize had eluded him until now.

The tournament itself proved chaotic, with top seed Jannik Sinner and Novak Djokovic bouncing out in consecutive days, while Carlos Alcaraz remained absent due to injury. This left Zverev as the strongest player remaining in the draw.

Cobolli, ranked number 14 in the world, reached his first Grand Slam final without playing a semifinal match after his Italian compatriot Matteo Arnaldi withdrew due to illness. The 22-year-old felt the occasion intensely from the start, playing poorly in the opening set as Zverev dominated 6-1.

Cobolli regrouped in the second set, improving his forehand and using his drop shot effectively. Zverev's familiar vulnerabilities emerged, including problems with his forehand and second serve. The German made two double faults at 3-3 and committed unforced errors on break point, allowing Cobolli to level the match.

Zverev reasserted control in the third set, particularly through his serving. He broke Cobolli at 5-4 when the Italian sprayed three wild errors from 30-0 up. Yet as the finish line approached, Zverev's nerves took hold. He appeared overwhelmed throughout much of the fourth set, though Cobolli also struggled. In a tiebreak, Cobolli mounted a comeback from 3-1 down, winning with a running forehand winner to force a fifth set.

However, the Italian's energy reserves had been depleted. Cobolli completely faltered at the start of the fifth set, losing his opening service game and never recovering.

After the match, Zverev addressed his team, saying: "We've been through injuries. We've been through heartbreak. We've been through losses. We've been losers at times in the important moments. But at the end of the day, we're grand slam champions now and that's what counts."

Zverev also reflected on his personal connection to Roland Garros, mentioning both his greatest moments and his lowest point on the court. He noted overcoming a serious injury, adding: "I had the worst moment of my life on this court. I was laying in that corner over there with seven broken ligaments and two fractured bones. But now, finally, it's a happy end."

Two Virginia residents filed a federal lawsuit Saturday to stop a UFC event scheduled for the White House South Lawn. The fight card, called UFC Freedom 250, is being held as part of America's 250th anniversary celebration and coincides with President Trump's 80th birthday.

The plaintiffs are challenging the National Park Service and the Department of the Interior, the federal agencies overseeing the White House grounds. The lawsuit seeks to prevent the mixed martial arts spectacle from taking place at the presidential residence.

CBS Correspondent Scott Pelley Discusses Departure in First Extended Interview

The longtime CBS News journalist spoke publicly about being fired after 37 years with the network in an interview with The New York Times.

7:00 PM ET

Scott Pelley gave his first extended interview to The New York Times following his firing from CBS News after 37 years with the network. During his tenure, he held prominent roles including White House correspondent and correspondent for the newsmagazine 60 Minutes.

In a public statement, Pelley accused CBS's new executives of instructing him to "inject falsehoods and bias" into his reporting. He claimed the network's leadership silenced employees and gave politicians influence over interview content. Pelley emphasized that 60 Minutes' decades-long success as America's top program stemmed from the audience's trust in the show's integrity and quality.
