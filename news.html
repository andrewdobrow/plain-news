The United States and Iran have signed a memorandum of understanding to end their three-month war and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, according to U.S. officials. President Trump signed the agreement remotely, and it is now in effect. The signing occurred ahead of a scheduled ceremony planned for Friday in Switzerland.

The agreement concludes more than three months of conflict that began when the U.S. and Israel launched military operations against Iran. The war disrupted global shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway for international oil transport. About 20 percent of the world's oil and liquefied fossil gas passed through the strait during peacetime, making the closure devastating to global energy markets and supply chains.

According to a diplomat from a mediating country, discussions had been underway to move up the signing date. Both parties wanted to open the strait sooner than Friday. Political pressure on the White House to release the text of the memorandum may have also accelerated the timeline.

The conflict has created severe economic consequences worldwide. Germany halved its 2026 growth forecast to 0.5 percent, while Greece announced additional aid for households and farmers struggling with fuel costs. Countries in Asia dependent on Gulf oil have faced particularly acute shortages of fuel, fertilizer and raw materials.

The disruption to shipping has stranded approximately 20,000 seafarers and 2,000 ships in the Gulf, according to the International Maritime Organization. The head of the U.N. maritime agency has appealed for assistance for these seafarers.

The deal has received mixed reactions in Washington. Top House Democrats, including the ranking members of committees overseeing foreign affairs, defense, and intelligence policy, have demanded that Secretary of State Marco Rubio provide an immediate briefing. However, they welcomed the administration's shift toward diplomacy and its decision to end what they called a war of choice.

Robert Malley, who served as President Biden's envoy on Iran and negotiated the 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action, expressed support for the Trump administration's deal. He stated the memorandum is "far preferable to any of the alternatives" and that he does not see much value in other available options.

The agreement follows months of stalled peace negotiations. Pakistan had acted as mediator, preparing a luxury hotel in Islamabad for talks, but they never materialized. Iran had initially suggested that a wide gap remained between the sides regarding key terms.

Trump has insisted the deal ensures that Iran will never buy, develop or produce a nuclear weapon. However, the text of the agreement reportedly falls short of that comprehensive guarantee, leaving many key issues to be negotiated in future discussions.

Finland announced it has lifted its ban on nuclear weapons, a move the Nordic nation said would strengthen NATO as it confronts an uncertain security environment along its 830-mile border with Russia. The decision marks a significant shift in Finnish defense policy three years after the country joined the NATO alliance.

The policy change allows Finland to host nuclear weapons on its territory if NATO determines such deployment is necessary. Finland had maintained a long-standing prohibition on nuclear arms as part of its tradition of military non-alignment, which ended when it applied for NATO membership following Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2024. The country was formally admitted to the alliance in 2023.

Finnish officials framed the decision as a contribution to collective defense within NATO, which relies on nuclear deterrence as a core element of its security strategy. The announcement comes as European nations reassess their defense postures amid concerns about Russian aggression and questions about the reliability of U.S. security commitments under different administrations.

Finland shares one of the longest borders any NATO member has with Russia. The country fought two wars against the Soviet Union during World War II and maintained a careful policy of neutrality throughout the Cold War. Its decision to join NATO represented one of the most significant geopolitical shifts in Northern Europe in decades.

The timing of the announcement coincides with ongoing debates within NATO about burden-sharing and defense capabilities. Finland has already increased its defense spending and maintains one of Europe's largest artillery forces and a system of universal male conscription. The nuclear policy change adds another dimension to the alliance's strategic posture in Northern Europe.

A few minutes ago

The Federal Reserve kept interest rates unchanged at Kevin Warsh's first policy meeting as chair, maintaining the rate range at 3.5% to 3.75% where it has remained since December. The decision received unanimous support from the Fed's voting committee, but the announcement sparked a significant market decline, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average closing 500 points lower and the S&P 500 and Nasdaq each dropping over 1.2%.

The Fed's policy statement reflected a notably shorter format than previous announcements, part of Warsh's effort to overhaul the central bank's public communications. The committee acknowledged that "inflation remains elevated relative to the committee's 2% goal, in part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy."

New projections released alongside the decision showed a dramatic shift in Fed officials' expectations. Nine of the voting members now project at least one rate increase before the end of the year, representing a major reversal from March when 12 of 19 officials had predicted at least one rate cut. Notably, Warsh was the sole board member who did not contribute to the projections.

The inflation picture remains complicated. Energy prices spiked due to Middle East conflict, pushing overall inflation to 4.2%, the highest since 2023 and well above the Fed's 2% target. However, core inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy prices, rose only modestly to 2.9%. Meanwhile, the strong labor market continues to hold, with unemployment steady at 4.3%, though wage gains have been eroded by price increases as hourly earnings dropped to a seasonally adjusted 0.7%.

During his first press conference as chair, Warsh outlined plans to establish five new taskforces to examine the broad conduct of monetary policy, including communications, the Fed's balance sheet, data productivity, and jobs. He emphasized that these groups would "examine current practice, consider alternatives, and ultimately propose next steps for policy-maker consideration."

Warsh also struck a notable tone regarding inflation, arguing that monetary policy "cannot have a very significant effect on particularly prices." He stated that while energy and food prices deserve attention, the Fed's main responsibility is ensuring these price changes don't spread more broadly throughout the economy.

The policy decision comes amid geopolitical uncertainty and political pressure. While President Trump has continued advocating for lower rates, he recently stated he doesn't "want to have a big influence" on Warsh. The new chair appears positioned to receive more favorable treatment from the administration than his predecessor, Jerome Powell, who faced repeated presidential criticism over rate decisions.

5:00 PM ET

Apple plans to raise prices on some products as the company confronts a shortage of memory chips, CEO Tim Cook announced. The price increases are driven by surging demand for the advanced memory components needed to support artificial intelligence features across Apple's device lineup.

The shortage is affecting major chip suppliers Samsung and SK Hynix, who are struggling to keep pace with demand as technology companies race to integrate AI capabilities into consumer devices. The tech industry's push to build artificial intelligence infrastructure has led memory manufacturers to prioritize higher-margin datacenter chips over consumer products, tightening supplies for companies like Apple.

Cook stated in an interview that "price increases are unavoidable" as the company works to absorb the rising costs being passed along by suppliers. Apple is attempting to shield customers from the full extent of these increases, but some price adjustments appear unavoidable.

The memory crunch is part of a broader pattern affecting the technology sector. Sony recently announced a $100 price increase for its PlayStation 5 consoles in the United States, bringing the standard model to $649.99 from $549.99. The company cited rising costs of key components including memory chips as a primary reason for the adjustment. Microsoft also raised Xbox prices last year amid similar supply pressures.

Industry analysts have noted that geopolitical factors are compounding the supply challenges. Iran's attack on Qatar's natural gas export facility forced a shutdown that threatens helium supplies. Qatar produces about a third of the world's helium, and the disruption is expected to reduce helium exports by 14 percent. Helium is essential for manufacturing semiconductors used in computers and other technology devices.

The memory shortage reflects the intense competition among major technology companies to equip devices with artificial intelligence features. Where rivals have already released products with advanced language processing and machine learning capabilities, Apple is positioning itself to compete more aggressively in this space.

For Apple, the strategy includes expanding AI functionality to a broader range of devices rather than limiting advanced features to premium models. This approach requires more memory across the product lineup, intensifying pressure on already-constrained chip supplies.

The exact timing and scale of Apple's price increases have not been disclosed. Historically, Apple has maintained relatively stable pricing on its flagship products, making this announcement notable for how it signals shifting industry dynamics. The shortage and resulting price pressures are expected to have ripple effects across the consumer technology market as companies grapple with both component costs and evolving consumer demand for AI-powered devices.

Cape Verde goalkeeper to be reunited with mother before Uruguay match

Cape Verde goalkeeper Vozinha's mother has been granted a visa to travel to the United States ahead of the team's next World Cup match.

3:42 PM ET

Vozinha's mother has been granted a visa to travel to the United States for Cape Verde's World Cup match against Uruguay. The reunion comes after cost barriers initially prevented her from attending the team's earlier historic draw with Spain.

Cape Verde citizens faced a $15,000 returnable bond requirement in addition to visa fees. Though the Trump administration waived the requirement for World Cup ticket holders, the timing came too late for Vozinha's mother. US House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries announced the visa approval, allowing the 40-year-old goalkeeper's mother to finally watch her son play in the tournament.

Cape Verde Goalkeeper Vozinha to Reunite With Mother at World Cup

Cape Verde goalkeeper Vozinha's mother received a visa to travel to the United States ahead of the team's next match.

3:42 PM ET

Cape Verde goalkeeper Vozinha will be reunited with his mother at the World Cup after she was granted a visa to travel to the United States. The approval comes after cost barriers previously prevented her from attending his team's historic goalless draw against Spain.

Ana Candida Evora had been unable to afford the trip due to a $15,000 returnable bond requirement imposed on citizens of Cape Verde by the US government, in addition to visa fees. US House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries announced the visa approval and waived fees, allowing her to watch her 40-year-old son play in Cape Verde's upcoming match against Uruguay.
