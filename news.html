Swiss voters are deciding this weekend on a controversial initiative from the far-right Swiss People's Party that would limit the country's population to 10 million. The current population stands at approximately 9.1 million.

The proposal would require the Swiss government to implement restrictions if the permanent population reaches 9.5 million. These restrictions would target newcomers, including asylum seekers and family members of foreign residents. If the population reaches 10 million, further measures would take effect. If numbers do not decline, the government would be required to withdraw from its free-movement agreement with the European Union, Switzerland's largest export market.

The initiative reflects concerns about rapid population growth straining infrastructure and services. Switzerland's population has grown about five times faster than average in surrounding EU member states over the past decade, driven by economic success that attracts both skilled workers and corporate professionals. About 27% of Swiss residents are not citizens.

The SVP, the country's largest political party, frames the measure as addressing what it calls a "population explosion" that is inflating housing costs and overwhelming public services. The party has campaigned heavily against immigration through its history of electoral success since 1999.

Switzerland's system of direct democracy allows citizens to propose popular initiatives that reach a ballot if they collect 100,000 signatures within 18 months. Only about 10% of such initiatives pass, though a December poll showed 48% of voters supported this one, indicating deep divisions about the country's immigration approach.

Opposition to the measure comes from major multinational corporations including Roche, UBS, and Nestlé, as well as from parliament and business organizations. Opponents warn the proposal would jeopardize bilateral agreements with the EU that underpin Switzerland's prosperity. Economiesuisse, a leading business lobby, has called it the "chaos initiative," warning that Swiss companies depend on EU and European workers. Many firms would relocate abroad if unable to recruit them, damaging tax revenue and public services.

About half of Switzerland's exports go to the EU, making these economic relationships critical. Business groups also point out that population growth stems primarily from natural increase and longer life expectancy rather than immigration alone, meaning restrictions may prove ineffective.

Previous far-right proposals from the SVP, including automatic deportation for minor offenses and ending free movement with the EU, have not gained sufficient support at the ballot.

Results from this weekend's vote will be announced after polls close. The outcome will likely influence European debates over immigration policy and population management.

Yesterday, 8:10 PM ET

A federal judge has ruled that Donald Trump's name must be removed from the Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts in Washington, D.C., determining that only Congress has the authority to change the institution's official name.

U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper issued a 94-page opinion on Friday directing the Trump administration to take down all physical signage bearing Trump's name and remove references to a "Trump Kennedy Center" from official materials within 14 days. "The Kennedy Center's organic statute makes crystal clear that the Center is to be named for President Kennedy, and it cannot bear any other formal name or public memorial based on the Board's unilateral say-so," Cooper wrote. "Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name, and only Congress can change it."

The judge concluded that the Kennedy Center's board overstepped its authority by unilaterally renaming the institution after Trump. Work to remove the signage began in the early hours of Saturday, shortly after the venue missed the Friday deadline.

The dispute stems from Trump's efforts to reshape Washington's cultural institutions after taking office. In February, Trump appointed himself chair of the Kennedy Center's board of trustees, which he described as a "take-over." He replaced board members with his own appointees, and in December the new board voted to add his name to the building.

The lawsuit was brought by Joyce Beatty, an Ohio Democratic representative and board member. "Today's ruling rightly affirms that this administration's efforts to rename and close the Center have no basis in law," Beatty said. "The Kennedy Center is an institution that belongs to the American people, not to Donald Trump."

The ruling also addressed Trump's proposal to close the Kennedy Center for two years of renovations. Cooper temporarily blocked the closure, stating the board made an "ill-informed and seemingly preordained" decision based on insufficient information and without properly considering the center's statutory obligations.

Trump responded by posting on Truth Social that he had instructed the Department of Commerce to arrange a complete transfer of the Kennedy Center to Congress. He expressed that unless he could lead the institution's restoration, he had no interest in continuing involvement.

The Kennedy Center naming dispute has already had significant consequences. Multiple performing groups canceled bookings in protest, including a run of the Tony award-winning musical Hamilton. The executive director of the National Symphony Orchestra left for another position, and Richard Grenell, a Trump appointee who led efforts to overhaul the institution, stepped down as president in March.

The Kennedy Center receives federal funding and operates as a nonprofit, serving as the nation's premier performing arts venue and a living memorial to President John F. Kennedy.

Yesterday, 10:30 AM ET

The Department of Justice has cleared the way for Paramount Skydance to acquire Warner Bros. Discovery in a deal valued at $111 billion, concluding an eight-month antitrust investigation into one of the entertainment industry's largest consolidations. The approval allows the two media giants to combine their streaming services, film studios, and television networks.

The Justice Department's antitrust division determined that the merger would not harm competition or consumers across streaming video, linear television, and theatrical film distribution. In a statement, the agency noted it had received over two million documents from more than 80 sources during its investigation. The deal combines major content libraries including Star Trek, DC Comics properties, and HBO's programming alongside streaming platforms Paramount Plus and Max, along with cable networks including CNN and HBO.

Paramount celebrated the approval as a pro-competitive move that would strengthen its position against larger technology platforms. "This deal is pro-competitive, resulting in a stronger company better positioned to compete against dominant technology platforms in an industry increasingly defined by intense competition for audiences, talent, technology, and investment," the company said in a statement.

However, significant obstacles remain. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority has opened its own investigation and set an August 7 deadline to determine whether a more detailed review is needed. European regulators are also scrutinizing the funding behind the merger, which includes $24 billion in commitments from Gulf sovereign wealth funds. Australia has already approved the deal, and several other countries have provided clearance.

Critics have raised concerns about the merger's impact on competition and news independence. The combination would likely merge CNN and Paramount's CBS News, potentially resulting in significant job cuts as the companies pursue $6 billion in promised synergies. Some CNN staff have expressed worry that David Ellison, who would control the combined company through his family's Skydance investment, could alter editorial direction despite pledges to protect independence.

"The merger of Warner Bros and Paramount is not a done deal and remains under investigation by my office," California Attorney General Rob Bonta said on Friday, signaling that state-level legal challenges may follow.

Craig Aaron, co-chief executive of Free Press, criticized the approval, stating that "Paramount Skydance has feted, flattered and promised sweeping changes to news coverage to win the administration's approval." Senator Elizabeth Warren called the decision "terrible news for every American who doesn't want Trump-aligned billionaires to control what they watch and how much they pay."

The merger still requires shareholder votes at both companies and faces potential legal action from state attorneys general in coming weeks. The deal's completion timeline remains uncertain pending these additional reviews and approvals.

In a post on Truth Social, Trump stated that Iran "no longer want a Nuclear Weapon, nor will they have one, either through purchase, development, or any other form of procurement."

However, Iran's foreign ministry immediately cast doubt on the timeline. Esmaeil Baghaei, an Iranian foreign ministry spokesperson, said the exact date of signing remained uncertain. "We will have to wait and see about the exact date of the signing of the memorandum of understanding, although it will not be tomorrow," Baghaei said. He added that signing could happen "in the coming days" but was not guaranteed for Sunday.

Pakistan's prime minister, Shehbaz Sharif, who has served as a key mediator in the talks, expressed more optimism. He said on Saturday that Islamabad was preparing for an electronic signing within 24 hours, to be followed by technical-level talks the following week. "We are closer to a peace deal than ever before," Sharif wrote on social media.

The disagreement between the US and Iran over what the agreement contains has added to the uncertainty. According to sources quoted by Iran's Mehr news agency, the deal would end conflict on all fronts, including Lebanon, release 24 billion dollars of Iran's frozen assets, and allow Iran to levy service charges on passage through the Strait of Hormuz. Negotiations on Iran's nuclear program would occur afterward.

US officials presented a different picture, telling reporters that the agreement would require Iran's nuclear material to be destroyed and its nuclear program dismantled. They also said none of Iran's frozen money would be released until Iran met certain demands and stopped supporting allied militant movements in the Middle East.

Trump has made repeated claims about reaching a deal with Iran over the past three months, creating skepticism about whether an agreement will actually be finalized as announced. The president noted that if the deal fails, the US has "the ultimate alternative, hopefully never to be used again."

The announcement drew criticism from Democratic Representative Seth Moulton of Massachusetts, who called the reported details a terrible deal. "It's basically a surrender document from Donald Trump to the supreme leader of Iran," Moulton told reporters on Saturday.

Recent days have witnessed intense clashes between Iran, Israel, and the US since a ceasefire began in April. The proposed agreement would also reopen the Strait of Hormuz and lift the US naval blockade on Iranian ports, which has been in place since mid-April.
