8:23 PM ET

A federal judge has ordered the Trump administration to restore signs and exhibits at national parks that address topics including slavery and climate change. The ruling blocks the removal of educational materials that had been targeted under a recent executive directive.

The order requires officials to re-install displays that had been taken down from parks across the country. The affected exhibits include historical information about slavery and environmental content related to climate change. Multiple national park sites had begun removing or covering such materials following the administration's directive.

The judge's decision halts what the court characterized as censorship of park exhibits. The ruling comes after challenges were filed against the removal policy, with plaintiffs arguing that the directive violated educational and historical preservation mandates. The order applies to materials that had been removed or were scheduled for removal under the executive action.

National parks serve as educational institutions that preserve and interpret American history and natural resources for millions of visitors annually. The affected exhibits provide context about historical events and scientific information that parks have traditionally presented as part of their mission. The removal of such materials had drawn objections from historians, scientists, and park advocacy groups.

The Trump administration has not yet indicated whether it will appeal the ruling. Park officials must now work to restore the removed materials to their original locations. The decision represents a significant check on the executive directive and affirms the role of national parks in presenting comprehensive historical and scientific information to the public.

The Trump administration's Department of Justice has approved the $111 billion merger between Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery, clearing a major regulatory obstacle for the deal. The antitrust division completed its review after an eight-month investigation, determining that the transaction poses no competitive threat.

The Justice Department stated that it "received from the Parties over two million documents from over 80 custodians, substantial productions of data, as well as extensive documents, data, and advocacy from third parties across the media and entertainment ecosystem." Based on this review, regulators concluded the merger is unlikely to harm competition or consumers in streaming video on demand, linear television, or theatrical film distribution.

The combination will unite two major film studios and place CNN under the same corporate umbrella as CBS News. Paramount stated that the deal is "pro-competitive, resulting in a stronger company better positioned to compete against dominant technology platforms in an industry increasingly defined by intense competition for audiences, talent, technology, and investment."

However, significant obstacles remain. The UK Competition and Markets Authority opened an investigation into the merger and set an August 7 deadline to determine whether a more in-depth review is necessary. European regulators are also investigating the funding behind the merger, which includes $24 billion in commitments from three Gulf sovereign-wealth funds. Australia already approved the deal, and numerous other countries have provided clearance.

Concerns persist among journalists and industry observers. Staff members at both CBS News and CNN have expressed worry about potential job losses, as the companies have promised $6 billion in synergies from the deal. Some CNN staffers have also voiced concerns about editorial direction, given that the Ellison family, which controls Paramount, has close ties to the Trump administration. While David Ellison pledged in March that CNN's editorial independence would be protected, speculation continues about potential leadership changes.

A coalition of U.S. state attorneys general could still file a lawsuit to block the merger. California Attorney General Rob Bonta indicated Friday that the deal "remains under investigation by my office" and stressed that it is "not a done deal."

Opponents criticized the approval decision. Craig Aaron, co-chief executive of Free Press, said that "the fix was in at the Trump Justice Department from the start." Senator Elizabeth Warren called the approval "terrible news for every American who doesn't want Trump-aligned billionaires to control what they watch and how much they pay," adding that the merger "has reeked of corruption and influence-peddling."

The Justice Department's decision represents a significant step toward completing the transaction, but legal challenges and international scrutiny could still affect the merger's final outcome.
