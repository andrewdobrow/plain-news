US Restricts Foreign Access to Anthropic's Most Advanced AI Models

Anthropic has disabled access to its most powerful artificial intelligence models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, following a US government directive restricting foreign nationals from using the technology. The company announced it will "abruptly disable" the models for all users in compliance with the order, citing national security concerns. The government issued an export control di...

12:45 PM ET

Anthropic has disabled access to its most powerful artificial intelligence models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, following a US government directive restricting foreign nationals from using the technology. The company announced it will "abruptly disable" the models for all users in compliance with the order, citing national security concerns.

The government issued an export control directive targeting these advanced systems without providing Anthropic with specific details about the security threat. Anthropic expressed frustration with the decision, stating in a blog post: "We disagree that the finding of a narrow potential jailbreak should be cause for recalling a commercial model deployed to hundreds of millions of people."

The restrictions stem from concerns about the models' cybersecurity capabilities. The UK's AI Security Institute warned that Mythos represents a significant step up from previous models in terms of the cyber threat it poses. The system can identify flaws in IT systems and conduct attacks requiring multiple actions without human intervention, tasks that typically take cybersecurity professionals days to complete. During testing, Mythos successfully completed a 32-step simulated cyber attack three out of ten times, marking the first AI model to accomplish this benchmark.

These concerns intensified after an unauthorized access incident. Bloomberg reported that a small group of people gained access to the Mythos model through a third-party contractor environment before the official limited release. The individuals accessed the system using methods employed by cybersecurity researchers. While they reportedly have not conducted cybersecurity tests on the model and are primarily interested in exploring the technology rather than causing harm, the breach raised alarms among government officials.

UK AI Minister Kanishka Narayan warned that UK businesses "should be worried" about Mythos's ability to identify IT system vulnerabilities that hackers could exploit. The unauthorized access demonstrated that safeguards protecting the model could potentially be bypassed, prompting the government action.

The decision significantly impacts Anthropic's international business operations. Companies outside the United States that had integrated these models into their products and services now face service interruption. The move also reflects a broader shift in how governments approach advanced artificial intelligence, applying regulatory frameworks similar to those governing weapons technology and strategic semiconductors.

Industry observers note this represents a new phase in AI regulation, where the most capable systems face government oversight linked to national security. Anthropic, which competes with OpenAI and other major AI developers, had previously made these models available internationally. The restrictions may accelerate development of alternative AI capabilities in other countries as organizations seek systems not subject to US export controls.

The full economic impact on Anthropic and the broader AI industry remains uncertain as companies navigate the intersection of technological advancement, national security requirements, and international commerce.

A federal judge has ordered the Trump administration to restore signs and exhibits at national parks that were removed under a directive affecting content related to slavery and climate change. The ruling blocks the administration from continuing to remove what the judge characterized as historically accurate information from National Park Service sites.

The decision addresses changes made to interpretive materials at parks and historic sites across the country. These alterations affected displays and signage that addressed topics including the history of slavery at certain sites and climate-related information. The judge found that the removal directive constituted a form of censorship that violated legal standards for managing public lands and historical interpretation.

The ruling requires the National Park Service to undo changes made under the directive and restore previously displayed materials. This includes reinstalling plaques, signs, and exhibits that had been taken down or altered. The order applies to parks nationwide where such modifications occurred.

The case represents one of several legal challenges to administration policies affecting how history is presented at federally managed sites. National parks serve as educational resources visited by millions of Americans annually, with interpretive materials designed to provide historical context about the locations and events they commemorate. The dispute centered on whether the administration had the authority to direct such widespread changes to established historical interpretation.

The judge's order takes effect immediately, requiring the Park Service to begin restoration work. The administration has not yet indicated whether it will appeal the ruling. The decision leaves in place the underlying historical interpretation that existed before the directive was issued, maintaining continuity in how these sites present information to visitors.

Tyra Banks filed a lawsuit against Netflix on Saturday, alleging the streaming service manipulated her interview for the documentary "Reality Check: Inside America's Next Top Model." Banks is suing Netflix, 89 Blocks Holdings, EverWonder Studio, Netflix Music, and co-directors Mor Loushy and Daniel Sivan for false light, defamation by implication, breach of contract, and false endorsement.

The lawsuit claims Banks's testimony was "reassembled to support a false and defamatory narrative unrelated to what she actually expressed." According to co-director Mor Loushy, Banks received no advance questions, had no approval power over her presentation, and could only view the finished series when it became available to the public. "She had no say, no influence, no anything," Loushy told Vanity Fair.

Banks agreed to participate in the documentary after being told it would proceed with or without her involvement. She intended to present her perspective and express her commitment to making modeling more inclusive. Instead, the series sparked intense criticism from former contestants and judges.

The documentary became a major success for Netflix, drawing more than 14 million views in its first week and topping the platform's English-language charts. However, the publicity has intensified scrutiny of the reality competition show, which Banks created and hosted across 24 cycles from 2003 to 2018. The series has faced renewed criticism over its treatment of contestants and industry practices.

Former contestants expressed disappointment with Banks's response in the documentary. Brittany Brower called the series "triggering" and criticized Banks for failing to take accountability. Brittany Corinne Hatch stated that the show was "a systemic labor violation and a psychological experiment."

Banks did acknowledge going too far in one infamous moment from the show's fourth cycle. During an interaction with contestant Tiffany Richardson, Banks stated "You know, I lost it." According to judges interviewed in the documentary, Banks's outburst was more severe than what aired and resulted in her removal from the set. Richardson disputed Banks's account on social media, calling her a bully.

Her former judges offered limited support. Creative director Jay Manuel said he would have appreciated greater vulnerability from Banks. Catwalk coach J Alexander suffered a stroke in December 2022 and spent five weeks in a coma. Banks reportedly sent only a text message rather than visiting him in person.

Not all reactions were negative. Isis King, the show's first transgender contestant, acknowledged the positive impact the show had on her life.

The lawsuit represents Banks's attempt to hold Netflix and the production companies accountable for how she was depicted in the final cut. Neither Netflix nor the other defendants have publicly responded to the legal action.

12:58 PM ET

The United States and Iran are preparing to electronically sign a memorandum of understanding on Sunday that will extend their ceasefire by 60 days, reopen the Strait of Hormuz, and begin discussions about Iran's nuclear program. The virtual signing will include Pakistani and Qatari mediators who helped negotiate the deal.

The agreement comes after nearly three months of negotiations between the U.S. and Iranian governments, with assistance from Pakistan, Qatar, Egypt, and Turkey. The deal aims to end armed conflict in the region and provide stability after months of escalating tensions.

President Trump announced the signing plans on Truth Social, stating that "the deal is scheduled to get signed tomorrow." He highlighted that the U.S. relationship with Iran under his administration differs from previous approaches, though he did not elaborate on specific terms beyond the framework outlined by mediators.

The reopening of the Strait of Hormuz carries major economic consequences worldwide. This waterway handles between 10 and 25 percent of global oil and gas supplies. Its closure had disrupted energy markets and increased fuel prices internationally. Interior Secretary Doug Burgum addressed gasoline prices on Friday, attributing variations across states primarily to state policies and taxes rather than the Strait situation.

The 60-day ceasefire extension provides a negotiating window for Iran's nuclear program, which has long been a source of tension between Washington and Tehran. The virtual format reflects the sensitivity of the agreement and the ongoing diplomatic challenges between the two nations.

More than 40 countries have coordinated efforts to pressure Iran on the strait issue. UK Foreign Secretary Yvette Cooper said after chairing a virtual summit that coordinated action was essential. She emphasized that Iran's actions were affecting nations worldwide, influencing petrol prices, mortgage rates, jet fuel availability, fertilizer shipments to Africa, and gas supplies to Asia.

Britain's Permanent Joint Headquarters will convene a military discussion next week to explore options for clearing sea mines and assisting trapped ships. The UN is also examining whether a humanitarian shipping corridor can be established to ensure fertilizer reaches poorer nations and prevent food shortages.

World Bank predictions suggest continued blockage could push 9 million people into food insecurity while driving unsustainable increases in oil and food prices affecting households and businesses globally.

Not all political figures have supported Trump's approach. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch stated that if Trump started the conflict, he should not abandon resolving it, citing the principle that "if you break it, you own it." Reform UK leader Nigel Farage expressed difficulty understanding the president's motivations at times, while Liberal Democrat leader Ed Davey urged Prime Minister Starmer to strengthen efforts to reopen the shipping route.
