The U.S. Treasury Department moved to cut off cryptocurrency exchange MEXC from the American financial system over allegations the platform processed $16 billion in illicit transactions linked to Iran, North Korea and Russia. The action targets MEXC Global for alleged sanctions violations.

The Treasury decision blocks MEXC from accessing U.S. financial infrastructure and conducting transactions with American counterparties. The enforcement measure represents one of the largest actions against a cryptocurrency exchange based on alleged illicit transaction volume.

The alleged $16 billion in flows involve transactions connected to sanctioned countries including Iran, North Korea and Russia. The Treasury did not specify the time period over which the transactions allegedly occurred or detail the nature of the illicit activity.

In response to the Treasury action, digital asset company NatGold Digital announced it would immediately delist its NATG token from MEXC. The move reflects the immediate market consequences for platforms cut off from the U.S. financial system.

Del Casher, the guitarist and inventor credited with creating the wah-wah pedal that shaped decades of rock guitar, has died at 88. Casher died October 1 following a battle with cancer, according to a fundraising page for his widow Eiko.

Born Delton Kacher in Hammond, Indiana, Casher performed with artists including Gene Autry and Lawrence Welk while experimenting with new guitar technologies. In the early 1960s, while playing with the Thomas Organ company's Vox Ampliphonic Orchestra, he asked about making his guitar sound like a trumpet with the ability to suppress the instrument mid-playing. Though Casher often credited Thomas Organ engineer Brad Plunkett with the device's engineering, the wah-wah pedal was considered Casher's creation.

The pedal, first called the Cry Baby, initially failed to catch on when released in 1967. However, Frank Zappa gave one to Jimi Hendrix after Casher sat in with the Mothers of Invention in 1966. Hendrix used the pedal throughout his album Electric Ladyland, including on Voodoo Child and All Along the Watchtower. Eric Clapton employed it on Cream tracks like Tales of Brave Ulysses, and guitarists including Jimmy Page, Tony Iommi, and David Gilmour soon followed. By 1971, the wah-wah pedal appeared on Isaac Hayes' Theme From Shaft, a number one single and Oscar-winning song.

Casher also contributed to creating the Ecco-Fonic and the Fender Electronic Echo Chamber. He worked with Elvis Presley, appearing in the 1964 film Roustabout, and later contributed film scores. "I feel gratified that I was there at the beginning," Casher told the New York Times. "I would just like guitar players to think of me once in a while. The only thing I ask is: remember me."

Researchers trace worldwide power grid frequency drops to synchronized battery checks

German scientists solve mystery of minute-by-minute frequency sags occurring simultaneously on grids across continents.

3:30 PM ET

Researchers at Forschungszentrum Jülich in Germany have identified the cause of mysterious synchronized frequency drops occurring every 60 seconds on power grids worldwide. The team, led by Dirk Witthaut, determined that battery backup systems and uninterruptible power supplies check their charge status at the same time each minute because their internet-connected clocks are synchronized. In Ireland, where data centers consume 18 percent of grid electricity, the frequency drop quadrupled between 2016 and 2021.

Del Casher, guitarist who invented the wah-wah pedal, dies at 88

Del Casher, the guitarist and inventor credited with creating the wah-wah pedal that shaped decades of rock guitar, has died at 88. Casher died October 1 following a battle with cancer, according to a fundraising page fo...

6:00 PM ET

Del Casher, the guitarist and inventor credited with creating the wah-wah pedal that shaped decades of rock guitar, has died at 88. Casher died October 1 following a battle with cancer, according to a fundraising page for his widow Eiko.

Born Delton Kacher in Hammond, Indiana, Casher performed with artists including Gene Autry and Lawrence Welk while experimenting with new guitar technologies. In the early 1960s, while playing with the Thomas Organ company's Vox Ampliphonic Orchestra, he asked about making his guitar sound like a trumpet with the ability to suppress the instrument mid-playing. Though Casher often credited Thomas Organ engineer Brad Plunkett with the device's engineering, the wah-wah pedal was considered Casher's creation.

The pedal, first called the Cry Baby, initially failed to catch on when released in 1967. However, Frank Zappa gave one to Jimi Hendrix after Casher sat in with the Mothers of Invention in 1966. Hendrix used the pedal throughout his album Electric Ladyland, including on Voodoo Child and All Along the Watchtower. Eric Clapton employed it on Cream tracks like Tales of Brave Ulysses, and guitarists including Jimmy Page, Tony Iommi, and David Gilmour soon followed. By 1971, the wah-wah pedal appeared on Isaac Hayes' Theme From Shaft, a number one single and Oscar-winning song.

Casher also contributed to creating the Ecco-Fonic and the Fender Electronic Echo Chamber. He worked with Elvis Presley, appearing in the 1964 film Roustabout, and later contributed film scores. "I feel gratified that I was there at the beginning," Casher told the New York Times. "I would just like guitar players to think of me once in a while. The only thing I ask is: remember me."
