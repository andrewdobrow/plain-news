Only six companies in the S&P 500 index are currently valued higher, with Nvidia leading at $5.2 trillion. The previous IPO record holder is Saudi Aramco, which floated in 2019 at a valuation of $1.7 trillion and raised $25.6 billion.

If the IPO proceeds as planned, Musk's total net worth could potentially exceed $1 trillion, making him the world's first trillionaire.

SpaceX's sheer size means it will have substantial weighting in the S&P 500, exposing most people with investment accounts or pension funds to the company's stock performance. Up to a quarter of shares may be reserved for individual investors rather than institutional funds and banks, a larger allocation than is typical for mega-IPOs.

The sprawling SpaceX business encompasses three main divisions: the rocket-launching unit, the Starlink satellite broadband service, and the AI company that includes X, the social media platform formerly known as Twitter. While the overall business is loss-making, Starlink operates profitably. SpaceX lost $4.9 billion in 2025 on revenues of $18.7 billion, though revenue grew by one-third compared to the previous year.

The offering arrives as SpaceX continues developing its Starship vehicle, central to the company's Mars ambitions. The company has established itself as a leader in reusable rocket technology and operates critical contracts with NASA, which depends on SpaceX rockets for most of its launches. SpaceX has also acquired Musk's xAI company to develop solar-powered infrastructure supporting AI operations.

The IPO represents a significant opportunity for Wall Street banks competing for roles in the deal. The United States military launched strikes against Iran after President Donald Trump blamed Tehran for downing an American Apache helicopter near the Strait of Hormuz, threatening a fragile ceasefire established in April. Iran responded within hours by firing drones and missiles at U.S. military targets in Kuwait, Bahrain, and Jordan, marking a sharp escalation in hostilities between the two nations.

The helicopter went down early Tuesday morning off the coast of Oman during a patrol operation. A U.S. official told the Associated Press that the helicopter collided with an Iranian drone, though it remained unclear whether the collision was intentional. The two crew members were rescued in an unprecedented operation using an unmanned 24-foot drone boat called a Corsair, according to U.S. Central Command.

Trump accused Iran of shooting down the helicopter and declared the U.S. must respond. He directed the military to launch strikes at 10 p.m. UK time on Tuesday. U.S. media reported that Central Command targeted radar and air defense facilities along the Strait of Hormuz. Centcom described the mission as "a proportional response to unjustified Iranian aggression."

Iran's retaliation came Wednesday morning. Tehran claimed it had struck the U.S. al-Azraq base in Jordan with long-range missiles and targeted F-35 fighter jet hangars and command-and-control centers. Kuwait reported its air defenses firing after drones targeted Ali Al Salem base near the Iraqi border. U.S. officials told Reuters that nearly all missiles and drones launched by Iran were intercepted. Jordan's armed forces said they shot down five missiles before they reached their targets.

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi responded on social media, stating that Iran "will leave no attack or threat unanswered" and calling on the U.S. to "leave our region if you want to be safe." Iran's chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, warned: "We prefer the language of diplomacy, but we speak other languages far more fluently. Break your commitments, and we'll switch to what we speak best."

The Strait of Hormuz remains a critical waterway through which a significant portion of the world's oil supply passes. This military confrontation comes as ongoing conflict between the U.S., Israel, and Iran has disrupted global energy markets and increased prices for food and basic goods worldwide.

Trump played down the helicopter incident in an interview with the Wall Street Journal, calling it "not a big deal" and noting the pilot was fine. Just before the helicopter downing, he had expressed optimism about negotiations, saying there was a good chance of signing a deal within two or three days. Vice President JD Vance said the war could conclude within a week or a few months.

The exchange represents the most serious military confrontation since the April ceasefire, raising questions about whether diplomatic efforts can prevent further escalation.

A few minutes ago

The Department of Justice has closed the main San Francisco immigration court, a facility that was one of the busiest in the country and had one of the highest approval rates for asylum applications among U.S. immigration courts. The closure of the courthouse at 100 Montgomery Street took effect in January 2027, following news reports earlier in the year announcing the shutdown.

The decision has raised serious concerns among legal experts and immigration advocates. The closure comes after the Trump administration removed 20 of the court's 22 judges over the past year. The Department of Justice's executive office for immigration review described the closure as "cost effective" in a statement. A smaller San Francisco immigration court remains operational, but the majority of cases will be redirected to a facility 35 miles away in Concord, which opened in 2024.

The impact on pending cases is substantial. San Francisco currently has approximately 120,000 pending immigration cases, according to research from Syracuse University's Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse. Legal experts question whether the Concord court has adequate capacity to handle this influx, especially since six judges were recently removed from that location as well.

"With so few judges at the Concord court, we're going to see a lot of people waiting years and years and years to have their cases heard," said Milli Atkinson, director of the San Francisco Bar Association's immigrant legal defense program. Atkinson warned that the closure has created significant chaos, with court dates being rescheduled and notices being sent out with conflicting information.

The extended delays pose serious problems for asylum seekers. Shira Levine, a former judge at the San Francisco immigration court and now deputy legal director for the Immigration Institute of the Bay Area, explained that memories of witnesses and applicants can fade over years, weakening their case presentations. "Even if you submit the written evidence, years later, someone may not be available to testify in support of that evidence," Levine said.

There are additional practical concerns. Atkinson noted that many migrants have unstable addresses or may not receive mail notices about their cases. Notices in English may also not reach non-English speakers effectively. If someone misses their hearing due to incorrect information about dates, times, or locations, they could end up on Immigration and Customs Enforcement's radar. "If someone gets the wrong date, gets the wrong time, gets the wrong place, doesn't file something exactly correct, the consequences are in some cases, where they really do have a serious fear of return, life-threatening," Atkinson said.

Nationwide, there are 3.75 million pending immigration cases as of September 2025, according to the Department of Justice's executive office for immigration review. The San Francisco closure represents another significant challenge within an already overburdened immigration court system.

Born Chinnasamy Periyamaya Theva on July 17, 1941, in Allinagaram in what is now Theni district of Tamil Nadu, Bharathiraja worked primarily in India's Tamil-language film industry as a director. His career spanned nearly 50 years, during which he became recognized as a pioneer for bringing authentic portrayals of rural life to Tamil cinema. His films focused on the lives, struggles, and culture of people living in the countryside, departing from the urban-centered narratives that had dominated the industry.

Bharathiraja's work earned him recognition as a transformative figure in Indian regional cinema. His approach to filmmaking emphasized naturalistic storytelling and highlighted the experiences of rural communities, which had been largely underrepresented in mainstream Tamil films. Through his career, he directed numerous films that explored themes of village life, tradition, and social dynamics in rural Tamil Nadu.

The director died in Chennai, the capital of Tamil Nadu and the center of the Tamil film industry. His death marks the end of an era for Tamil cinema, where his influence extended beyond his own films to inspire subsequent generations of filmmakers. Bharathiraja's focus on rural narratives helped establish a distinct genre within Tamil cinema that continues to influence storytelling in the region.

His nearly five-decade career left a lasting impact on how rural India is portrayed in cinema. The filmmaker's work demonstrated that stories centered on village life could achieve both critical recognition and cultural significance, paving the way for more diverse representation in Indian regional cinema.

House Republicans pass $70 billion bill to fund immigration enforcement through 2028

House Republicans approved a $70 billion spending package on Tuesday that will fund immigration enforcement agencies through the end of President Trump's term. The bill passed with a vote of 214-212, largely along party lines, after the Senate approved the measure the previous week with a 52-47 vote. The legislation allocates $38 billion to Immigration and Customs Enforcement,...

4:40 AM ET

House Republicans approved a $70 billion spending package on Tuesday that will fund immigration enforcement agencies through the end of President Trump's term. The bill passed with a vote of 214-212, largely along party lines, after the Senate approved the measure the previous week with a 52-47 vote.

The legislation allocates $38 billion to Immigration and Customs Enforcement, $26 billion to Customs and Border Protection, and $5 billion to the Department of Homeland Security through September 2029. The bill now awaits Trump's signature.

The funding measure ends a months-long standoff between Republicans and Democrats over immigration enforcement resources. Democrats had blocked DHS funding in January following federal agents' fatal shooting of two US citizens in Minneapolis during an intensive immigration enforcement campaign. The Democratic boycott forced a 75-day government shutdown of the Department of Homeland Security beginning in mid-February. After the department reopened at the end of April, Democrats agreed to fund all DHS operations except ICE and CBP, while Republicans moved forward with this separate bill to provide multi-year funding for those agencies.

House Speaker Mike Johnson framed the bill's passage as an end to Democratic obstruction. "With today's vote, House and Senate Republicans have officially ended the third Democrat government shutdown of this Congress," Johnson said. He argued that Democrats had demonstrated opposition to border security policies rejected by voters.

House Democrats unanimously opposed the legislation. Democratic Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries criticized the measure, saying it would "waste $70 billion in taxpayer money to give a blank check to ICE without any guardrails, any oversight, any accountability."

Republicans countered that the bill funds critical law enforcement operations. House Majority Leader Steve Scalise stated that supporting the legislation meant "voting to fund law enforcement" and that opposing it amounted to "voting to defund the police."

The bill's passage represents a significant legislative achievement for Johnson, who manages a historically slim Republican majority. The measure had faced additional complications during its path through Congress. Democrats attempted to insert language blocking government settlements to anyone convicted of assaulting a police officer during the January 6 insurrection, but Republicans voted down the amendment. Some Republicans also sought bipartisan compromise on blocking Trump's proposed "anti-weaponization" fund, but those efforts were unsuccessful.

Senate Republicans also removed a controversial provision that would have allocated $1 billion for security improvements to a ballroom Trump is building at the White House, after the chamber's parliamentarian ruled it could not be included under the budget reconciliation procedure used to bypass the Democratic filibuster.

The multi-year funding approach removes these agencies from annual appropriations battles that have frequently threatened government shutdowns, providing budgetary certainty for immigration enforcement operations under the current administration.
