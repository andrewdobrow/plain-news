US crude oil inventories have dropped to their lowest level in over two decades as the conflict with Iran continues to drain strategic reserves. The stockpiles posted their sixth consecutive weekly decline, bringing total reserves to levels not seen since 2004. The draw on US oil supplies comes as the closure of the Strait of Hormuz has disrupted global oil flows.

The sharp reduction in crude inventories reflects both increased domestic consumption and the strategic decision to tap reserves amid the ongoing Iran conflict. The Strait of Hormuz, a critical shipping channel through which nearly one-fifth of global oil passes, remains closed to commercial traffic. This has forced the US and other consuming nations to rely more heavily on their own stockpiles to meet demand.

Despite the significant supply shock, oil prices have not reached the extreme levels some analysts predicted. Industry observers note that several factors have prevented oil from reaching $200 per barrel, including demand adjustments, increased production from other sources, and strategic reserve releases coordinated among consuming nations. The current price environment reflects a complex interplay between physical supply constraints and market expectations about the duration of the crisis.

The depletion of US crude reserves raises questions about long-term energy security if the conflict extends further. Strategic petroleum reserves are designed to provide a buffer during supply disruptions, but sustained draws reduce the cushion available for future emergencies. Energy analysts are monitoring inventory levels closely as the government balances immediate supply needs against maintaining adequate reserves for potential future shocks.

The oil market situation adds economic pressure alongside the geopolitical tensions in the Middle East. While prices have remained below worst-case scenarios, the combination of low inventories and ongoing supply disruptions creates uncertainty for businesses and consumers. How long the US can sustain current draw rates from its strategic reserves while maintaining adequate emergency capacity remains a central question for policymakers and energy markets.

A few minutes ago

Bill Maher joked on his HBO show Friday that he can gauge President Trump's temperament by monitoring his social media attacks. The comedian said he knows when Trump is in a bad mood because the president starts tweeting about him, referencing a pattern of presidential attacks directed at him personally.

Maher suggested Trump's approval ratings have affected the president's mood and tweeting behavior. The comedian's show has regularly featured political commentary on the Trump administration.
