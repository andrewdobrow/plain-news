8:49 AM ET

A federal judge has ordered the Trump administration to restore historical exhibits and educational materials that were removed from national parks, finding the administration's actions constitute censorship and set a dangerous precedent.

The ruling requires officials to reinstate any history or science materials removed from public monuments and national parks. One case involved a slavery exhibit at Independence National Historical Park in Philadelphia, where the National Park Service had dismantled a display about enslaved people who lived at the President's House, the former residence of Presidents George Washington and John Adams.

The city of Philadelphia sued the federal government over the removal, and U.S. District Judge Cynthia Rufe granted the city's request to temporarily block the removal and order restoration of the exhibit. Rufe, who was appointed by President George W. Bush, opened her ruling with a quote from George Orwell's 1984 about erasing uncomfortable facts from history.

"As if the Ministry of Truth in George Orwell's 1984 now existed, with its motto 'Ignorance is Strength,' this Court is now asked to determine whether the federal government has the power it claims to dissemble and disassemble historical truths," Rufe wrote, concluding: "It does not."

The judge ordered the National Park Service to restore the site to its physical status as of January 21, 2026, the day before the exhibit was removed. During a court hearing, Rufe described the Trump administration's argument that a president could unilaterally order changes to exhibits as both "horrifying" and "dangerous."

In her ruling, Rufe explained the historical significance of the exhibit. The display documented nine enslaved individuals owned by Washington who were brought to the presidential residence and rotated in and out of Pennsylvania to prevent them from petitioning for freedom under Pennsylvania law. The names of those nine people were etched into a wall at the exhibit.

The order came after Trump signed an executive order in March 2025 titled "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History," directing the secretary of interior to examine monuments, memorials, and statues altered after January 2020 to represent what the administration called a "false construction of American history."

Another federal judge also blocked the national parks order, characterizing the administration's actions as censorship and calling the removals a "dangerous precedent of censorship and sanitization." Officials were given 21 days to comply with restoration orders.

The rulings represent significant legal setbacks for the administration's efforts to control the content displayed at federally managed sites. The judges' decisions underscore the role national parks play as educational resources for millions of annual visitors seeking to understand American history.

Peace negotiations between the United States and Iran began in Islamabad on Friday, with senior officials from both nations meeting face-to-face for the first time since 1979. Pakistan's role as mediator has positioned the country as a critical bridge between Washington and Tehran during a period of intense conflict.

The talks brought together high-level delegations from each side. Vice President JD Vance is leading the American team, which also includes Steve Witkoff, a real estate developer serving as Trump's personal envoy, and Jared Kushner, the president's son-in-law. Iran's negotiating team is headed by Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, the parliamentary speaker, and Abbas Araghchi, the foreign minister. Pakistan's Army Chief Asim Munir, who brokered an earlier ceasefire, was present during discussions.

Pakistani state television confirmed that American and Iranian officials were "sitting directly at the same table," a significant development given their usual practice of remaining in separate locations during previous negotiations. The first round of talks lasted approximately two hours, followed by technical discussions focusing on security issues, financial matters, and control of the Strait of Hormuz. Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif hosted a dinner for the delegations at his residence.

A senior Pakistani official expressed optimism about the negotiations, stating that both delegations "have come to Islamabad with complete authority from their capitals and have stepped back from extreme positions." The official added that the talks were "in a strong position" and that "the entire world is looking for a breakthrough and an end of the war."

On arrival in Islamabad, Vance warned against any Iranian attempts at manipulation, saying the negotiating team would not be receptive if Iran tried to play games with the United States. Iranian media reported that the U.S. had agreed to unblock six billion dollars of Iranian assets frozen in Qatar, though American officials promptly denied this claim.

The preliminary discussions revealed Iranian demands for a ceasefire in Lebanon, reparations, and access to unfrozen assets as part of any peace agreement. However, the path forward remains uncertain, with significant complications continuing on the ground. Israeli forces have continued striking targets in Lebanon and southern areas, while Iranian state television threatened that any American warship crossing the Strait of Hormuz would be attacked within thirty minutes.

Additional negotiations are planned between Lebanese and Israeli ambassadors in Washington, where they will discuss ceasefire terms and the start date for direct talks between their countries. The complex nature of these discussions suggests that while optimism exists among negotiators, implementation and verification of any agreement would present substantial challenges requiring sustained commitment from all parties involved.

Yesterday, 8:06 PM ET

The Justice Department has cleared Paramount's $111 billion acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery, moving forward one of the largest media consolidations in recent history. The antitrust division completed its eight-month investigation and determined the merger would not harm competition or consumers.

"The Division has completed its analysis of the proposed merger of Paramount and Warner Bros and determined based on the evidence received in its investigation that the transaction is not likely to result in harm to competition or American consumers, including with respect to streaming video on demand, linear television, and studio development, production, or distribution of films for theatrical release," the agency stated.

The merger will unite Paramount, which owns CBS and the Paramount+ streaming service, with Warner Bros. Discovery, parent company of HBO, Discovery Channel, Max streaming platform, and CNN. The combined company will control extensive film and television production assets alongside major news operations. The Justice Department's eight-month investigation involved reviewing over two million documents from more than eighty custodians and substantial data from third parties across the media and entertainment sectors.

Despite the federal approval, significant hurdles remain. The UK Competition and Markets Authority opened an investigation into the merger this week with an August 7 deadline to determine whether a more thorough review is needed. European regulators are also investigating the funding behind the deal, including $24 billion in commitments from three Gulf sovereign-wealth funds. Australia has already approved the merger, determining it would not substantially lessen competition in theatrical film distribution.

State-level challenges may also emerge. California's attorney general, Rob Bonta, indicated on social media that the merger remains under investigation by his office and is not yet a done deal. Legal experts have suggested a coalition of state attorneys general could file a lawsuit to block the merger in coming weeks.

The decision has drawn criticism from media advocacy groups and politicians. Craig Aaron, co-chief executive of Free Press, argued that the Trump Justice Department approved the deal without adequate scrutiny, calling on state attorneys general to file suit. Democratic senator Elizabeth Warren characterized the approval as "terrible news for every American who doesn't want Trump-aligned billionaires to control what they watch and how much they pay."

Concerns focus on potential job losses and editorial changes. The companies have promised $6 billion in synergies from the deal, which industry observers expect could result in significant layoffs. Journalists at CBS News and CNN have expressed concerns about a possible merger of the two news networks. Some CNN staff members worry that Paramount's owner, David Ellison, and his father Larry, a longtime Trump associate, could influence the network's editorial direction, though David Ellison pledged in March that CNN's editorial independence would be protected.

The combined company will position itself to compete against dominant streaming platforms in an increasingly competitive media landscape where companies vie for audiences, advertising revenue, and subscription income.

The U.S. Commerce Department has directed Anthropic to disable access to its Fable 5 and Mythos 5 AI models following concerns about potential security vulnerabilities. The directive, issued as an export control order, requires the company to suspend access for all foreign nationals, according to statements from Anthropic.

The government's decision centers on worries that safeguards built into the models could potentially be bypassed, and that the systems might be used to identify software vulnerabilities. The Commerce Department did not provide Anthropic with specific details about the national security concerns underlying the order.

Anthropic has publicly disagreed with the government's assessment. The company stated that discovering a narrow potential jailbreak in Fable 5 does not justify removing a commercial product from service for hundreds of millions of users. The firm expressed its objection in a blog post following the announcement, emphasizing that the vulnerability's limited scope did not warrant such drastic action.

The shutdown represents a significant instance of federal intervention forcing a major artificial intelligence company to pull widely deployed models from service. Both the Fable and Mythos product lines are affected by the directive, disrupting access for the substantial user base that relied on these systems.

Anthropic has positioned itself as a leader in developing safe and responsible artificial intelligence, publicly warning about potential risks associated with advanced AI systems. The company emphasizes safeguards and ethical development practices in its corporate messaging. The government's action creates a notable tension with Anthropic's own transparency about vulnerabilities and safety measures.

The directive comes amid broader regulatory scrutiny of artificial intelligence companies. Anthropic has previously engaged with government agencies on security matters and stated it remains committed to supporting national security objectives. The company indicated it wants to continue negotiations with the government while challenging this particular action.

This federal directive reflects growing concerns about advanced AI systems and their potential security implications as these technologies become more capable and widely deployed. The jailbreak vulnerability referenced in the order refers to techniques that could potentially circumvent safety controls built into AI models, a category of security threat that has become increasingly prominent in discussions about artificial intelligence regulation.

Anthropic's experience demonstrates the complex relationship between AI companies, government regulators, and national security considerations. While the company has emphasized its commitment to responsible AI development and safety, the Commerce Department viewed potential vulnerabilities as serious enough to warrant immediate action. The shutdown will affect users who depended on these models for various applications, while raising questions about how federal authorities will balance security concerns against broader access to commercial AI products going forward.

A few minutes ago

Immigration and Customs Enforcement investigators have obtained individual voter files directly from local election officials in two counties, according to emails shared with Axios. The agency bypassed state-level channels to access the records as part of an expanding federal effort to investigate alleged noncitizen voting.

President Trump has long maintained that millions of people, including noncitizens, voted illegally in the 2016 and 2020 elections. He has cited these claims as the reason he lost the popular vote in 2016 and the general election in 2020. What was once Trump's personal assertion has now evolved into a coordinated multi-agency initiative that reaches directly into state and local voter systems.

The shift in approach is notable. Rather than working through state election boards or secretaries of state, ICE investigators are contacting county election officials directly to obtain voter data. This marks a significant change in how federal immigration authorities interact with local voting infrastructure and raises questions about the appropriate relationship between immigration enforcement and election administration.

The actual scope of documented noncitizen voting is far smaller than Trump's claims suggest. The Heritage Foundation, a conservative think tank that tracks voter fraud convictions and court records, has documented only 100 cases of noncitizen voting between 1982 and 2025. Election officials and voting rights advocates have long noted that existing safeguards in voter registration systems effectively catch the vast majority of ineligible applicants before they can vote.

Federal law already prohibits noncitizens from voting in federal elections, with violations carrying potential criminal penalties including fines and deportation. This legal framework means existing protections are already in place.

The extent of ICE's data collection effort remains unclear from the available information. The two counties where voter files were obtained have not been publicly identified. Federal officials have not disclosed how many additional jurisdictions may have received similar requests or what specific information ICE is seeking from voter registration records.

The effort comes as Trump has moved to significantly increase resources for immigration enforcement. Earlier this week, he signed a new law giving the Department of Homeland Security roughly 70 billion dollars in additional funding, which bankrolls his mass deportation campaign through the end of his term.

This direct access to local voter systems represents an expansion of federal immigration enforcement into areas traditionally managed by state and local election officials. The approach raises concerns among voting rights advocates about the potential for misuse of voter data and the blending of immigration enforcement with election administration activities.
