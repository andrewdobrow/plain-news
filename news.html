Reserve Bank Holds Rates Steady While Signaling Further Increases Remain Possible

The Reserve Bank of Australia left its official interest rate unchanged at 4.35% on Tuesday, delivering a brief respite to borrowers already struggling with previous increases. However, the central bank made clear that additional rate hikes could still occur if inflation fails to decline sufficiently.

Governor Michele Bullock stated that another rate increase remains a possibility if necessary to combat persistent price growth. "If we need to increase again, we will," Bullock said. The board did not even consider raising rates at Tuesday's meeting, with the decision to hold rates being unanimous.

The decision comes after three consecutive rate hikes earlier in 2026 that have significantly increased borrowing costs for Australian households and businesses. For someone with an average new mortgage of $745,000 at a typical rate of 6%, the year's increases have raised monthly repayments from $4,114 to $4,467. A further rate increase in August would add approximately $120 to monthly payments.

The central bank is navigating a difficult balance between controlling inflation and avoiding economic damage. Bullock said the RBA does not want to push the economy into recession but needs to slow spending enough to bring inflation back down to target levels. She expressed that the rising unemployment rate to 4.5% is not alarming, as the jobs market remains "a bit tight."

Economic activity is already showing signs of weakness. Real GDP growth slowed to just 0.3% in the March quarter, down from 0.9% in the previous quarter. Household spending on non-essentials barely increased during the same period, with consumers cutting back on savings to pay for essentials like electricity and fuel.

Bullock acknowledged that businesses are becoming reluctant to raise prices as household demand weakens. This is a key part of the process to reduce excess demand and bring inflation under control, she explained. However, Australia's inflation challenges will persist even if global oil supplies normalize, she noted, suggesting that underlying price pressures require continued attention.

Market reactions were mixed following the announcement. Financial futures indicated approximately a 55% chance of another rate hike by December, while Westpac predicted a hike in August. However, currency and stock traders appeared to bet that rate increases had become less likely. The Australian dollar fell slightly from 70.54 to 70.49 US cents, and the stock market rose, with the S&P/ASX200 index climbing from 8,890 to 8,914 points.

Major banks offered differing forecasts. Commonwealth Bank and ANZ maintained predictions that interest rates have peaked and cuts will occur in 2027. Treasurer Jim Chalmers welcomed the decision to hold rates steady, saying it neither eases nor worsens the financial pressure on Australian families.

Yesterday, 5:49 PM ET

Thirty-two college students received antibiotic treatment after being exposed to a deadly pathogen during a laboratory class where they were supposed to identify a harmless microorganism. The students instead identified a dangerous germ during what was intended to be a routine microbiology exercise.

The incident occurred during a standard lab identification exercise, a common component of college-level microbiology courses where students learn to culture and identify microorganisms. Students typically work with benign bacterial samples that pose minimal health risks. In this case, the organism present turned out to be a pathogen capable of causing serious illness or death.

All 32 exposed individuals began antibiotic treatment as a precautionary measure following the discovery. The specific pathogen involved was not identified in available information, nor was the college where the incident took place. Lab identification exercises typically involve students using various tests and procedures to determine the species of an unknown bacterial sample provided by instructors.

The exposure represents a significant breach of laboratory safety protocols. College microbiology labs follow strict procedures to ensure student safety, including careful selection and verification of teaching samples. Instructors typically culture and verify teaching strains before distribution to students to prevent exactly this type of incident. The mix-up raises questions about sample handling and verification procedures at the institution.

Antibiotic prophylaxis after pathogen exposure is standard medical practice to prevent infection before symptoms develop. The decision to treat all 32 students suggests health officials considered the exposure risk significant enough to warrant preventive measures. The incident highlights the inherent risks present even in educational laboratory settings and the importance of rigorous quality control in preparing teaching materials for student use.

6:00 AM ET

President Trump has demanded the Senate cancel its scheduled five-week August recess to debate legislation and advance funding for military operations against Iran. The directive has created significant tension within the Republican conference and thrown the chamber's schedule into uncertainty.

Trump wants senators to remain in Washington to consider the Safeguard American Voter Eligibility Act and to advance a budget reconciliation package that would fund ongoing military operations against Iran. The request breaks with the traditional summer recess period when senators typically return to their home states to campaign and conduct constituent services.

Senate Republicans are divided over whether to comply with the president's demand. Vulnerable Republican incumbents facing reelection had planned to spend August campaigning in their states, making the cancellation request particularly difficult for those fighting to keep their seats. The disagreement reflects broader tensions within the GOP conference over Trump's expanding influence on congressional operations and scheduling.

Pentagon operations related to Iran are drawing increased scrutiny from lawmakers in both parties. The Defense Department has begun reclassifying casualty figures and declining to disclose details about recent airstrikes, drawing criticism from those who argue Congress needs accurate information to make informed funding decisions. The military has also refused to hold regular press conferences to discuss operations.

Senate Majority Leader John Thune faces pressure from multiple directions as he manages the request. Thune has previously resisted some Trump demands while maintaining his leadership position, but this latest directive tests his ability to balance the president's priorities with the political needs of his conference members. The way the Senate responds will signal whether Trump can reshape congressional schedules and procedures beyond traditional executive branch boundaries.

The timing of Trump's demand creates practical challenges for the Senate. August recess is typically used for senators to return home, hold town halls, and build support among constituents before the challenging midterm season. Canceling it would require senators to remain in the Capitol during the traditionally slower legislative period and the hottest month of the year in Washington.

Some Republicans have expressed concern that staying in session would limit their ability to campaign in their districts and states, potentially weakening their political standing back home. Others may be more inclined to support Trump's request, viewing it as necessary to address what they consider urgent legislative priorities.

The fate of Trump's demand remains unclear as Senate leadership weighs the competing pressures. Thune's decision will likely determine whether the August recess proceeds as scheduled or whether the Senate remains in session as the president has requested.
