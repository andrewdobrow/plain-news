Justice Department moves to strip citizenship from 17 naturalized Americans

4:15 PM ET

The Justice Department has filed denaturalization proceedings against 17 naturalized US citizens, representing a significant expansion of citizenship revocation efforts under the Trump administration. The move follows a June 11 memo directing federal attorneys to prioritize denaturalization cases against people who either "illegally procured" citizenship or obtained it through "concealment of a material fact or by willful misrepresentation."

The policy targets naturalized citizens across multiple priority categories, including those involved in war crimes, human rights abuses, and criminal activity. According to the memo, denaturalization efforts should focus on naturalized criminals, gang members, and individuals convicted of crimes who pose ongoing threats to the country. The directive also expands the scope to include cases involving false statements on immigration forms, financial fraud, and medical fraud.

Denaturalization is a rare legal process that allows the government to revoke citizenship from naturalized Americans. Unlike deportation proceedings for non-citizens, denaturalization requires federal court review. The government must prove that citizenship was obtained illegally or fraudulently. If successful, the proceedings would strip individuals of their US citizenship and could lead to deportation.

One case already concluded involved Elliott Duke, a military veteran originally from the UK who was convicted of distributing child sexual abuse material. A judge ordered revocation of his citizenship on June 13 after he failed to disclose the conviction during naturalization.

The policy has raised concerns among immigration advocates. Sameera Hafiz, policy director of the Immigration Legal Resource Center, told NPR: "It is kind of, in a way, trying to create a second class of US citizens." Civil rights attorneys note that denaturalization through civil litigation strips individuals of certain protections available in criminal cases, including the right to a government-provided attorney and a higher standard of proof. The civil process lowers the evidentiary threshold and speeds up proceedings.

The memo's focus on denaturalization reflects broader changes within the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division. The division, traditionally tasked with combating racial discrimination, has been redirected toward the Trump administration's policy priorities, including efforts to end diversity, equity, and inclusion programs. According to NPR, approximately 250 attorneys, or 70 percent of the division's lawyers, left the department between January and May.

The estimated 25 million US citizens who immigrated to the country represent the broader population potentially affected by the administration's approach. Each denaturalization case will proceed independently through federal court, where judges will evaluate whether the government has met its burden of proof. The individuals targeted retain the right to legal representation and can contest the allegations in court.

In a Monday blog post, OpenAI acknowledged the filing and stated that it expects details to eventually become public. "We recently submitted a confidential S-1. We expect it to leak so we're just announcing it," the company wrote. The ChatGPT maker is expected to be valued at more than $850 billion.

OpenAI emphasized that it has not yet decided on timing for the actual IPO launch. "We have not decided on timing yet; it may be a while because there are things we want to do that are likely easier as a private company. But it's a complicated set of tradeoffs and this gives us the option to go public sooner if that ends up being best," the company stated.

The confidential filing process allows companies to begin preparing for an IPO while keeping detailed financial information private until closer to their public debut. This preliminary step gives OpenAI flexibility as it weighs the benefits and drawbacks of remaining private versus going public.

The filing intensifies competition between the two leading AI companies. Both firms have been racing toward public markets for roughly a year, with Anthropic filing first on June 1st. Wall Street banks are closely monitoring both companies as they position themselves to capture underwriting fees and offer shares to institutional clients.

OpenAI's decision to publicly announce its confidential filing, rather than keeping it private, suggests the company wanted to control its own narrative. The company acknowledged that such filings typically become public knowledge eventually, so it chose to communicate the news directly to stakeholders.

The timing reflects broader trends in the AI industry. As artificial intelligence technology matures and competition intensifies, private AI companies face mounting pressure to provide investors with liquidity. OpenAI has raised billions in venture capital funding in recent years, reaching a valuation that made it one of the most valuable private technology companies globally.

The move also signals confidence in investor appetite for AI companies, despite recent volatility in technology stocks. The company's eventual public offering will subject it to greater financial scrutiny and mandatory quarterly reporting requirements that private companies typically avoid.

The path from confidential filing to actual trading typically spans several months. OpenAI will eventually need to publicly release detailed financial statements and risk disclosures before conducting a roadshow to pitch institutional investors. Potential shareholders will examine the company's revenue growth, profitability prospects, and competitive positioning against both established technology giants and other AI-focused startups.

The confidential filing process typically extends over several months as regulators review submissions and companies prepare for roadshows to pitch their offerings to institutional investors. Former Justice Department Attorneys Criticize Live Nation-Ticketmaster Settlement

Two former senior antitrust attorneys at the U.S. Department of Justice criticized the department's surprise settlement with Live Nation and Ticketmaster, calling it deeply troubling selective non-prosecution of political allies. The settlement was reached just one week after trial began in a major antitrust case brought by the DOJ and over 30 states against the company. The f...

A few minutes ago

Two former senior antitrust attorneys at the U.S. Department of Justice criticized the department's surprise settlement with Live Nation and Ticketmaster, calling it deeply troubling selective non-prosecution of political allies. The settlement was reached just one week after trial began in a major antitrust case brought by the DOJ and over 30 states against the company.

The former attorneys, who were instrumental in building the case against Live Nation and Ticketmaster, expressed serious concerns about the abrupt resolution. They questioned the timing and terms of the deal, suggesting it raised troubling questions about how the department handled this high-profile enforcement action.

The original lawsuit represented one of the most significant antitrust cases in the entertainment industry in recent years. The DOJ and attorneys general from 30 states, including California, Massachusetts, Utah and Wyoming, accused Live Nation of monopolistic practices that dominated the live entertainment and ticketing markets. New York's attorney general told jurors that Ticketmaster keeps an average of $7.58 from each ticket sold at major concert venues, claiming the company harms artists, venues and fans through its market dominance.

Under the settlement agreement, Live Nation will establish a $280 million fund for participating states. Ticketmaster must open parts of its ticketing platform to rival companies and divest from exclusive booking agreements with 13 U.S. amphitheaters. The deal also caps Ticketmaster service fees at 15 percent of the ticket price and limits long-term exclusivity contracts with venues.

In a statement, Live Nation CEO Michael Rapino said the settlement represented progress for the concert industry. "By giving artists greater flexibility in choosing their promotional partners and ticketing strategy while also keeping the cost of a concert more affordable for fans, we are putting more power where it should be with artists and fans," he said.

However, the settlement has faced significant pushback. A bipartisan group of state attorneys general refused to accept the terms, arguing the deal fails to address Live Nation's core monopoly in ticketing. Letitia James, New York's attorney general, stated that the settlement would benefit Live Nation at consumers' expense and does not rectify the company's dominance.

"The settlement recently announced with the U.S. Department of Justice fails to address the monopoly at the center of this case," James said. "My attorney general colleagues and I have a strong case against Live Nation, and we will continue our lawsuit to protect consumers and restore fair competition to the live entertainment industry."

The trial had been underway for only one week when the settlement was announced, cutting short what legal observers expected would be months of testimony and evidence presentation. The quick resolution surprised the legal community and raised questions about the strength of the government's position going forward.

OpenAI has filed confidentially to go public on the US stock market, the company announced Monday. The ChatGPT maker is expected to be valued at more than $850 billion.

In a blog post, OpenAI stated: "We recently submitted a confidential S-1. We expect it to leak so we're just announcing it. We have not decided on timing yet; it may be a while because there are things we want to do that are likely easier as a private company. But it's a complicated set of tradeoffs and this gives us the option to go public sooner if that ends up being best."

An S-1 is an investor prospectus that companies submit to the Securities and Exchange Commission ahead of an initial public offering. The confidential filing process allows firms to submit their IPO paperwork to regulators without immediately revealing financial details to the public.

The move comes just over a week after Anthropic, OpenAI's main rival and maker of the Claude chatbot, announced its own confidential IPO filing with the SEC. The near-simultaneous filings from the two leading artificial intelligence companies intensify the competitive race between them to reach the public markets.

OpenAI has raised billions in venture funding from investors including Microsoft, while Anthropic has secured substantial backing from Google and other investors. Both companies are now pursuing paths that will subject them to quarterly earnings reports and shareholder oversight once they become publicly traded entities.

The timing reflects growing investor appetite for artificial intelligence companies as the technology gains wider adoption across industries. Both firms will need to demonstrate sustainable business models and revenue growth to justify their valuations as they transition from private operations to public market scrutiny.

OpenAI's announcement that it expects its filing to become public underscores the practical reality that confidential submissions often leak before companies are ready for full disclosure. By announcing the filing themselves, the company controlled the narrative around the announcement rather than waiting for information to emerge through other channels.

The confidential filing process typically extends over several months as regulators review submissions and companies prepare for roadshows to pitch their offerings to institutional investors. The SEC's review period gives both companies time to make adjustments to their financial disclosures before they formally proceed with a public offering.

Neither company has provided a specific timeline for when their shares might begin trading. OpenAI indicated that the decision to go public depends on weighing various factors, suggesting the company wants to remain private for the near term if certain strategic objectives are easier to accomplish outside the public market structure. The competition between the two AI firms to complete their offerings could influence how the market receives both debuts.
