The US military launched strikes against multiple targets in Iran on Wednesday evening following a helicopter incident near the Strait of Hormuz, according to US Central Command. The operation began at 5:15 p.m. Eastern time at President Trump's direction after an American Apache helicopter went down off the coast of Oman earlier in the week.

A US official told the Associated Press that the helicopter collided with an Iranian drone, though it remains unclear whether the collision was intentional. Trump initially stated the helicopter had been "shot down" on social media, blaming Iran for the incident. However, in an interview with the Wall Street Journal, Trump downplayed the crash, calling it "wasn't a big deal" and noting that "the pilot is fine." Both crew members were rescued in what the US military described as an unprecedented operation using an unmanned drone boat.

The strikes represent the latest escalation in a cycle of attacks between the two countries. Iran had launched retaliatory strikes on Wednesday morning with missiles and drones targeting facilities in Kuwait, Bahrain, and Jordan. Iran's Revolutionary Guards claimed they had struck the US al-Azraq base in Jordan using long-range missiles and targeted F-35 fighter jet hangars and a command-and-control center. US officials told Reuters that nearly all the missiles and drones launched by Iran were intercepted, with Jordan's armed forces saying they shot down five missiles.

US Central Command characterized the American response as a "proportional response to unjustified Iranian aggression." Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth had warned earlier Wednesday that forthcoming attacks would be strong and clear, indicating the military would conduct operations over multiple nights if needed.

The escalating tensions threaten a fragile ceasefire that the US and Iran announced in April. Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi responded to the American strikes on social media, stating Iran "will leave no attack or threat unanswered" and warned the US to "Leave our region if you want to be safe."

This cycle of strikes follows broader conflict in the Middle East, with Iran and Israel exchanging fire on Monday in what officials described as significant blows to the already strained ceasefire. The ongoing military operations have had global economic consequences, raising energy prices worldwide and making basic goods more expensive.

Despite the military escalation, Trump had expressed optimism about negotiations with Iran just before the helicopter incident. He had said there was "a good chance" of signing a deal within "two or three days," though he provided no details. The president had also suggested that a negotiated settlement would be preferable to extended military campaigns that could cause significant casualties.

4:41 PM ET

A federal judge has permanently blocked Alabama from executing Jeffery Lee using nitrogen gas, determining that the execution method violates the constitutional prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The ruling prevents the state from carrying out Lee's scheduled execution on Thursday at an Alabama prison.

Judge Emily C Marks issued the 26-page decision after the 11th Circuit Court of Appeals had reversed her earlier ruling in May that allowed nitrogen gas executions to proceed. In that first decision, Marks had cited testimony from three medical experts stating that "air hunger" caused by nitrogen gas did not constitute an unconstitutional level of pain. However, the appeals court panel disagreed, writing that Alabama's nitrogen hypoxia protocol presented a "substantial risk of serious harm" involving severe pain beyond what is necessary to carry out a death sentence.

Alabama became the first state to use nitrogen gas as an execution method in January 2024, but the practice has faced repeated legal challenges. The method involves placing a mask over the condemned person's face and replacing breathable air with pure nitrogen, causing death by oxygen deprivation.

In her latest ruling, Marks wrote that the constitution does not guarantee a painless death and that litigation will likely accompany any execution method the state pursues. She stated that Lee was not entitled to an injunction preventing execution through lethal injection or the electric chair, the two other authorized execution methods in Alabama. She noted that were Alabama to adopt firing squad, that method would also likely face constitutional challenges.

The case highlights concerns about Lee's sentencing. In 2000, a jury voted to sentence Lee to life without parole for his 1998 murders of Jimmy Ellis and Elaine Thompson. However, a judge overrode the jury's recommendation and sentenced him to death instead. Alabama banned judicial override in 2017 under Governor Kay Ivey, but the law applied only to future cases, not past cases like Lee's. His legal team has requested that Ivey grant clemency and apply the ban retroactively.

Concerns about nitrogen gas executions intensified after the first such execution in the United States. Kenneth Eugene Smith's execution by nitrogen took 22 minutes in 2024. Journalist Lee Hedgepeth, who witnessed the execution, told the BBC: "I've been to four previous executions and I've never seen a condemned inmate thrash in the way that Kenneth Smith reacted to the nitrogen gas."

A spokesman for Alabama's Attorney General Steve Marshall said the state is appealing Marks's decision. The case is likely to reach the United States Supreme Court, which has previously allowed nitrogen executions to proceed.

5:01 PM ET

Consumer prices rose at their fastest pace in three years, reaching 4.2 percent annually in May as energy costs surged following the onset of conflict between the United States and Iran. The acceleration marks the third consecutive monthly increase since the war began and has outpaced wage growth, squeezing household budgets across the country.

Energy prices have climbed sharply as oil markets react to the military operations. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has disrupted global oil supplies and pushed crude prices higher. These increases have rippled through the economy, affecting transportation costs, heating bills, and the price of goods that depend on fuel for production and distribution. Before the conflict began, inflation stood at 2.4 percent.

Companies face mounting pressure from rising input costs but appear reluctant to pass those increases directly to consumers. Many businesses worry that shoppers, already stretched by months of elevated prices, will reduce spending if prices climb further. Wages have not kept pace with inflation, leaving workers with less purchasing power than they had earlier in the year.

President Trump responded to the inflation figures with an unusual statement, saying "I love the inflation" and expressing no concern about the rising prices due to recent developments in the conflict. His comments represent a significant departure from typical political responses to inflation, which usually emphasize efforts to lower prices. A Trump administration spokesperson stated that the president is focused on the domestic economic situation and is working to bring down prices and reopen the Strait of Hormuz.

The inflation data sent stock markets lower, with major indexes falling more than one percent. Oil prices surged following Trump's remarks about Iran and the ongoing military strikes. Investors now watch to see whether the Federal Reserve will adjust its monetary policy in response to the accelerating price pressures, and whether consumers will reduce spending as their paychecks stretch less far.

The inflation surge extends beyond the United States. Across the eurozone, inflation soared to 3 percent in April as the Iran war drove up energy prices. Energy costs rose 10.9 percent year on year across the euro area, up from 5.1 percent the previous month. The increase pushed eurozone inflation further above the European Central Bank's 2 percent target. Economic growth across the eurozone slowed to just 0.1 percent in the first quarter, down from 0.2 percent previously. Germany outperformed expectations with 0.3 percent growth, while France recorded no growth, with foreign trade making a negative contribution to economic output and household consumption falling.

The ShinyHunters hacking gang claims to have compromised the Oracle PeopleSoft servers of more than 100 organizations, including many universities. The alleged breach represents a significant security incident affecting enterprise resource planning systems used by institutions across multiple sectors.

Oracle PeopleSoft is a widely deployed enterprise software platform that organizations use to manage human resources, financial operations, and student administration systems. Universities have adopted the platform extensively for managing enrollment, payroll, and academic records. The claimed compromise raises concerns about potential exposure of sensitive institutional and personal data stored within these systems.

ShinyHunters has previously claimed responsibility for data breaches targeting major companies and institutions. The group has a history of selling stolen databases and credentials on underground forums. The gang's latest claim suggests a coordinated campaign targeting organizations running PeopleSoft infrastructure, though the full scope of any data exfiltration has not been independently verified.

The security implications for affected organizations could be substantial. PeopleSoft systems typically contain employee records, student information, financial data, and authentication credentials. Universities may need to assess whether sensitive information was accessed and notify affected individuals if personal data was compromised. Organizations running PeopleSoft will need to review their server configurations and security protocols to determine vulnerability.

Oracle has not publicly commented on the alleged breach. Universities and other organizations using PeopleSoft will likely conduct internal security audits and may implement additional protective measures while investigating whether their systems were affected. The incident highlights ongoing cybersecurity challenges facing institutions that rely on enterprise software platforms to manage critical operations and sensitive data.
