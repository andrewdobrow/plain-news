5:25 PM ET

The Supreme Court has ruled 8-1 against AT&T and Verizon in a case challenging the Federal Communications Commission's power to impose penalties for mishandling customer location information. The decision affirms the agency's ability to levy fines through its internal regulatory process without triggering constitutional protections for jury trials.

The dispute centered on whether the FCC's administrative penalty system violated the companies' Seventh Amendment rights. AT&T and Verizon argued that financial sanctions for violations should require a jury trial rather than proceed through the agency's in-house proceedings. The justices rejected this challenge, upholding the FCC's enforcement framework.

The case involved both carriers' actions in allowing third parties to access sensitive location data without proper safeguards. The FCC determined that the companies had failed to implement adequate protections before sharing this information, prompting the agency to pursue enforcement action and impose fines.

The Supreme Court's decision represents a significant outcome for regulatory enforcement and privacy advocates seeking to hold telecommunications companies accountable for their data practices. Location information reveals highly personal details about individuals, including their homes, workplaces, places of worship, and medical facilities. By backing the FCC's administrative penalty system, the court strengthens the agency's ability to enforce meaningful consequences without the delays associated with jury trials.

The ruling also has broader implications for federal agencies' enforcement capabilities. It establishes that administrative penalties for telecommunications violations do not automatically trigger jury trial requirements under the Constitution, allowing regulators to proceed with enforcement actions more efficiently.

For AT&T and Verizon, the decision closes off a legal strategy that could have limited federal oversight of their data practices. Both companies must now pay the fines originally assessed by the FCC. The case sets precedent for future regulatory actions involving telecommunications companies and customer privacy protections.

The decision comes as regulators and lawmakers have increased focus on how telecommunications companies handle customer data. Location information has proven particularly sensitive because of its ability to reveal intimate details about individuals' lives and movements. The FCC's enforcement action against these carriers reflected growing concerns about third-party access to such data.

The 8-1 vote indicates strong consensus among the justices on the scope of the FCC's regulatory authority, with only one justice dissenting from the majority opinion. This overwhelming support for the agency's enforcement framework suggests the court sees administrative penalty systems as legitimate regulatory tools that do not violate constitutional protections.

The ruling also comes during the Trump administration's tenure and represents a victory for the FCC's regulatory authority during that period.

In the letter, Zelensky proposed meeting in a neutral third country and argued that only direct engagement between Ukraine and Russia could bring the war to an end. The message mixed diplomatic overtures with pointed criticism of Putin's 26-year tenure in power, suggesting that age is taking a toll on the Russian leader. The proposal comes at a time when the United States has directed diplomatic attention elsewhere, potentially creating an opening for bilateral discussions between the two nations.

Both armies are showing signs of exhaustion while sustaining heavy casualties and conducting strikes on each other's energy infrastructure.

Analysts suggest that a diplomatic breakthrough remains unlikely in the near term. 9:13 PM ET

Ohio State University's board of trustees has approved a $100 million settlement to resolve lawsuits filed by survivors of sexual abuse perpetrated by Richard Strauss, a physician who treated students at the institution for two decades.

Strauss worked at Ohio State from 1978 to 1998 and sexually abused male students who sought medical treatment. An independent investigation commissioned by the university in 2018 determined that Strauss had abused at least 177 male students. The investigation also revealed that university officials had received complaints about Strauss as early as 1979 but failed to take action to stop him. Strauss died in 2005.

As of February, Ohio State had already reached settlement agreements totaling more than $60 million with more than 100 survivors. More than 100 additional plaintiffs had litigation still pending at that time. The new $100 million settlement represents the final resolution of the formal litigation process, with all but one of the Strauss survivors agreeing to settle with the university.

The university stated that none of the settlements involved taxpayer funds, tuition money, or restricted donor funds, according to reporting by the National Public Radio affiliate WOSU.

The Ohio State case emerged during a challenging period for the institution. The university faced significant backlash surrounding former trustee and prominent alumnus Les Wexner, a retail mogul with a well-documented relationship with late financier Jeffrey Epstein. Following Wexner's congressional testimony about his connection to the disgraced Epstein in February, growing calls emerged for the university to remove Wexner's name from campus and health system facilities.

The settlement came shortly after Ohio State's president, Walter "Ted" Carter Jr., resigned following disclosure of an inappropriate relationship. Carter, who had led the university since 2024, stated that he "made a mistake in allowing inappropriate access to Ohio State leadership." The board accepted his resignation after determining he "had an inappropriate relationship with someone seeking public resources to support her personal business." Board Chair John Zeiger said the board was "surprised and disappointed" to learn of the matter and emphasized that it "takes the situation and its potential impact on the university very seriously."

Carter had previously served as president of the University of Nebraska from 2020 to 2023. He succeeded Kristina Johnson, who resigned from Ohio State in November 2022.

Ohio State has implemented new policies and procedures aimed at preventing similar abuse in the future, including enhanced reporting mechanisms and mandatory training for staff who work with students. The university has apologized to survivors and acknowledged that it failed to protect students during the Strauss period.

Trump directs $700 million into coal using wartime powers as energy costs rise

President Donald Trump announced Thursday that he is directing $700 million in federal investment into the coal industry using wartime presidential authority. The move represents an escalation of his administration's efforts to revive America's declining coal sector. Trump invoked the Defense Production Act, a Cold War-era statute designed to accelerate industrial output durin...

8:03 PM ET

President Donald Trump announced Thursday that he is directing $700 million in federal investment into the coal industry using wartime presidential authority. The move represents an escalation of his administration's efforts to revive America's declining coal sector.

Trump invoked the Defense Production Act, a Cold War-era statute designed to accelerate industrial output during national emergencies, to provide grants to coal-fired power plants and mining operations across the country. "Today, we're taking historic action to bring down the price of energy and the cost of living for all Americans with the power of clean, beautiful coal," Trump said at a White House press conference.

The $700 million investment will protect 14 coal plants and 42 coal mines, while also funding construction of two new coal plants and one large export terminal. The new export facility will be built in Oakland, California, and an existing facility in Maryland will be restarted. Coal plants in 10 states will remain operational through the funding: West Virginia, Kentucky, North Carolina, Indiana, Tennessee, Arkansas, Arizona, Oklahoma, North Dakota and Wisconsin. Trump noted that all 10 of these states voted for him in recent elections. The two new plants will be constructed in Alaska and West Virginia.

The coal industry has welcomed the announcement. Rich Nolan, chief executive of the National Mining Association, said the investment will help the US meet surging electricity demand driven by the artificial intelligence sector. "Coal generation shields consumers from the impacts of volatile energy prices and supply challenges," he said.

Environmental groups have strongly criticized the decision. Patrick Drupp, climate policy director of the Sierra Club, called it "disgusting and reprehensible" and said the funding betrays Trump's promises to lower energy costs. "This handout betrays everything Donald Trump promised and only serves his big coal buddies," Drupp said. Kit Kennedy, a senior climate campaigner at the Natural Resources Defense Council, questioned the investment's logic. "What's next, a taxpayer bailout to build new phone booths?" he asked.

Coal remains the most carbon-intensive fossil fuel and a leading contributor to climate change. Research estimates that air pollution from coal plants caused approximately 460,000 deaths in the US between 1999 and 2020. The coal industry has faced decades of decline, with US coal production now less than half of 2008 levels as natural gas and renewable energy sources have become cheaper alternatives.

Trump has long championed coal's revival, even creating a mascot called Coalie to promote the fuel. The administration has also dismantled environmental rules limiting toxins from coal plants and forced ratepayers to subsidize aging facilities. Despite these efforts, employment in coal has declined by more than 90 over the past century, with more Americans now working at Waffle Houses than in coal mining.

9:30 PM ET

The Cleveland Browns traded star edge rusher Myles Garrett to the Los Angeles Rams in exchange for linebacker Jared Verse and three draft picks, marking a significant shift in the defensive landscape for both franchises.

Garrett, 30, is a two-time NFL Defensive Player of the Year and seven-time Pro Bowl selection. Since the Browns selected him with the first overall pick in 2017, he has accumulated 125.5 sacks, 413 pressures, and 149 tackles for loss—more than any other player during that span. In 2025, he set a single-season sacks record with 23 tackles.

Garrett had requested a trade from the Browns in 2024 after the team finished with a 3-14 record, expressing his desire to join a championship contender. Despite his trade request, he signed a four-year, $160 million extension with Cleveland that made him the highest-paid non-quarterback in league history at the time. The deal included $122.8 million guaranteed and an average annual salary of $40 million.

The Browns again disappointed in 2025, finishing with a 5-12 record and missing the playoffs for the second consecutive year. Garrett was the lone Browns player to earn All-Pro recognition. The franchise subsequently fired head coach Kevin Stefanski and hired former Baltimore Ravens offensive coordinator Todd Monken.

General manager Andrew Berry said the decision came after extensive discussions about the team's future direction. "As discussions intensified we were stuck at a legitimate crossroads: do we hold on to a truly generational player who has become the identity of our team, or do we make the difficult decision that we think is best for the organization over the long run?" Berry stated.

To make the trade possible, the Browns and Garrett modified his contract in March, deferring option payments over the 2026-28 seasons. A payment that was originally due on March 28 was moved to near the start of the regular season.

The Rams acquired a defensive player who immediately strengthens their pass-rushing unit. They also lost to the eventual champion Seattle Seahawks in last season's NFC Championship Game. The team has a history of making aggressive moves, including trading Jared Goff to acquire Matthew Stafford in 2021. Stafford led Los Angeles to a Super Bowl victory in his first season.

In return, the Browns received Verse, a 25-year-old linebacker who was the Rams' first-round pick in 2024. Verse earned Defensive Rookie of the Year honors and was selected to the Pro Bowl this past season with 12 career sacks and 22 tackles for loss. Cleveland also received a 2027 first-round selection, a 2028 second-round pick, and a 2029 third-round selection.

The new agreement addresses growing concerns about artificial intelligence in the entertainment industry by establishing specific conditions under which producers can use synthetic actors. These provisions represent a significant negotiation point between the union and studios as technology continues to reshape film and television production.

Beyond the AI protections, the contract consolidates the union's dual pension fund structure. This merger streamlines the pension system for members, combining what were previously two separate financial arrangements into a unified framework. The consolidation is expected to simplify administration and potentially improve oversight of retirement benefits for performers.

The ratification concludes a negotiation process between SAG-AFTRA and the major studios over contractual terms governing how film and television productions operate. The discussions centered on balancing the industry's desire to adopt new technology with the union's need to protect members' livelihoods and working conditions.

The AI provisions are particularly noteworthy given the ongoing debates throughout Hollywood about how artificial intelligence should be integrated into creative work. By establishing guidelines for synthetic actors, the contract attempts to create a framework that allows technological innovation while maintaining protections for union members.

With this ratification, the union and studios have established a path forward for the next four years of cooperation. The agreement demonstrates an effort to address contemporary industry challenges while maintaining the fundamental relationship between labor and management in film and television production.

The modest turnout for the ratification vote reflects broader patterns in union voting, where participation rates often fall below 50 percent of eligible members. Nevertheless, the overwhelming support among those who did vote indicates that the negotiating team's agreement resonates with the active membership.
