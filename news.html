Yesterday, 2:18 PM ET

California lettuce farmers are plowing under fields of healthy crops as demand for fresh greens collapses in response to a nationwide cyclospora outbreak. The parasite, which causes severe gastrointestinal illness, has been detected in at least 34 states, with Michigan reporting more than 5,000 confirmed cases alone.

The outbreak has been traced to Taylor Farms, a major produce supplier. The company recalled shredded iceberg lettuce grown at its facility in Guanajuato, Mexico, distributed across 27 states as recently as Thursday. Taylor Farms products reach major retailers including Costco, Target, and Walmart, as well as organic brands like Earthbound Farm. The FDA has also identified contaminated products served at Taco Bell locations in Indiana, Kentucky, Michigan, Ohio, and West Virginia, with more than 1,600 cases linked to the same product.

The scale of this outbreak far exceeds typical summer cyclospora cases. Last year, the entire US saw only 249 confirmed cases total. This year's numbers represent a dramatic spike, though health officials warn that actual infections may continue rising for at least another two weeks.

The destruction of ready-to-harvest lettuce represents a significant financial loss for growers who invested months of labor and resources into the crops. Fear of contamination has driven shoppers away from commercial lettuce, despite the outbreak being traced to a specific supplier and growing region.

Cyclospora is a parasite that contaminates food through contact with fecal matter, often via contaminated water used for irrigation or washing. The illness it causes includes severe diarrhea, stomach cramps, nausea, and fatigue that can last for weeks without treatment.

The economic impact extends beyond individual farms. The crisis has disrupted the supply chain for fresh greens, affecting distributors, retailers, and food service operations. Taco Bell was the first restaurant to announce it would stop serving produce in several affected states. The outbreak arrives during peak growing season for lettuce in California, which produces the majority of fresh greens consumed in the United States.

The recall has created confusion among consumers and retailers. Taylor Farms did not specify the brand names of recalled products or where they were served and sold, complicating efforts to identify contaminated items. The FDA has warned that "additional implicated brands, restaurants, retailers, or distribution channels may be identified as the investigation continues."

This is not Taylor Farms' first serious food safety incident. In 2013, the company was linked to a cyclospora outbreak at restaurants including the Olive Garden and Red Lobster. In October 2024, an E. coli outbreak linked to McDonald's Quarter Pounders was traced to the producer's slivered onions.

Public health authorities continue investigating the source while the industry awaits clarity on which products remain safe.

Yesterday, 4:26 PM ET

A federal judge rejected xAI's legal challenge to Minnesota's ban on applications that create fake nude images, allowing the state restriction to move forward. The decision represents a significant victory for state regulators attempting to address harms created by artificial intelligence technology.

The Minnesota law targets nudify apps, which use AI to digitally remove clothing from photos of people without their consent. The state legislature passed the measure in response to growing concerns about nonconsensual intimate imagery generated through AI. xAI filed a lawsuit seeking to block enforcement of the ban, but the court denied the company's request for a preliminary injunction.

The denial of xAI's request means Minnesota can proceed with implementing and enforcing penalties against developers and distributors offering nudify technology within the state. The ruling reflects broader efforts by state governments to regulate AI applications they consider harmful, particularly those that create deepfake content or synthetic intimate imagery.

The case highlights ongoing tensions between technology companies and state regulators over AI regulation. Several states have already moved to restrict deepfake technology and nonconsensual synthetic media. Minnesota joins these jurisdictions in establishing legal frameworks specifically targeting AI-enabled image manipulation.

Advocates for the ban have pointed to the disproportionate impact of such apps on women and minors. Nonconsensual intimate imagery created through AI tools has become an increasing concern for lawmakers and privacy advocates nationwide. The technology allows bad actors to create convincing fake nude images of real people, often without their knowledge or permission.

The judge's decision to uphold the ban suggests courts are willing to support state efforts to regulate AI in ways that protect individuals from specific harms. This ruling comes as the Trump administration and state governments continue debating the appropriate scope of AI regulation and who should control such policy.

xAI, owned by billionaire Elon Musk, has not publicly detailed the specific legal arguments it made against Minnesota's nudify app ban. The company's challenge apparently focused on constitutional or regulatory grounds, but the court found those arguments insufficient to block the law's enforcement.

The outcome may influence other states considering similar restrictions on AI-generated intimate imagery. As more jurisdictions implement their own AI regulations, technology companies will likely face additional legal battles over restrictions on their tools and services.

The ruling allows Minnesota to begin enforcing the law while any further legal proceedings continue. State officials have yet to fully detail how they will implement enforcement mechanisms or what penalties app developers and distributors will face for violating the ban.
