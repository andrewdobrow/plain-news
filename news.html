A few minutes ago

President Trump said his administration is exploring the possibility of acquiring equity stakes in leading artificial intelligence companies as a way to generate government profit from the sector's growth.

The proposal would involve direct federal investment in exchange for ownership shares in firms developing advanced AI systems. Trump is scheduled to meet with AI company leaders as soon as next week to discuss the plan, according to reports from multiple news outlets.

The discussions represent a notable shift in how the federal government might approach the rapidly expanding AI industry. By taking equity positions rather than simply regulating the sector, the administration would position itself to benefit financially from the companies' success while maintaining some degree of influence over their operations.

The meetings with OpenAI and other major AI developers mark an unusual convergence of political perspectives. The idea of public ownership stakes in the AI sector has drawn support from across the political spectrum, with both Trump and Senator Bernie Sanders now advocating for forms of government involvement in the industry.

This approach differs from traditional regulatory frameworks that focus on oversight and compliance. Instead, it would make the federal government a direct stakeholder in the financial performance of these companies. The profit-sharing model suggests the administration views AI development as both a strategic priority and a potential revenue source.

The timing of these meetings reflects growing attention to AI's role in the economy and national security. As these companies continue to develop increasingly powerful systems, questions about government involvement have intensified. Some policymakers argue that direct investment would give the government a seat at the table in shaping how these technologies are developed and deployed.

The proposal also raises questions about potential conflicts of interest and the appropriate role of government in private sector investment. Critics might argue that taking equity stakes could compromise regulatory independence, while supporters contend that government participation ensures public interests are protected as the technology advances.

The meetings scheduled for next week will likely provide clarity on which companies the administration is targeting and what specific terms might be under consideration. The discussions could set a precedent for how governments engage with major technology companies going forward.

Both OpenAI and other AI firms will need to evaluate how accepting government investment might affect their operations, governance structures, and ability to operate independently. The outcome of these negotiations could influence the broader landscape of AI development in the United States and shape policy approaches globally.

The convergence of Trump and Sanders around some form of public ownership in AI, despite their general political differences, suggests this issue may have genuine bipartisan appeal among policymakers concerned about ensuring the public benefits from transformative technologies.

Canada has implemented an import ban on cattle from Texas after a second case of screwworm was confirmed in the state. The discovery prompted Texas to declare a state of disaster as officials work to contain the outbreak.

Screwworm is a parasitic fly larva that burrows into the flesh of warm-blooded animals, including livestock and humans. The parasite can cause severe tissue damage and death without treatment. The United States successfully eradicated screwworm in the 1960s through a sustained campaign, making any reappearance a serious concern for agricultural and public health officials.

The identification of a second infected calf in Texas triggered the state's disaster declaration. This action allows authorities to mobilize additional resources and implement emergency measures to contain the outbreak and prevent further spread. The appearance of a second case suggests the parasite may have established a foothold in the state, raising concerns about broader contamination.

Canada's import ban protects its own cattle industry from the parasite. The restriction will impact Texas ranchers who export cattle across the border, adding economic pressure to an industry already facing containment costs and potential losses. Live cattle trade between the United States and Canada represents a significant portion of cross-border agricultural commerce.

Federal and state veterinary officials are working to determine the source of the infestation and identify how many animals may have been exposed. The response includes quarantines, inspections of nearby herds, and treatment protocols for affected animals. The speed and effectiveness of containment efforts will determine whether the outbreak remains localized or spreads to other parts of Texas and neighboring states.

Screwworm has been absent from the United States for decades due to eradication efforts. Its reappearance in Texas marks a significant development for the livestock industry, which depends on disease-free herds to maintain trade relationships with major trading partners like Canada. The parasite typically enters livestock through open wounds or natural body openings.

The economic consequences of the outbreak extend beyond individual ranchers. Trade restrictions can cascade through the supply chain, affecting processors, exporters, and consumers. Both countries have established protocols for disease management, but the sudden discovery of multiple cases has triggered precautionary measures.

Veterinary teams are collecting samples and conducting epidemiological investigations to understand how the parasite entered Texas. The findings will inform whether additional restrictions are necessary and when trade may resume. Officials anticipate that aggressive containment and treatment efforts will be essential to controlling the outbreak and restoring confidence in Texas cattle exports.

The screwworm outbreak represents a test of cross-border agricultural coordination between the United States and Canada. Both nations maintain strict animal health standards to protect their industries and ensure food safety.

The Justice Department told a federal court that the Trump administration's nearly $1.8 billion anti-weaponization fund will not continue, marking the effective end of the compensation program. Two DOJ attorneys filed the announcement in the Eastern District of Virginia while also requesting the court reject a challenge brought by a former federal prosecutor and several others.

The fund was established to compensate individuals who claimed they were targeted by federal law enforcement for political reasons. The compensation program had become a signature initiative of the Trump administration's efforts to address what it described as politicization of federal agencies.

The court filing provided no explanation for why the administration decided to discontinue the fund. The Justice Department's lawyers combined the announcement with their request to dismiss the legal challenge, suggesting the lawsuit may now be moot given the fund's termination.

The decision affects the nearly $1.8 billion that had been allocated for the program. The former federal prosecutor and other plaintiffs who brought the challenge had contested aspects of how the fund was structured or administered, though details of their specific objections were not provided in available filings.

The anti-weaponization fund represented one of the Trump administration's responses to longstanding complaints from supporters about federal investigations and prosecutions. Its termination signals a policy shift, though the administration has not publicly stated what prompted the change or whether any alternative measures are being considered.
