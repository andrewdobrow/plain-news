12:58 PM ET

The United States and Iran are expected to hold a virtual meeting on Sunday to electronically sign a memorandum of understanding that will extend their ceasefire by 60 days, reopen the Strait of Hormuz, and launch negotiations over Iran's nuclear program. The agreement comes after nearly three months of negotiations between the two nations, with mediation from Pakistan, Qatar, Egypt, and Turkey.

President Trump confirmed the signing on Saturday afternoon, writing on Truth Social that "The Deal is scheduled to get signed tomorrow." Trump described the current relationship with Iran as "a much different and better one than previous Administrations have had." The memorandum is intended to end the regional conflict and potentially stabilize global energy markets.

However, Iran's foreign ministry has cast doubt on the timeline. According to BBC News, Iranian officials said an exact date for signing has not been decided, stating it "will not be tomorrow."

The agreement has drawn criticism from some members of Congress. Democratic Representative Seth Moulton of Massachusetts told MS NOW on Saturday that the reported details constitute a terrible deal. "It's basically a surrender document from Donald Trump to the supreme leader of Iran," Moulton said, reflecting bipartisan concerns about the terms being negotiated with Tehran.

The blocked Strait of Hormuz has created significant global pressure for resolution. British Foreign Secretary Yvette Cooper chaired a virtual summit attended by more than 40 countries to discuss measures to pressure Iran into reopening the strait. Cooper said Iran's actions were affecting nations worldwide through rising petrol prices, mortgage rates, jet fuel availability, fertiliser supplies to Africa, and gas shipments to Asia.

The World Bank has predicted that continued blockage could push 9 million people into food insecurity. The strait carries between 10 and 25 percent of the world's oil and gas supplies.

Australia's Foreign Minister Penny Wong participated in discussions about coordinated diplomatic efforts to reopen the route. She stated that "Iran is deliberately inflicting economic pain on communities worldwide, including the Indo-Pacific, with the costs borne disproportionately by the most vulnerable."

Conservative Party leader Kemi Badenoch said that Trump should not abandon "a mess that he's made" in the Middle East by leaving other countries to reopen the strait. She argued that if Trump started the war, he should help resolve it.

Meanwhile, President Trump has claimed the US has been conducting a "secret mission" in the strait of Hormuz to help bypass Iran's blockade, asserting that hundreds of tankers have escaped with their transmitters off.

Interior Secretary Doug Burgum attributed rising gasoline prices to state policies rather than disruptions in the strait, telling Fox News that gas price variations stem largely from state policy and taxes rather than underlying fundamentals.

12:45 PM ET

Anthropic has announced it will disable its most advanced artificial intelligence models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, following a US government directive that bars foreign nationals from accessing the systems. The company received an export control order from the government but was not given specific details about the national security concerns behind the decision.

The restriction marks a significant intervention in the commercial AI sector. The government's action came after concerns emerged about the models' potential to identify software vulnerabilities and enable cyberattacks. The UK's AI Security Institute had previously warned that Mythos represented a "step up" in cyber-threat capability compared to earlier models, noting it could complete complex multi-step attacks that would normally require days of work by human professionals.

News of the ban followed reports of unauthorized access to the Mythos model. A small group of people gained access through a third-party vendor environment where one user worked as a contractor for Anthropic. According to Bloomberg, the group accessed the model using methods employed by cybersecurity researchers, though they had not attempted to run cyberattacks with the system.

Anthropic has expressed frustration with the government's decision. "We disagree that the finding of a narrow potential jailbreak should be cause for recalling a commercial model deployed to hundreds of millions of people," the company stated in a blog post. The company indicated it had received the directive without being given specific details of the security concerns that prompted it.

The move reflects growing US government focus on preventing advanced AI capabilities from reaching foreign adversaries. Officials have increasingly treated cutting-edge AI technology similarly to sensitive military or dual-use technologies subject to export controls. The restrictions align with broader efforts to control access to technology that could have national security implications.

The ban creates uncertainty for international businesses and researchers who had access to or were planning to use these advanced AI tools. Anthropic has not indicated whether foreign access might be restored in the future or what timeline might govern any potential reversal of the restrictions.

The decision could establish a precedent for how the US regulates access to advanced AI systems. Other companies developing similar technologies are likely watching to determine whether this represents a one-time intervention or signals the beginning of broader regulatory frameworks governing AI exports.

Industry observers have noted the irony that Anthropic's own safety research and transparency about the models' capabilities may have contributed to the government's decision to restrict them. The company's willingness to highlight security risks appears to have prompted official action rather than leading to collaborative solutions with regulators.

Tyra Banks filed a defamation lawsuit against Netflix on Saturday, claiming the streaming service manipulated her interview for the documentary "Reality Check: Inside America's Next Top Model." Banks alleges her words were edited in ways that misrepresented her statements and presented her in a false light.

The lawsuit names Netflix, 89 Blocks Holdings, EverWonder Studio, Netflix Music, and co-directors Mor Loushy and Daniel Sivan as defendants. Banks is pursuing charges of false light, defamation by implication, breach of contract, and false endorsement.

According to co-director Mor Loushy, Banks was told the documentary would proceed with or without her participation. She agreed to participate in a four-hour interview to share her perspective, intending to express her commitment to making modeling more inclusive. However, Banks received no advance questions, had no approval over how she was presented, and could only view the final series alongside the general public.

The documentary has proven to be a major success for Netflix, drawing more than 14 million views in its first week and topping the platform's English-language charts. However, the release has intensified criticism of Banks and the reality show she created and hosted from 2003 to 2018. Former contestants and judges have expressed outrage at what they perceive as Banks's failure to take adequate responsibility for the show's documented problems, including body-shaming and inadequate duty of care.

Former contestant Brittany Brower called the series "triggering" and criticized Banks for not taking accountability for how contestants were "completely used." Another former contestant, Brittany Corinne Hatch, described Top Model as "a systemic labor violation and a psychological experiment."

Even Banks's former judges have been critical. Creative director Jay Manuel said he was disappointed by Banks's lack of vulnerability, noting what he observed was "Television Tyra." Catwalk coach J Alexander, who suffered a stroke in December 2022, has not received a visit from Banks, though she did send a text message.

During the documentary, Banks acknowledged going "too far" during a famous outburst at contestant Tiffany Richardson in cycle four, saying "You know, I lost it." However, Richardson has disputed Banks's characterization, accusing her of being a bully both on and off camera.

Some supporters have emerged, including Isis King, the show's first transgender contestant, who said she cannot downplay the positive impact Top Model had on her life.

The lawsuit represents a significant legal challenge for Netflix and the production companies involved. Defamation cases involving edited interviews often turn on whether the final product substantially altered the meaning of statements or created false implications. Banks is seeking damages and other relief through the court system.
