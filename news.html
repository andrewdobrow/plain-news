Trump Extends Iran Ceasefire While Threatening Economic Pressure

President Trump announced an extension of a ceasefire with Iran on Tuesday, pausing military action while demanding Iranian leaders submit a unified peace proposal. The announcement came after weeks of escalating tensions and failed diplomatic efforts between the two nations.

Trump stated on his Truth Social platform that he was extending the ceasefire "until such time as their leaders and representatives can come up with a unified proposal." He credited requests from Pakistan's Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif and other officials in his decision to delay military strikes. However, Trump emphasized that the U.S. military would "remain ready and able" and that the blockade on Iranian ports would continue.

The decision marked a sharp reversal from Trump's earlier statements. Hours before announcing the extension, Trump said he "expected to be bombing" and that the military was "raring to go." The administration has threatened to target Iranian power stations and civilian infrastructure in any renewed attacks.

Iran's response has been dismissive. Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, speaker of the Iranian parliament and the regime's lead negotiator, rejected the ceasefire extension. His adviser, Mahdi Mohammadi, wrote on social media that Trump's move was "a ploy to buy time for a surprise strike" and that "the time for Iran to take the initiative has come."

The dispute centers on several key issues. Iran has insisted the U.S. must end its blockade of Iranian ports before negotiations can resume. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent warned that the blockade would continue, stating that within days Iranian oil storage facilities would be full and Iranian wells would shut down.

Trump has also announced plans for what he calls "economic D-Day" against Iran, declaring it "the most crushing economic operation ever taken against any country." Bessent has promised to announce "the toughest sanctions in history" against Iran. The administration has threatened to target any country, business, or banking institution that supports Iran economically.

The ongoing conflict has disrupted global energy markets. Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz, a critical shipping lane through which about a fifth of the world's oil and gas passes. The International Energy Agency called the combined effects of this conflict and Russia's war with Ukraine "the biggest crisis in history" for global energy markets. Oil prices have risen above ninety dollars per barrel as a result.

Pakistan has emerged as a key mediator in the dispute. Prime Minister Sharif thanked Trump for the ceasefire extension and pledged to continue efforts toward "a negotiated settlement of the conflict."

The first round of talks in Islamabad ten days earlier ended without agreement on reopening the strait or other key issues. Iran's foreign minister has rejected U.S. threats, calling them "desperate" and insisting that Iran will not negotiate "under the shadow of threats."

A rapidly spreading wildfire in Nevada has forced nearly 90,000 residents to leave their homes as flames advance toward Reno. The fire, named the Hawk fire, has burned more than 15,000 acres and remains completely uncontained as of Sunday.

The evacuation zone has expanded to the edge of the University of Nevada campus as strong winds, low humidity, and dry vegetation accelerate the blaze's spread. Of those ordered to evacuate, 42,000 are under strict evacuation orders while an additional 45,000 are in the evacuation warning zone and encouraged to leave immediately.

"If you have been told to evacuate, please don't wait!" Reno Mayor Hillary Schieve said in a statement on social media. "Structures have already been burned in this fire and your life is more important than a building. Evacuating before the fire arrives also allows our firefighters to focus their efforts on battling the blaze."

The fire was human-caused, though authorities have not released details about how it started as investigations continue. Three civilians and three first responders have been injured, and several structures have been destroyed.

Governor Joe Lombardo declared a state of emergency in Washoe County and mobilized the Nevada National Guard to support response efforts. Two helicopter crews have been deployed for aerial firefighting operations, while 60 troops have been assigned to protect evacuated neighborhoods. Around 400 personnel from local, state, and federal agencies are working on the incident, including ground crews and air resources.

The fire's rapid growth has challenged firefighting efforts. Washoe County Sheriff Darin Balaam described how changing wind patterns sent flames in different directions, making conditions "extremely confusing." On Saturday night, the fire moved exceptionally fast, jumping over fire trucks and complicating efforts for responders to reach hotspots while residents evacuated.

The expanding fire has caused widespread disruption. Nearly 10,000 homes and businesses in Washoe County lost power on Sunday. At least 20 road closures were in effect, including portions of US Route 395, a major north-south highway. The Reno-Stead airport closed to general aviation but remained open to firefighting aircraft. The University of Nevada's Reno campus remained just outside the evacuation zone with an enrollment of about 24,000 students.

Emergency officials opened the Reno-Sparks Convention Center to evacuees, though pets were not permitted there. Small and large animals were directed to two separate locations. Casinos in Reno offered discounted hotel rooms to those evacuated.

Authorities also monitored a county detention facility located several blocks from the evacuation warning zone's outer edge, with plans in place for potential evacuation if conditions worsen. The Hawk fire follows three other wildfires that forced more than 13,000 residents to evacuate earlier in August.

The Dutch Data Protection Authority is imposing a fine of 825 million euros on Uber for violations related to its automated driver suspension system. The penalty represents the second largest enforcement action issued under the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation.

The fine addresses concerns about how Uber processed personal data through its automated systems for suspending drivers. The Dutch authority concluded that the company's practices violated GDPR requirements, which mandate strict protections for personal data and impose limits on fully automated decision-making that significantly affects individuals.

Uber operates across Europe with a network of drivers who work through its app-based platform. The company's automated suspension system has been a point of contention, as drivers can lose access to the platform and their income through decisions made without direct human review. The GDPR requires companies to provide meaningful information about automated decision-making and give individuals the right to contest such decisions.

The 825 million euro penalty places this enforcement action behind only a previous fine issued to a major technology company. GDPR allows regulators to fine companies up to four percent of their annual global revenue for serious violations. European data protection authorities have increasingly scrutinized how companies use automated systems to make consequential decisions about individuals.

Uber has faced regulatory challenges across multiple European jurisdictions over its employment practices and data handling. The company must now address the Dutch authority's findings or potentially appeal the decision through European courts. The penalty adds to mounting pressure on technology platforms to provide greater transparency and human oversight in automated systems that affect workers and users.

Coco Gauff defeated Jessica Pegula 6-2, 6-4 in the Cincinnati Open final to claim her first title of 2026, arriving at the US Open with significant momentum after one of her smoothest tournament runs.

The victory holds particular significance given Gauff's struggles exactly one year earlier. At the 2025 Cincinnati Open, the American's serve had become a major liability, with double faults plaguing her matches and undermining her confidence in her overall game. That loss to Jasmine Paolini prompted a dramatic decision: Gauff hired biomechanics expert Gavin MacMillan to completely rebuild her serve from the ground up, beginning just before last year's US Open.

The timing of that overhaul was challenging. Making such a fundamental change to her technique while preparing for her most important tournament of the year drew considerable scrutiny. During her second round match at the US Open last year, Gauff cried on court as she grappled with the transformation. Over the following twelve months, she continued to struggle with double faults as she worked through the extensive biomechanical changes.

Now, Gauff has finally found confidence with her serve, and the improvement has transformed her overall performance. "I think it was a long journey, but I'm happy," she said after winning Cincinnati. "Last year I was frustrated with my game and felt like I was declining. It's not something any player wants to feel. You always want to feel like you're getting better. I made sudden decisions that most people probably wouldn't do before a slam, but I think it's worth it. It's night and day, the player I am from last year to now."

In Cincinnati, Gauff demonstrated the benefits of her serve overhaul. After dropping her first set against Liudmila Samsonova, she won ten consecutive sets to reach the final. Known for her dramatic three-set victories, Gauff deliberately sought to finish matches in straight sets this week to preserve energy before the US Open. In the final, she repeatedly hit first serves above 120 miles per hour and aggressively moved forward when opportunities presented themselves.

Her defensive excellence also proved decisive against Pegula. The third seed struggled to find answers to Gauff's mobility and court coverage. "It's just so hard when you play somebody that moves the way she does," Pegula said. "She's the best mover on tour by far. The way she's able to defend out of the corners is better than anybody else."

Pegula's path to the final was noteworthy in its own right. She won three consecutive three-set matches, including a victory over defending champion Iga Swiatek, positioning herself as another in-form American heading to the US Open.

Arthur Fils captured the men's title, defeating Frances Tiafoe 6-3, 1-6, 6-0 to claim his first Masters 1000 title. The 22-year-old French player is now ranked number 11 in the world following his breakthrough victory.

Shelley Fabares, the Emmy-nominated actress known for her roles in The Donna Reed Show and the sitcom Coach, has died at age 82. Fabares became a household name playing Mary Stone on The Donna Reed Show, a role she held for five years that launched her career in both television and film.

Fabares appeared in several Elvis Presley films during the 1960s, including Girl Happy, Spinout, and Clambake, becoming part of Hollywood's musical film tradition. Her work opposite Presley helped establish her as a leading actress during that era. She also pursued a music career, releasing recordings that found success on the charts.

In later years, Fabares found renewed television success as Christine Armstrong on the long-running sitcom Coach, which aired from 1989 to 1997. Her performance on the series earned her two Emmy nominations for Outstanding Lead Actress in a Comedy Series. The role introduced her to a new generation of viewers and demonstrated her range as a comedic actress.

Fabares also appeared in other television series throughout her career, including roles on One Day at a Time and various guest appearances on popular shows. She was married to actor Mike Farrell, best known for his role on MAS*H, since 1984. The couple became known for their work together on various humanitarian causes.

Her career spanned more than four decades in entertainment, moving from 1950s wholesome television roles through the evolving landscape of Hollywood into the 1990s. Fabares represented a bridge between different eras of American television, maintaining relevance across multiple generations of viewers.
