3:45 PM ET

Workers removed former President Donald Trump's name from the Kennedy Center's facade in the early hours of Saturday, following a federal judge's order and after the venue missed a Friday deadline to complete the task.

The removal came after US District Judge Christopher Cooper ruled that Trump's name must be taken down from the performing arts center in Washington DC. The work began shortly after 11:59pm Friday, when the deadline passed. An appeals court in Washington DC had rejected an emergency appeal filed by Justice Department lawyers on Friday seeking to pause the removal.

Judge Cooper's 94-page opinion stated that the Kennedy Center's founding statute makes clear the venue is named for President John F. Kennedy and cannot bear another formal name without congressional action. "Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name, and only Congress can change it," Cooper wrote. The ruling also temporarily blocked the venue from closing for renovations, stopping a two-year "revitalization project" that Trump's handpicked board had approved.

The facade previously read "The Donald J Trump and The John F Kennedy Memorial Center for the Performing Arts." Trump's name has now been fully removed from the building's exterior.

Before the physical removal, the Kennedy Center had already taken steps to comply with the court's broader order. The center's general counsel sent a memo to employees directing them to change email signatures, letterheads, and other documents to reflect the name "The John F Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts" or simply "Kennedy Center." Additional changes to templates, forms, signage, brochures, and website pages were required by June 12.

The removal of Trump's name from the website came days before that deadline, according to reports from the Washington Post.

Trump responded to the judge's decision with a lengthy statement on social media last month, saying he had no interest in what he called a "hopeless journey into NEVER NEVER LAND." The Justice Department's subsequent emergency appeal attempted to stay Cooper's removal order but was unsuccessful.

The Kennedy Center, which opened to the public in 1971, serves as the national performing arts center and the nation's cultural hub in Washington. Congress established it in 1958 as the National Cultural Center and renamed it to honor President Kennedy following his assassination in 1963. The venue hosts theatrical performances, concerts, and cultural events throughout the year while receiving federal funding alongside private donations and ticket sales.

The US government has ordered Anthropic to suspend access to its most advanced artificial intelligence models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, for all foreign nationals, the company announced. The directive came through an export control order citing national security concerns, though Anthropic said it was not given specific details about the security risks.

In response to the government order, Anthropic said it will "abruptly disable" these models for all users. The company had previously released Fable 5 to the public this week and was providing Mythos 5 to a limited group of companies including Apple and Goldman Sachs for testing purposes.

The timing of the restriction follows a series of security concerns about Mythos. Bloomberg reported that unauthorised users gained access to the model through a third-party contractor environment, raising alarms about how powerful AI technology could be protected from misuse. Anthropic confirmed it was investigating the breach claim.

Mythos has drawn particular attention from government authorities and international officials because of its potential to enable cybersecurity attacks. The UK's AI Security Institute warned that Mythos represents a "step up" from previous models in terms of the cyber-threat it poses. The institute said the model could carry out complex attacks requiring multiple steps and discover weaknesses in IT systems without human intervention, tasks that would normally take professionals days to complete.

The UK's AI minister, Kanishka Narayan, expressed concern about the model's ability to identify flaws in IT systems that hackers could exploit. Mythos was the first AI model to successfully complete a 32-step simulation of a cyber-attack created by the UK institute, solving it in three of ten attempts.

The restrictions mark a significant shift in Anthropic's global operations. The company had previously made its AI models available to users worldwide, but will now limit access to US-based users only. This decision affects how the company competes internationally against rivals like OpenAI and Google, which also operate advanced language models. By restricting Fable 5 and Mythos 5 to domestic users, Anthropic may lose international market share while attempting to comply with government guidance on AI access controls.

The move reflects broader government scrutiny of AI technology exports and foreign access to powerful American-developed systems. As artificial intelligence capabilities advance, US officials have expressed growing interest in controlling access to the most sophisticated models, particularly amid competition with other nations in AI development. Anthropic has not specified whether the restrictions are temporary or what conditions might allow foreign access to resume in the future.

The suit names Netflix, 89 Blocks Holdings, EverWonder Studio, Netflix Music, and co-directors Mor Loushy and Daniel Sivan as defendants. Banks is pursuing claims for false light, defamation by implication, breach of contract, and false endorsement. According to the filing, her participation was used in ways that violated the terms under which she agreed to be interviewed.

Banks created and hosted America's Next Top Model, which ran from 2003 to 2018 and became a defining force in reality television. The show launched modeling careers and influenced fashion competition programming for years. Netflix's documentary examined the series and its impact through interviews with former contestants and figures connected to the production.

The heart of the lawsuit concerns how Banks' words were presented in the final documentary. According to the filing, the editing created false implications that harm her reputation. Banks states that viewers received a misleading impression of her statements regarding the show and its controversies.

According to the documentary's directors, Banks was given no advance questions, no approval over her presentation, and could only watch the finished series when it premiered publicly. "She had no say, no influence, no anything," co-director Mor Loushy told Vanity Fair. The directors said Banks agreed to participate so she could tell her side of the story and express her intentions to make modeling more inclusive.

The documentary has been a major success for Netflix, drawing over 14 million views in its first week and topping the platform's English-language charts. However, the series sparked significant criticism from former contestants and judges, many of whom felt Banks failed to take adequate responsibility for the show's toxic elements, including body-shaming and poor duty of care.

During the documentary, Banks acknowledged going "too far" with a famous confrontation with contestant Tiffany Richardson in cycle four, saying "You know, I lost it." However, former judges and contestants expressed frustration with what they viewed as insufficient accountability. Former judge Jay Manuel said Banks was not "willing to be a little more vulnerable" and that viewers saw "Television Tyra" rather than genuine remorse.

The lawsuit raises important questions about documentary editing practices and the responsibilities filmmakers have toward interview subjects. Banks seeks damages for what she describes as harm to her professional reputation and violations of her participation agreement.

Netflix has not publicly responded to the lawsuit. The case represents a significant legal challenge for the streaming platform and the documentary's production team.
