Connecticut defeated Michigan 85-77 on Friday night at TD Garden in Boston, marking the earliest rematch between the previous season's men's national championship game participants since Duke and UConn met in November 1999. The fourth-ranked Huskies defeated the 16th-ranked Wolverines in a game that featured 59 free throws and lasted nearly two and a half hours. It was only the second time in the past 17 seasons that the men's title game was replayed the following season.

UConn guard Silas Demary Jr. and Braylon Mullins combined for 44 points, 11 rebounds and three steals to lead the Huskies. Mullins scored a game-high 23 points, while Demary contributed 21 points and has 12 assists with zero turnovers through two games this season. Both players struggled in last season's championship game, with Demary scoring just two points while playing on a severely sprained ankle. Demary told CBS Sports he had not watched footage of that loss and had the rematch circled since the defeat.

Connecticut shot 40.9 percent from three-point range and made 30 of 36 free throws. Michigan was led by sophomore Trey McKenney, who scored all of his team-high 22 points in the second half, but the Wolverines shot just 7-for-28 from beyond the arc. The loss was Michigan's first game against a ranked opponent this early in a season since 1993, and the first time the Wolverines failed to start a season 2-0 since 2000-01. Michigan coach Mike Boynton is in his first season after Dusty May left to take the Dallas Mavericks job during the offseason.

UConn coach Dan Hurley said the victory was critical given the Huskies' challenging schedule ahead, which includes games against Ohio State, Arizona and Duke later this month. The win extended Connecticut's streak to 10 consecutive regular-season, neutral-site victories over ranked opponents. Freshman Colben Landrew added eight rebounds in 22 minutes, and shooting guard Solo Ball, initially expected to miss the season with a broken wrist, could return within the next month.

12:28 PM ET

Richard Lloyd, the Television co-founder and guitarist whose intricate interplay with Tom Verlaine helped define punk-era guitar work on the band's 1977 debut Marquee Moon, has died at 75. Lloyd died Saturday morning at his home in Chattanooga, Tennessee, from cholangiocarcinoma, a bile duct cancer, his family said in a statement. His wife Sheila and his dog Willie Mays were with him. Lloyd was diagnosed with the cancer in March 2026.

Television stood apart from many punk contemporaries through the twin guitar attack of Lloyd and Verlaine, who died in 2023. While peers favored power chords, Lloyd and Verlaine developed an intricate interplay of riffs and licks inspired by jazz, art-rock, and jam bands. Lloyd described their partnership to The Stranger in 2015, saying he saw that Verlaine had something he was missing and vice versa. Lloyd said he brought the fleshing out of songs and a rock and roll heart, playing melody during verses while Verlaine sang.

On Marquee Moon, Lloyd pushed to double-track some of his guitar parts, insisting to Verlaine and co-producer Andy Johns that he could replicate intricate passages note-for-note to give the record depth beyond a live recording. He selectively used the technique across the album and laid down eight guitar tracks on "Guiding Light" to make it sound powerful and thick. Despite critical regard, Marquee Moon failed commercially, and Television broke up in 1978 after releasing Adventure. The band's output consisted of those two albums plus a 1992 reunion record.

Lloyd released nine solo albums between 1979 and 2018, toured regularly, worked as a session musician with artists including Matthew Sweet and John Doe, and gave guitar lessons. He rejoined Television for the 1992 reunion and again in the early 2000s but left before a 2007 tour marking Marquee Moon's 30th anniversary. Lloyd told The Stranger he got into music not for money but to become a renowned guitarist who made an irrevocable impact on rock and roll history.

Alternative cosmology model proposing 26.7 billion-year-old universe passes major observational tests

A University of Ottawa study finds a model requiring neither dark matter nor dark energy holds up against key astronomical data.

10:00 AM ET

An alternative cosmology model proposing the universe is 26.7 billion years old and requires neither dark matter nor dark energy has passed several major observational tests, according to a University of Ottawa study published in the journal Universe. Researchers Rajendra Gupta and Nikolaos Samaras tested the model, which suggests evolving fundamental constants could explain effects typically attributed to dark matter and dark energy.

The model's predictions matched observations for light element formation after the Big Bang, cosmic expansion measurements, and galaxy rotation curves from 175 galaxies. The researchers report it could also explain unusually mature early galaxies by allowing about 4.6 billion years for formation at cosmic dawn rather than the standard model's 300 million years.

Researchers convert wind turbine blades into graphene and silicon carbide using flash heating

A new method heats hard-to-recycle blades to 2,000°C in seconds, transforming them into valuable manufacturing materials.

2:02 AM ET

Researchers have developed a method to recycle wind turbine blades by subjecting them to flash joule heating, according to a study published in Chem Circularity. The process heats blades made from silica-containing fiberglass and carbon-rich polymer resin to approximately 2,000 degrees Celsius in seconds, converting them into silicon carbide and graphene.

At such high temperatures, carbon reacts with silica to form silicon carbide, a hard and heat-resistant material, while carbon also reorganizes into graphene, known for its strength and conductivity. The research team found that adding the broken-down blades to other materials before heating improved the usefulness of the resulting material for creating new rotor blades.

Small asteroid temporarily captured as Earth's mini-moon will break free by early 2027

A roughly one- to two-meter-wide asteroid named 2026 QQ8 has been orbiting Earth since April.

6:41 AM ET

Astronomers have confirmed that a small asteroid named 2026 QQ8 has been temporarily captured by Earth's gravity and is orbiting the planet as a mini-moon. The roughly one- to two-meter-wide space rock entered Earth's gravitational sphere of influence in April and was first observed in August by Hungarian astronomer Krisztián Sárneczky. The International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center confirmed it as a natural object on October 29 after follow-up observations ruled out an artificial origin.

The asteroid is expected to remain temporarily bound to Earth through December before breaking free and continuing its orbit around the Sun in early 2027. Its closest predicted distance during this period is about 568,000 kilometers, roughly one-and-a-half times the average Earth-Moon distance, and it poses no impact threat.
