India's first space-tech unicorn Skyroot Aerospace successfully launched its Vikram-1 rocket on Saturday, marking a significant milestone in the country's growing commercial space sector. The launch represents a step forward in India's ambitions to transform its position in the global space industry through private sector participation.

Skyroot Aerospace achieved unicorn status as a privately held startup valued at over one billion dollars, becoming the first Indian space technology company to reach that threshold. The Vikram-1 launch demonstrates the company's technical capabilities in rocket development and orbital delivery systems.

The successful flight comes as India expands its space program beyond government-led initiatives. Private companies like Skyroot are developing commercial launch capabilities that could provide lower-cost access to orbit for satellites and other payloads. The launch vehicle is designed to carry small satellites into various orbital configurations.

India has been building its space technology sector following regulatory reforms that opened the industry to private investment and operations. The government has encouraged commercial space ventures while maintaining its own robust program through the Indian Space Research Organisation, which has conducted missions to the Moon and Mars.

The Vikram-1 launch positions Skyroot among a growing number of private space companies worldwide developing small to medium-lift rockets for the commercial satellite market. The success of this mission could attract additional investment to India's space sector and demonstrate the country's capabilities in manufacturing and operating orbital launch systems.

SpaceX shares have declined below their initial public offering price of $135 per share, closing at $131.11 during Thursday trading. The drop marks a significant retreat from the company's peak market value of over $2.6 trillion in the first three days after its mid-June listing, which was the largest debut in stock market history.

The share price decline has affected major investors worldwide. Australian billionaire Gina Rinehart's company, Hancock Prospecting, purchased over $1 billion worth of SpaceX shares at the IPO. Based on the stock's peak value, Rinehart has experienced a paper loss of approximately $500 million, with an additional $30 million in losses following Thursday's close.

Elon Musk's personal net worth has fallen to $838 billion as of Thursday, according to Forbes. This represents a significant decline from when Musk briefly became the world's first trillionaire following the IPO announcement.

Short sellers have benefited substantially from the stock's decline. Bloomberg reported that investors betting against SpaceX have recorded profits of $3.88 billion as shares have fallen in seven of the last eight trading sessions. The company experienced its largest single-day drop on June 22 when it announced plans to issue billions in additional debt through bonds.

Despite the losses, major investors appear to be holding their positions for the long term. Hancock Prospecting has not indicated whether it has sold any of its stake. A spokesperson for the company declined to comment on whether it has bought or sold additional shares since the listing, though it has republished articles about its initial investment on its website.

Market analysts suggest that long-term investors view the decline as a temporary pullback in what they consider a growth opportunity. Tony Sycamore, a market analyst at IG Australia, noted that investors backing SpaceX were typically looking at a decade-long investment horizon rather than short-term gains.

The IPO generated unprecedented retail interest in Australia. CommSec, the country's largest retail share broker, received applications from 28,000 Australians seeking to purchase SpaceX shares, setting a record for applications. The company received three times more applications globally than it could accommodate. While CommSec declined to disclose exactly how many Australian applicants successfully purchased shares, the level of interest exceeded previous IPO records by a significant margin.

Market analysts attributed the recent stock decline to easing investor euphoria rather than fundamental company concerns. Some retail investors who participated in early trading likely sold at the peak to lock in profits, while others appear committed to holding their shares through the company's long-term development.

The 45-point output marked a career high for Clark and also set a new franchise record for the Fever. Her performance demonstrated her ability to excel across multiple statistical categories simultaneously. The game also saw Clark contribute four steals and two blocks, showcasing her defensive impact alongside her offensive dominance.

Clark's playmaking efficiency proved particularly remarkable. Distributing 10 assists while scoring 45 points requires exceptional court vision and the ability to create opportunities for teammates while maintaining offensive aggression. This combination had never been achieved before in WNBA history at such elite levels in a single contest.

The historic performance came during a period of heightened scrutiny surrounding Clark's professional career. Recent weeks have seen debates about officiating and physical play in the league, particularly following an incident involving Phoenix Mercury forward Alyssa Thomas, who hit Clark in the throat during a game. Thomas was later suspended for one game, though no foul was called at the time. The incident sparked broader conversations about player safety and fair officiating.

NBA Commissioner Adam Silver commented on the situation, saying that Clark has become a "political football" amid debates about officiating and physical play in the WNBA. Silver noted that the issues aren't largely about officiating itself, but rather broader concerns about how the league addresses these matters.

Beyond the record-setting performance, Clark also reached another milestone. She became the fastest player in WNBA history to make 200 career three-pointers, achieving this mark in just 74 games. This broke the previous record of 81 games set by Katie Smith.

Clark's transition from college basketball to the professional ranks has been closely watched by fans and media. Her college career at Iowa generated significant attention, and expectations followed her to Indiana. The historic performance provides concrete evidence of her impact at the professional level and her capacity to produce at volumes rarely seen in the league.

The Fever's victory over Seattle also carried playoff implications as teams position themselves for the postseason. Clark's ability to deliver in meaningful games adds another dimension to her value beyond the statistical milestone. The performance establishes a new standard for what constitutes an elite all-around offensive game in women's professional basketball.

Yesterday, 9:20 PM ET

Christopher Nolan has adapted Homer's ancient epic "The Odyssey" into a major cinematic undertaking, sparking widespread discussion about heroism, classical storytelling, and how timeless myths translate to modern audiences. The film, which reportedly cost $250 million, marks an ambitious pivot for the director following his Oscar-winning success with "Oppenheimer."

The project reunites Nolan with actor Matt Damon, who plays Odysseus, the King of Ithaca. The cast also includes Anne Hathaway as Penelope and Tom Holland as Telemachus. The story follows Odysseus's ten-year journey home after the Trojan War, filled with encounters involving monsters, gods, and supernatural obstacles that test both his physical endurance and emotional resilience.

Nolan has explained his motivation for tackling the ancient poem. "The Odyssey underpins almost all of cinema," he stated. "It's certainly there in every movie I've ever done, to a degree I never before realised." The director sees the narrative threads of Homer's epic reflected across filmmaking history, from the hero's journey structure found in everything from "The Wizard of Oz" to "Star Wars" to the trauma-haunted protagonists that populate his own filmography.

The decision to adapt "The Odyssey" emerged from a family discussion. According to Nolan's producing partner and wife, Emma Thomas, one of their children suggested the project. "All our kids are into classical mythology," Thomas explained. "And one of them, Oliver, really loves these stories. And Oliver said: 'What about The Odyssey? Nobody's really done that in a big movie form.'"

The film has generated considerable critical attention, with publications like The Guardian praising Nolan's ambition in bringing the Homeric legend to the screen. Critics have noted that the adaptation explores the psychological toll of war and the difficult process of homecoming, themes that resonate with contemporary audiences grappling with generational trauma and PTSD.

The question of Odysseus's heroism has become central to discussions surrounding the film. National Geographic consulted classicists to address what scholars argue audiences commonly misunderstand about the original epic and its protagonist. Experts have highlighted how ancient Greek conceptions of heroism differ significantly from modern interpretations, with Odysseus portrayed as a flawed character shaped by trauma and guilt rather than a purely noble figure.

The adaptation arrives at a significant moment culturally. Nolan has previously positioned himself as a champion of theatrical cinema over streaming content. By choosing to adapt one of Western literature's foundational texts at an unprecedented scale, the filmmaker appears to be making a statement about cinema's capacity to deliver transformative storytelling experiences.

The film's release marks the culmination of Nolan's most commercially and critically successful period, suggesting audiences and critics will closely scrutinize how this major gamble has paid off.

On Friday, American airstrikes struck bridges in Iran's southern Hormozgan province that serve as key transit points for Bandar Abbas, the nation's main port. The strikes killed at least seven people according to Iranian state television.

Additional US attacks targeted infrastructure at Chabahar port on the Gulf of Oman, where the military claimed the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps used facilities to coordinate attacks on vessels in the Strait of Hormuz. The campaign also struck electrical infrastructure and Iranshahr airport. Following the strikes on power facilities, Iran's energy ministry urged citizens to reduce electricity consumption as the power grid experienced strain, with the southern regions facing extreme heat compounded by damage to power infrastructure.

The strikes represent a significant escalation in scope. Human rights experts have warned that attacks on civilian infrastructure not used for military purposes could constitute war crimes. By Friday morning, Iranian health ministry spokesperson Hossein Kermanpour reported that renewed US strikes had killed at least 38 people and wounded more than 400.

The expanded campaign appears connected to President Donald Trump's stated intention to target Iranian infrastructure and power plants. Reports indicate Trump met with senior department officials this week to discuss an aerial campaign aimed at forcing Iran to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway that handles approximately one-fifth of the world's oil and gas supply before the conflict began.

The fighting has now entered its seventh consecutive day and has undermined an interim deal signed between the countries last month intended to keep the strait open for negotiations toward a permanent truce. Iran has shut the strait while the US reimposed a blockade of Iranian ports and ships as of Wednesday.

In response to Friday's strikes, the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps warned of a "devastating price" for countries hosting US military bases if attacks on infrastructure continued. Iran subsequently targeted multiple US allies across the region, striking Bahrain, Kuwait, Jordan, Oman, and Qatar. In Kuwait, strikes damaged a power and desalination plant that supplies approximately 90 percent of the nation's drinking water.

The fighting has disrupted global shipping significantly. Cargo shipments through the Strait of Hormuz dropped by nearly a quarter at the month's beginning. A tanker traveling near Oman came under attack on Friday, sustaining minor damage though no crew were injured. Iranian state media reported the US also struck an empty oil tanker docked at Kharg Island, Iran's primary oil export terminal.

The escalating violence has driven up fuel prices in the United States, with diesel reaching over five dollars per gallon and regular gasoline approaching four dollars, nearly a dollar and a quarter higher than a year ago.
