Republican senators have expressed dissatisfaction with how the Trump administration presented its emergency request for $70 billion in defense spending related to the Iran conflict. The lawmakers say the Pentagon must provide a clearer explanation of its military strategy before they can support the funding.

The criticism emerged after senators attended a briefing with the Vice President and Pentagon officials about the supplemental funding request. Even members of the president's own party, who traditionally back defense spending increases, have raised concerns about both the size of the request and how the money would be used.

The U.S.-Israel war on Iran, which launched in February, has already cost tens of billions of dollars. Since the operation began, hundreds of U.S. service members have been wounded and 18 have been killed. A tentative peace deal fractured in recent weeks, and the conflict continues as Trump approaches the November midterm elections, where the war has become a political issue.

Senators indicated they need more detailed information about the Pentagon's strategic goals in Iran and a clearer timeline for military operations before they can justify the spending to their constituents. The bipartisan concern suggests the administration may face difficulty getting quick approval of the funding package, even with Republican control of the Senate.

Democrats have been more vocal in their opposition. Senator Elizabeth Warren called Republicans "gelatinous blobs" who have "had their spines surgically removed," arguing they have surrendered Congress's constitutional authority to the president. Senator Chris Van Hollen introduced a war powers resolution directing Trump to withdraw military forces from Iran without explicit congressional approval, saying "ultimately, Congress will have to turn off the funding spigot for this war" and pledging he would "not vote for a dime for this illegal war."

Congress has already attempted to rein in Trump's military actions. The House passed a war powers resolution directing withdrawal from Iran on a 214-208 vote, with four Republicans joining all Democrats in support. However, the Senate resolution failed 49-50. An earlier Senate resolution passed in June, but it remains unclear how recent votes will proceed.

The war remains deeply unpopular with American voters. According to a Washington Post-Ipsos poll, only 29 percent of Americans approve of Trump's handling of the Iran situation, while 69 percent disapprove.

Van Hollen said Democrats should continue pressing for votes on the war powers resolution to hold Republican senators accountable. "I would rather see Republicans assert their constitutional duties and help us end the war now," he said, adding that Republican senators may eventually hear from their constituents on the matter.

Construction begins on border wall at Big Bend National Park

4:00 AM ET

Bulldozers have begun construction on border security infrastructure at Big Bend National Park in Texas, drawing fierce opposition from environmental groups, local business leaders, and elected officials. The project marks the first time the Department of Homeland Security has used its waiver authority to install border security infrastructure inside a national park.

The Trump administration waived numerous environmental and historical preservation laws to allow construction to proceed. The waived protections include the National Environmental Policy Act, the Endangered Species Act, the Clean Water Act, and the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, among others.

The current plan calls for a 17-mile vehicle barrier system in four separate locations along the park's river frontage, composed of steel rails and posts measuring 4 to 6 feet tall. The project also includes 205 miles of roads up to 24 feet wide equipped with detection technology, utility poles, lighting, and surveillance cameras. However, the broad waiver gives Customs and Border Protection the authority to build virtually any security infrastructure it wants, including a potential 30-foot steel bollard wall.

The 800,000-acre park draws around half a million visitors annually who come to hike, camp, stargaze, and float the Rio Grande. The area is home to several endangered species, a struggling bighorn sheep population, and extensive Native American rock art and petroglyphs.

U.S. Representative Lloyd Doggett, a Texas Democrat, criticized the project as wasteful in a region where unauthorized crossings are already rare. "Billions of taxpayer dollars are being wasted on this unnecessary project, as Big Bend's rugged mountains make illegal crossings nearly impossible, with crossings in the area accounting for under half a percentage point of all illegal border crossings nationwide last year," Doggett said.

Bob Krumenaker, former Big Bend National Park superintendent and head of the advocacy group Keep Big Bend Wild, expressed concern about the massive environmental impact. "You're looking at some of the most remote parts of a remote national park," he said, describing it as massive destruction.

Krumenaker also raised alarms about the waiver's implications. "Waiving the law undermines all credibility and makes them completely unaccountable to anyone. They don't care about the impact on the environment. If they have, say, a fuel spill, they're not subject to any laws." The waiver casts aside Clean Water Act and Clean Air Act protections, he noted.

Customs and Border Protection said in a statement that its infrastructure plans "in the areas adjacent to the Big Bend National Park and State Park are still in the planning stages, while CBP focuses on other higher priority locations."

Local businesses have organized a coordinated response, with owners pledging to refuse service to contractors involved in the wall project.

The strategic shift represents a long-term assessment by Gulf states that depe...

4:17 AM ET

Gulf oil exporters are investing heavily in new pipeline infrastructure designed to provide alternative export routes that bypass the Strait of Hormuz. These projects reflect growing concerns about the vulnerability of the critical waterway, which handles a substantial share of global oil shipments.

The strategic shift represents a long-term assessment by Gulf states that depending solely on the Strait of Hormuz carries unacceptable risks. Regional tensions, particularly involving Iran, have highlighted how any disruption to this single transit route could have severe economic consequences for both exporters and global energy markets.

Several Gulf nations have already begun constructing alternative pipeline systems that would transport oil across land to ports outside the strait. These infrastructure projects are expected to take several years to complete but would provide exporters with backup options if the strait becomes impassable due to military conflict, regional instability, or other emergencies.

The investments come at a time when energy security concerns have intensified globally. Gulf exporters recognize that even temporary shipping disruptions through the strait could trigger significant economic damage. The new pipeline infrastructure would ensure oil continues flowing despite regional conflicts or other unforeseen circumstances that might affect maritime routes.

The Strait of Hormuz has long been identified as a potential chokepoint for international energy markets. Any closure or major disruption in the area could threaten supplies to major importing nations worldwide. This vulnerability has prompted Gulf states to take proactive measures to reduce their reliance on this single transit point.

The infrastructure projects demonstrate that Gulf exporters are willing to commit substantial capital upfront to mitigate these risks. While expensive, the pipelines serve as insurance against potential future disruptions. They would also provide exporters with greater flexibility in managing oil shipments and accessing different international markets.

Industry experts note that these pipeline investments signal a significant strategic reassessment by Gulf nations regarding their export infrastructure. The projects indicate that policymakers view the security risks associated with the Strait of Hormuz as substantial enough to justify major capital expenditures on alternative systems.

The timing of these investments is notable regardless of current diplomatic developments. Even if a ceasefire materializes in existing regional conflicts, Gulf exporters have concluded that the long-term risks of relying exclusively on the Strait of Hormuz justify building diversified export infrastructure. This approach provides them with multiple pathways for transporting oil to international markets and reduces vulnerability to any single geographic chokepoint or geopolitical event.

3:32 AM ET

Amazon will use content from Twitch livestreams to train artificial intelligence models by default, prompting widespread criticism from the platform's streaming community. Users must actively opt out to prevent their content from being used in AI training, a reversal of standard opt-in practices common elsewhere in the technology industry.

The policy applies to video, audio, and chat content on Twitch, which Amazon acquired in 2014 for $970 million. Streamers discovered the change through updated terms of service that take effect immediately. The company characterized the practice as ongoing, suggesting Twitch content has already been used to train AI systems for an unspecified period before the disclosure.

Streamers expressed anger over the default opt-in approach, arguing they should have explicit control over how their creative work is used. Many creators rely on Twitch for income through subscriptions, donations, and advertisements, and view their streams as proprietary content. The backlash reflects broader tensions in the creator economy as technology companies seek training data for AI development while content producers demand compensation and control.

Twitch provided instructions for users who wish to opt out of AI training, though the process requires navigating account settings. The platform has not disclosed which specific AI products use Twitch data or whether Amazon's broader suite of AI services, including its cloud computing division's machine learning tools, benefit from the training material.

The controversy adds to growing scrutiny of how technology companies source training data for AI systems. Publishers, artists, and other content creators have increasingly challenged the practice of using copyrighted or user-generated material without explicit permission or payment. Amazon has not announced changes to the policy in response to the criticism.

The Trump administration waived numerous environmental and historical preservation laws to allow construction to proceed. The waived protections include the National Environmental Policy Act, the Endangered Species Act, the Clean Water Act, and the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, among others.

The current plan calls for a 17-mile vehicle barrier system in four separate locations along the park's river frontage, composed of steel rails and posts measuring 4 to 6 feet tall. The project also includes 205 miles of roads up to 24 feet wide equipped with detection technology, utility poles, lighting, and surveillance cameras. However, the broad waiver gives Customs and Border Protection the authority to build virtually any security infrastructure it wants, including a potential 30-foot steel bollard wall.

The 800,000-acre park draws around half a million visitors annually who come to hike, camp, stargaze, and float the Rio Grande. The area is home to several endangered species, a struggling bighorn sheep population, and extensive Native American rock art and petroglyphs.

U.S. Representative Lloyd Doggett, a Texas Democrat, criticized the project as wasteful in a region where unauthorized crossings are already rare. "Billions of taxpayer dollars are being wasted on this unnecessary project, as Big Bend's rugged mountains make illegal crossings nearly impossible, with crossings in the area accounting for under half a percentage point of all illegal border crossings nationwide last year," Doggett said.

Bob Krumenaker, former Big Bend National Park superintendent and head of the advocacy group Keep Big Bend Wild, expressed concern about the massive environmental impact. "You're looking at some of the most remote parts of a remote national park," he said, describing it as massive destruction.

Krumenaker also raised alarms about the waiver's implications. "Waiving the law undermines all credibility and makes them completely unaccountable to anyone. They don't care about the impact on the environment. If they have, say, a fuel spill, they're not subject to any laws." The waiver casts aside Clean Water Act and Clean Air Act protections, he noted.

Customs and Border Protection said in a statement that its infrastructure plans "in the areas adjacent to the Big Bend National Park and State Park are still in the planning stages, while CBP focuses on other higher priority locations."
