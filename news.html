European Union Fines Google $1 Billion for Competition Violations

A few minutes ago

The European Union imposed a 890 million euro fine on Google for violating competition rules under the bloc's Digital Markets Act. The penalty marks another significant regulatory action against the tech giant and addresses violations related to both its search engine and app store operations.

The European Commission, the EU's executive arm, determined that Google abused its market power in two distinct ways. First, Google gave priority to its own services such as shopping and hotel deals in search results, treating them more favorably than those of competing companies. Second, Google prevented app developers from directing consumers toward cheaper offers on websites or alternative app stores. The Commission split the fine accordingly, assigning 460 million euros for the search-related breach and 430 million euros for the app store violation.

The regulatory action comes as Google has already begun making changes to address these concerns. The company has started testing modifications to how it displays search results featuring its own services, including shopping and flight options. A senior EU official noted that these changes represent "substantial progress towards compliance" with the Commission's requirements.

Moving forward, Google must treat third-party services appearing in its search results in a "fair and non-discriminatory manner." The company must also allow app developers to make offers outside Google's app store and direct users toward alternative payment options. These measures are intended to create a more level playing field for competitors on Google's platforms.

A senior EU official stated that consumers will benefit directly from the decision, noting that search results will differ in Europe and Google will need to adapt its search engine going forward.

The fine reflects the EU's position as a global leader in technology regulation. The bloc has consistently challenged business practices it views as anticompetitive and has established itself through a series of antitrust actions against major American technology companies. Google has faced multiple penalties from European regulators over the past decade for various competition-related issues, making it one of the most heavily fined companies in EU antitrust history.

The timing of the fine drew attention due to broader trade tensions. The decision was issued just hours before temporary global tariffs against approximately 60 countries were set to expire. However, EU officials stated they had no advance knowledge of how the United States might react to the fine. One official emphasized that the EU had the "sovereign right" to regulate American technology companies within its own jurisdiction and that the timing was not connected to tariff concerns.

Google has the option to appeal the decision and can request interim measures, including a suspension of the penalty, through European courts. Any appeal process could take years to resolve.

5:00 AM ET

The Trump administration is pursuing nutrition education through medical schools even as it eliminated SNAP-Ed, a federal program that provided dietary guidance to low-income Americans. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. unveiled the medical school initiative this week, creating a stark contrast with the earlier decision to cut the community-based nutrition program.

Kennedy announced that 53 medical schools have voluntarily committed to a new nutrition education effort. Starting in autumn 2026, these institutions will provide 40 hours of nutrition education or its equivalent to medical students. Kennedy described the initiative as central to implementing the Make America Healthy Again agenda.

"This is how we implement the MAHA agenda," Kennedy said at the announcement event, calling it "a transformative breakthrough in medical education that will reshape the way we train doctors in our country."

The medical schools participating in the program must evaluate their current nutrition instruction levels, designate a faculty member to oversee nutrition education, and publish information about how they will meet the 40-hour requirement. Institutions span both Republican and Democratic-leaning states, including the University of Alabama at Birmingham, University of Florida, George Washington University, New York University, and Tufts University.

Several highly ranked universities declined to participate despite having respected medical schools. Brown University, Columbia University, and Cornell University chose not to join, even though they had previously reached funding-related agreements with the Trump administration.

Kennedy has also signaled broader intentions regarding food safety. Last week, he suggested the administration may seek to remove certain foods from the market if companies cannot demonstrate their safety. He specifically mentioned major coffee chains, stating the administration would ask them to provide safety data for high-sugar beverages marketed to teenagers.

The medical school initiative contrasts with the administration's elimination of SNAP-Ed, which provided nutrition education to millions of low-income Americans through community organizations, health departments, and educational institutions. The program offered cooking demonstrations, nutrition workshops, and educational materials to families receiving Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program benefits.

Many doctors and researchers have previously criticized Kennedy's positions, particularly regarding vaccines, as lacking scientific grounding. Health experts have noted that nutrition education represents a cost-effective intervention that can reduce long-term healthcare costs associated with diet-related chronic diseases.

The medical school push reflects a broader effort by the Trump administration to promote its priorities within American higher education, marking a departure from the country's traditional emphasis on academic independence. Kennedy's nutrition education initiative has managed to attract medical schools across diverse political landscapes, even as questions remain about how the medical focus aligns with the elimination of community-level nutrition assistance programs.

The disruptions have pushed Brent crude past $98 per barrel, approaching the $100 threshold not seen since the initial surge following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The Strait of Hormuz, which connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman, serves as a passage for roughly one-fifth of global oil supplies. Any sustained closure or disruption to shipping through the strait has immediate effects on energy markets worldwide.

The attacks represent an expansion of hostilities in the region. Houthi forces, backed by Iran, have previously targeted commercial shipping in the Red Sea as part of broader regional tensions. The reported fire near the Strait of Hormuz adds another flashpoint to an already volatile situation. Multiple vessels have now been affected across two key waterways that serve as vital arteries for global energy transport.

Rising oil prices carry immediate consequences for consumers and businesses. Transportation costs increase as fuel becomes more expensive, with those increases typically passed through to goods and services. The energy sector often serves as an early indicator of broader economic pressure, as sustained high oil prices can slow economic growth while accelerating inflation.

Markets are now watching whether prices will breach the $100 barrier and how long disruptions to shipping might continue. The situation remains fluid as Iran and the United States exchange threats and military posturing continues in the region. Energy traders are pricing in risk premiums as uncertainty about safe passage through these strategic waterways persists.

Trump administration signs nuclear cooperation deal with Saudi Arabia

The deal immediately generated criticism from nonproliferation advocates and Israel supporters who worry the technology could enable Saudi Arabia to develop nuclear weapons. The agreement represents a notable shift in U....

6:00 AM ET

The Trump administration has signed a nuclear cooperation agreement with Saudi Arabia that could eventually permit the kingdom to enrich uranium domestically. The deal immediately generated criticism from nonproliferation advocates and Israel supporters who worry the technology could enable Saudi Arabia to develop nuclear weapons.

The agreement represents a notable shift in U.S. nuclear policy toward Saudi Arabia. The U.S. Department of Energy announced that Secretary Chris Wright and Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman signed a "peaceful nuclear cooperation agreement" along with a "bilateral safeguards agreement." Under the deal, American companies will receive priority access to supply nuclear reactors and fuel to Saudi Arabia. The arrangement is expected to last for decades and could be worth billions of dollars to the U.S. nuclear industry.

The accord drew swift backlash from multiple quarters. Critics have highlighted the stark contrast between the administration's approach to Saudi Arabia and its stance on Iran. The U.S. is currently engaged in military conflict with Iran partly over uranium enrichment concerns, yet the Saudi deal explicitly permits the kingdom to potentially pursue enrichment activities after a joint study period.

Nonproliferation experts have expressed particular concern about safeguards in the agreement. The deal reportedly allows the Saudis to bar international inspectors from certain nuclear sites, which analysts warn could leave little oversight preventing a civilian nuclear program from becoming a covert weapons operation. Saudi Arabia, like Iran, is a signatory to the nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. The Saudi crown prince has previously stated his country would develop nuclear weapons if Iran did so.

Observers have noted the agreement is less stringent than a 2009 nuclear cooperation deal negotiated with the United Arab Emirates, another Gulf state. Some analysts suggest financial considerations may have influenced the administration's approach, as sales of nuclear reactors to Saudi Arabia could generate substantial revenue for American firms.

The timing of the announcement also proved controversial. It comes as the Trump administration seeks congressional funding for its ongoing military operations in the Middle East. Defense officials have requested billions in additional appropriations to sustain the campaign.

One notable element of the agreement is its separation from previous diplomatic conditions. An earlier version of a potential Saudi nuclear deal had been tied to Saudi Arabia normalizing relations with Israel. That requirement has now been dropped, with Saudi officials stating that recognition of Israel remains contingent on establishing "a credible and irreversible path to a Palestinian state."

House Democrats have called for congressional hearings on the agreement, signaling likely legislative scrutiny of the administration's approach to nuclear cooperation in the Middle East.
