Trump directs $700 million to coal industry under wartime powers

The announcement came during a White House ceremony where Trump received a bronze trophy fr...

Yesterday, 8:03 PM ET

President Trump signed an executive order directing the Department of Defense to enter long-term power purchase agreements with coal plants serving military installations and other critical facilities, marking the latest effort by his administration to revive the struggling coal sector.

The announcement came during a White House ceremony where Trump received a bronze trophy from the Washington Coal Club, an advocacy group with financial ties to the industry. James Grech, CEO of Peabody Energy, the largest coal company in the US, presented the award to Trump, stating: "We stand here today representing the thousands of coalminers across the country to express our deep gratitude to you, sir, for the actions you've taken to support our industry."

The event drew more than a dozen coal executives and miners, along with several Republican lawmakers and cabinet members, including EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin and Interior Secretary Doug Burgess, both known as coal advocates.

"We're going to be buying a lot of coal through the military now," Trump said at the ceremony. "Under our leadership, we're becoming a massive energy exporter. We're lifting up our hard-working American miners like nobody has ever done before."

In addition to the defense department purchases, the Department of Energy announced 175 million dollars in funding for six projects to modernize and extend the operational life of coal-fired power plants serving rural and remote communities in West Virginia, Ohio, North Carolina and Kentucky.

This executive order represents the administration's continued push to support coal production. In September, the White House announced it would open 13.1 million acres of public land to coal mining and allocate 625 million dollars for coal-fired power plants.

The coal industry has faced significant headwinds over recent decades. According to the Energy Information Administration, US coal production in 2023 fell to less than half its 2008 level. Coal plants generated only about 15 percent of US electricity in 2024, down from roughly 50 percent in 2000.

The administration's investment in coal represents a shift away from renewable energy sources and reflects the president's stated priorities regarding energy policy and American economic interests. The strategy signals a commitment to traditional fossil fuel production as a cornerstone of the administration's energy and industrial policy going forward.

These topics are likely to be central to next week's visit as well.

The outcome of the June meeting could shape the trajectory of North Korean diplomacy and its relationships with major powers in the coming months. How Xi and Kim address their bilateral relationship, economic ties, and regional security concerns may have far-reaching implications for stability in East Asia.

The 226-195 vote marks the second major foreign policy break between House Republicans and Trump within a week. The Ukraine aid and sanctions bill faced significant resistance from GOP leaders, who warned that the legislation would undermine negotiations they believed could achieve stronger results. Despite their objections, the measure passed with bipartisan support, with enough Republicans breaking ranks to join Democrats in backing the package.

The legislation targets key segments of Russia's economy through new sanctions while providing financial support to Ukraine as it continues its defense against Russian invasion, now more than two years in duration.

Republican leadership had actively lobbied against the bill before the vote, making the defection by House Republicans particularly notable. The strong bipartisan support demonstrated that a significant portion of Congress remains committed to supporting Ukraine despite political pressure from influential figures within the GOP.

The vote reflects divisions within the Republican Party over foreign policy priorities. The measure now advances to the Senate, where its prospects remain uncertain. Senate Democrats have already indicated they want to use spending measures as leverage on other issues, which could complicate the path forward for the Ukraine legislation.

The House vote comes amid broader debates about America's foreign policy direction. Some have suggested the United States has shifted its focus toward concerns about Iran, though the passage of Ukraine aid demonstrates continued congressional commitment to supporting European security alongside other international priorities.

The sanctions component of the bill specifically targets Russian entities and individuals involved in the ongoing conflict. Congressional support for Ukraine remains relatively strong despite the political pressures that have mounted in recent months.

The legislation represents a significant moment in congressional politics, with House Republicans demonstrating a willingness to break with both Trump and their own leadership on a major foreign policy question. The vote underscores the continued backing for Ukraine among substantial portions of both parties in Congress, even as the war enters its third year.

Microsoft's AI leadership has publicly questioned the pricing of Anthropic's AI models, arguing they are too expensive for most businesses to adopt widely. The criticism emerged during Microsoft's Build 2026 developer conference, where the company showcased its own AI infrastructure and strategic partnerships.

The timing is significant as Anthropic moves toward a public offering while reporting extraordinary financial growth. In May, the company announced its annualized revenue had reached $47 billion, a dramatic jump from approximately $9 billion at the end of 2025. As Anthropic prepares for its IPO, co-founder Daniela Amodei has worked to address investor concerns about returns on AI investments.

Microsoft used the Build conference to present its comprehensive AI development strategy across its product portfolio. The company unveiled Project Solara, an Android-based operating system built specifically for AI agents rather than conventional applications. Microsoft also introduced new capabilities for developing AI applications through Microsoft Fabric and its database services, positioning itself as focused on practical, cost-effective AI deployment rather than premium-priced cutting-edge technology.

The pricing dispute reflects deeper divisions within the AI industry over how companies should structure their business models and achieve profitability. Microsoft operates from a position of strength, maintaining partnerships with OpenAI while simultaneously developing its own AI models. This gives the company alternatives to Anthropic's offerings and flexibility in how it advises enterprise customers.

Microsoft's public stance on Anthropic's pricing suggests the company views cost as a significant obstacle to deploying sophisticated AI systems at scale across enterprise clients. By emphasizing its own approach during a major developer conference, Microsoft signaled that it sees cost-effectiveness as a competitive advantage in attracting business customers.

The Build conference itself reinforced Microsoft's broader strategy of positioning Windows as a trustworthy platform for AI development. The company demonstrated how it plans to integrate artificial intelligence throughout its entire product ecosystem, from cloud services to local applications.

This disagreement over pricing is likely to influence how other enterprises evaluate different AI providers as the market continues to develop. As companies decide which AI partners to work with, cost considerations will increasingly weigh against claims of superior capabilities. Microsoft's willingness to publicly criticize competitor pricing may be designed to shape perceptions among decision-makers at large organizations.

The tension between Anthropic's rapid revenue growth and Microsoft's cost concerns highlights a fundamental challenge facing the AI industry: whether premium pricing can be sustained as the technology becomes more widely available and competitive alternatives emerge.

Senate Republicans defeat Schumer amendment to block anti-weaponization fund

Senate Republicans narrowly blocked a Democratic effort on Thursday to prevent President Trump from establishing a $1.8 billion fund to compensate his allies. The vote on the amendment, sponsored by Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer, came during an extended series of amendment votes on a $70 billion budget reconciliation package designed to fund immigration enforcement age...

Yesterday, 9:00 PM ET

Senate Republicans narrowly blocked a Democratic effort on Thursday to prevent President Trump from establishing a $1.8 billion fund to compensate his allies. The vote on the amendment, sponsored by Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer, came during an extended series of amendment votes on a $70 billion budget reconciliation package designed to fund immigration enforcement agencies.

Schumer's amendment sought to insert language into Republican-backed legislation that would bar the Department of Justice from creating the so-called "anti-weaponization" fund. The measure failed 49-50, with three Republican senators breaking ranks to support the effort alongside all Democrats.

The three Republicans who voted with Democrats were Susan Collins of Maine, Dan Sullivan of Alaska, and Jon Husted of Ohio. All three represent seats considered vulnerable in upcoming midterm elections. Collins is the only Republican senator from a state won by Kamala Harris in 2024, while recent polling showed Husted trailing his Democratic challenger by significant margins.

The amendment vote stretched for hours as senators huddled on the chamber floor to discuss the contentious proposal. The dispute over the fund has exposed deep divisions within the Republican Party, even as the administration had previously indicated it would not move forward with the initiative. However, Schumer insisted that Congress should pass legislation to permanently block the fund from being used.

"Republicans are trusting the word of Todd Blanche, who built a career on lying," Schumer said, referring to the acting attorney general. He noted that Trump had expressed his enthusiasm for the fund just the previous day, calling it "so important." Schumer questioned whether Trump could resist using the fund for personal benefit.

The larger $70 billion bill will fund immigration agencies through 2029. Republicans are using the budget reconciliation process to pass it without Democratic support and avoid the filibuster. The reconciliation rules allow lawmakers to propose amendments before passage.

After Schumer's amendment failed, Republican Senator Thom Tillis proposed alternative language that would have prevented payouts from the anti-weaponization fund and instead directed that money toward Justice Department efforts to combat fraud. "This bill is unpopular. This administration has said they're not moving forward with it. This is an opportunity for us to put it to bed," Tillis said.

Democrats rejected Tillis's proposal. Oregon Senator Jeff Merkley argued that simply replacing one fund with another under the attorney general's control was insufficient. "The way to go is to get rid of these slush funds altogether," Merkley said. Tillis's amendment failed with support from only 11 other Republicans and three Democrats.

The standoff has complicated Republicans' efforts to settle a broader disagreement with Democrats over funding for immigration enforcement agencies, a dispute that previously sparked a 75-day partial shutdown of the Department of Homeland Security.
