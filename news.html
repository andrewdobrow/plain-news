9:36 AM ET

A federal judge has ordered the Trump administration to reinstate historical and scientific materials that were removed from national parks and monuments, ruling that the removals constitute censorship and set a dangerous precedent.

The order came after the city of Philadelphia sued the federal government over the removal of a slavery exhibit at Independence National Historical Park, which includes the former residence of George Washington. The National Park Service had dismantled the exhibit in response to Trump's claims of "anti-American ideology" at cultural institutions, a characterization rejected by civil rights groups.

Judge Cynthia Rufe, appointed by George W. Bush, granted Philadelphia's request to temporarily block the changes and ordered the National Park Service to restore the exhibit while litigation continues. She gave officials 21 days to comply with the order, which applies to any history or science materials removed from the nation's public monuments.

In her ruling, Rufe opened with a quote from George Orwell's 1984 describing how an authoritarian government constantly rewrites historical records. She drew an explicit comparison between the novel's "Ministry of Truth" and the Trump administration's actions.

"As if the Ministry of Truth in George Orwell's 1984 now existed, with its motto 'Ignorance is Strength,' this Court is now asked to determine whether the federal government has the power it claims to dissemble and disassemble historical truths when it has some domain over historical facts," Rufe wrote. "It does not."

During an earlier hearing, Rufe called the administration's argument that a president could unilaterally order changes to national park exhibits both "horrifying" and "dangerous."

The slavery exhibit at the President's House Site documented nine enslaved individuals owned by George Washington who were brought to the official presidential residence. The names of these nine people are etched into a wall within the exhibit. Historical research identified how Washington rotated enslaved people in and out of Pennsylvania specifically to prevent them from petitioning for freedom under Pennsylvania law. Two of the nine, Oney Judge and Hercules Posey, eventually escaped their enslavement.

The removal of the exhibit followed Trump's March 2025 executive order titled "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History." The order directed the secretary of interior to examine monuments and memorials to determine if they had been altered after January 2020 to represent what the administration characterized as a "false construction of American history."

Rufe's decision represents a significant legal challenge to the executive branch's authority over how national parks present American history and scientific information to visitors. The ruling preserves the educational mission of national parks to address historical and environmental topics that have long been part of their programming.

Yesterday, 8:06 PM ET

The Trump administration's Justice Department has cleared a $111 billion merger between Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery, concluding that the deal will not harm competition or consumers. The approval came after an eight-month antitrust investigation and removes a major regulatory hurdle for what will become one of the world's largest entertainment companies.

The Justice Department's antitrust division examined the merger across three key areas: streaming video on demand, linear television, and film studio operations. In its decision, the agency stated it "determined based on the evidence received in its investigation that the transaction is not likely to result in harm to competition or American consumers" in any of these categories.

The combined company will unite Paramount Pictures and Warner Bros. film studios with streaming services Paramount+ and Max. The deal also brings CBS News and CNN under single ownership, a prospect that has drawn significant concern from journalists and media watchdogs. The merger is expected to generate $6 billion in cost savings, likely through job cuts and operational consolidation.

Paramount defended the deal in a statement, saying it represents a pro-competitive move necessary to compete against large technology platforms. "This deal is pro-competitive, resulting in a stronger company better positioned to compete against dominant technology platforms in an industry increasingly defined by intense competition for audiences, talent, technology, and investment," the company said.

However, the Justice Department approval does not guarantee the merger will proceed. The deal still faces investigation by the UK Competition and Markets Authority, which has set an August 7 deadline to determine whether a deeper review is needed. European regulators are also examining funding for the transaction, including a combined $24 billion commitment from three Gulf sovereign wealth funds.

Additionally, California Attorney General Rob Bonta has indicated his office continues investigating the merger and said it "remains under investigation." There is speculation that a coalition of state attorneys general could file a lawsuit to block the deal in coming weeks.

Critics have attacked the Justice Department's decision. Craig Aaron, co-chief executive of Free Press, argued the investigation was not thorough, stating that "the fix was in at the Trump Justice Department from the start." Senator Elizabeth Warren called the approval "terrible news" and said the merger "has reeked of corruption and influence-peddling."

CNN and CBS News staff have expressed particular worry about potential news operation consolidation and editorial changes. While David Ellison, who controls Paramount through Skydance, pledged in March that CNN's editorial independence would be protected, speculation persists about potential leadership changes at the news networks.

Australia has already approved the merger. The combined entity now awaits final shareholder approval before the transaction can close.

Yesterday, 11:00 PM ET

The U.S. government has directed artificial intelligence company Anthropic to disable its most advanced AI models following concerns about potential security vulnerabilities. The directive represents a significant intervention in commercial AI deployment and highlights growing tension between innovation and national security.

Anthropic received an export control order from the U.S. government requiring the company to suspend access to its Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models for all foreign nationals. The government cited national security concerns, though Anthropic stated it was not provided specific details about the threat. According to the company, the government believes safeguards in these models can be bypassed and the tools could potentially be used to identify software vulnerabilities.

Rather than restrict access only to foreign users, Anthropic announced it would "abruptly disable" the models for all users globally. The decision affects advanced AI systems that had recently been released to the public.

The shutdown stems partly from a jailbreak discovered in Fable 5, which the government views as a potential security risk. Anthropic has expressed clear disagreement with the government's response. In a statement, the company wrote: "We disagree that the finding of a narrow potential jailbreak should be cause for recalling a commercial model deployed to hundreds of millions of people."

The disagreement underscores a fundamental difference in how Anthropic and government officials approach AI safety. Anthropic emphasizes its safety-focused development practices, including testing for vulnerabilities before releasing models to the public. The company argues that discovering a narrow jailbreak in an already widely deployed system does not justify a complete shutdown.

The Commerce Department maintains authority over technology exports and emerging technologies affecting national security. This enforcement action signals that federal agencies are increasing their scrutiny of AI systems and their potential security implications. As advanced AI technology becomes more powerful and widely deployed, regulators are grappling with how to balance innovation against security risks.

The case marks a notable moment in government regulation of artificial intelligence. Companies developing advanced AI systems now face mounting pressure to address security vulnerabilities while navigating evolving regulatory requirements. Federal agencies continue developing frameworks for AI oversight, and this action may establish precedent for how the government responds to discovered vulnerabilities in widely deployed systems.

The decision also raises questions about the process for such interventions. Anthropic's statement that it received no specific details about the national security concern suggests potential friction between the company and regulators over transparency in enforcement actions. As AI technology becomes increasingly central to business and infrastructure, how agencies communicate their security concerns may prove as important as the concerns themselves.

Pakistan Hosts First Direct US-Iran Talks Since 1979

Senior negotiators from the United States and Iran held face-to-face talks in Islamabad on Saturday, marking the highest-level direct meeting between the two countries since 1979. The discussions took place under Pakistani mediation, with officials from both delegations seated at the same table for the first time in decades.

The US delegation was led by Vice President JD Vance, while Iran's team was headed by Parliamentary Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi. Pakistan's Army Chief Asim Munir, who brokered a ceasefire earlier in the week, was also present during the negotiations. Other members of the American team included Steve Witkoff, a real estate developer serving as Trump's personal envoy, and Jared Kushner, the president's son-in-law.

The first round of discussions lasted approximately two hours and was conducted in what sources described as a positive atmosphere. This was followed by a second round focusing on technical matters, with talks divided into three key areas: security, finance, and the Strait of Hormuz. Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif hosted a dinner for both delegations at his residence.

Before the formal talks began, Iranian negotiators met separately with Pakistani mediators led by Sharif. During these preliminary discussions, Iran demanded a ceasefire in Lebanon, reparations, and the unfreezing of blocked assets as conditions for any peace agreement. The US responded cautiously, with Vance warning that Iran should not attempt to manipulate American negotiators.

The negotiations come after years of escalating tensions. Previous diplomatic efforts, including nuclear talks held in Geneva in late February, collapsed following coordinated US and Israeli bombing campaigns against Iran. Those strikes reportedly killed Iran's Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei.

Current tensions also extend to regional conflicts. Israeli forces have continued military operations in Lebanon, with Netanyahu indicating that Lebanon had approached Israel about potential peace negotiations. However, Netanyahu also vowed that Israel would continue fighting what he called Iran's terror regime and its proxies.

The US has also been conducting operations in the Strait of Hormuz. Trump announced that American forces were beginning to clear mines from the waterway and that all of Iran's mine-laying vessels had been sunk during the bombing campaign. Iran's state television countered by warning that any US warship crossing the strait would be attacked within 30 minutes. Qatar's transport ministry announced that full maritime navigation would resume on Sunday at 6am, subject to safety protocols.

Pakistani officials expressed optimism about the talks, noting that both delegations had arrived with complete authority from their capitals and had moved away from extreme positions. Reports suggested that China may offer guarantees to help secure any agreement, though Pakistani sources later clarified that a Chinese delegation had not physically traveled to Islamabad.
