2:12 AM ET

A federal judge has invalidated the Trump administration's $100,000 annual fee on H-1B visa applications, ruling the charge an unlawful tax that violated federal administrative law and the constitution.

US District Judge Leo Sorokin in Boston issued the 42-page ruling in response to a lawsuit filed by 20 Democratic state attorneys general. The decision struck down a fee that Trump announced in September, representing a dramatic 20-to-50 fold increase over existing application rates.

The H-1B visa program enables US employers to temporarily hire foreign workers in specialty occupations requiring advanced knowledge. Technology companies, consulting firms, and other industries rely on these visas to fill positions in fields such as software development, engineering, and medicine. The visa program operates under an annual cap on the number of new visas issued.

The Trump administration had defended the fee as part of broader immigration policy changes aimed at preventing foreign workers from displacing American employees. However, critics argued the substantial cost would effectively exclude many employers from the program and potentially harm American competitiveness in attracting global talent.

The ruling represents a significant setback for the administration's efforts to reshape legal immigration policy through executive action. The Trump administration has previously implemented various changes to visa and immigration programs, several of which have faced legal challenges in federal courts.

The decision affects thousands of employers who depend on H-1B visas to hire workers with specialized skills. Companies had faced the prospect of paying the $100,000 fee for each visa application, which could have totaled millions of dollars for large employers with substantial numbers of H-1B workers.

The Trump administration is widely expected to appeal the decision. The ruling allows employers to continue applying for H-1B visas without the additional fee while legal proceedings continue.

Judge Sorokin determined that the administration's implementation of the fee violated established procedures for federal agencies making major policy changes. The judge found the fee requirement and its underlying rationale to be arbitrary and capricious, a legal standard used to evaluate whether government actions are reasonable and based on proper analysis.

The invalidation of the fee removes a significant barrier to visa applications that had only recently taken effect. Employers who had prepared for the higher costs and incorporated the fee into their hiring calculations must now adjust their planning.

The case reflects broader tensions over immigration policy during the Trump administration's second term. As the administration pursues additional changes to immigration and visa programs through executive action, it faces ongoing legal challenges from states, civil rights organizations, and business groups concerned about the impact on the labor market and legal immigration pathways.

The Pentagon has designated major Chinese technology companies including Alibaba, Baidu, and electric vehicle manufacturer BYD as firms that support China's military capabilities. The action adds these prominent businesses to an official list that alerts American companies to potential risks associated with working with these entities.

The designation prevents the listed companies from securing U.S. defense contracts. The Pentagon's list serves as a warning mechanism rather than an immediate enforcement action, but it signals to American businesses that these firms may contribute to Chinese military advancement.

The expanded list also includes biotech firm WuXi AppTec and other Chinese technology companies. The Trump administration initially released this updated version of the list four months ago but then withdrew it without providing an explanation. The reasons for the delay and subsequent re-release remain unclear.

The move represents an escalation in U.S. efforts to limit China's technological development through restrictions on American corporate partnerships and investment. Previous Pentagon designations have primarily targeted Chinese telecommunications and surveillance companies. The inclusion of consumer-facing firms like Alibaba and BYD indicates a broader strategy to restrict China's access to American capital and technology.

BYD has become the world's largest electric vehicle manufacturer and continues expanding its global presence. Alibaba operates one of the world's largest e-commerce platforms alongside significant cloud computing services. These companies have substantial international operations and customer bases, making the designation potentially consequential for their global business relationships.

The Pentagon designation comes as the U.S. government has intensified scrutiny of Chinese technology companies across multiple sectors, including social media platforms and artificial intelligence development. American businesses working with blacklisted companies could face regulatory scrutiny and potential future restrictions on their own operations.

Companies on the list may encounter increased difficulty accessing American investors, establishing partnerships with U.S. firms, or operating in certain sectors of the American market. The designation complicates their ability to navigate an increasingly complex geopolitical landscape where technology development and national security concerns intersect with global commerce.

Chinese officials have previously criticized such designations as politically motivated efforts to obstruct Chinese technological progress. The affected companies have not yet issued public statements responding to the Pentagon's announcement. Industry observers expect that companies on the list will need to develop strategies for managing operations in an environment of heightened U.S. government scrutiny and potential restrictions on their business activities.

The blacklist designation reflects broader tensions between the United States and China over technological competition and military capabilities, with American policymakers seeking to prevent advanced technologies and expertise from indirectly supporting Chinese military modernization.

Russian Satellites Conducted Detailed Surveillance of Middle Eastern Military Targets

Russian satellites conducted extensive surveillance of military facilities and critical infrastructure across the Middle East shortly before Iranian attacks on US bases and other sites, according to a Ukrainian intelligence assessment reported by Reuters.

The assessment documented at least 24 satellite surveys of areas in 11 Middle Eastern countries between March 21 and 31. These surveys covered 46 targets including US and other military bases, airports, and oilfields. Within days of being surveyed, many of these locations were struck by Iranian ballistic missiles and drones.

One specific incident highlighted in the assessment involved Prince Sultan airbase in Saudi Arabia. A Russian satellite imaged the facility days before Iran attacked it on March 27, hitting a US E-3 Sentry airborne warning and control system aircraft. According to the Ukrainian report, a Russian satellite passed over the base again the next day to assess the damage.

Russian satellites also actively surveyed the Strait of Hormuz during this period. A Western military source and a separate regional security source both provided their own intelligence to Reuters confirming aspects of the Ukrainian assessment.

The Iranian foreign ministry declined to comment on the reports. Russia's defence ministry did not respond to Reuters' request for comment.

Beyond the satellite surveillance, the Ukrainian military also alleged that Russian and Iranian hackers were collaborating in cyber operations during the same timeframe.

The allegations come as Russian infrastructure faces repeated attacks from Ukrainian forces. Ukraine's military reported striking Russia's Ust-Luga oil terminal in the Leningrad region, saying it had preliminary confirmation of damage to three storage tanks operated by Transneft-Baltika company.

In a significant development affecting Russian energy exports, crude oil shipments from the Sheskharis terminal in the Black Sea port of Novorossiysk were suspended following a drone attack and fire. The terminal normally exports 700,000 barrels of crude oil daily and represents a key Russian oil outlet in the Black Sea.

Meanwhile, Russian attacks on Ukrainian cities continued to cause civilian casualties. In the eastern Dnipropetrovsk region, Russian forces targeted two buses, killing four people in Nikopol and injuring at least 16 others. In the southern city of Kherson, a half-hour Russian attack on a residential area killed four elderly people and injured seven more.

Ukrainian drone strikes killed five civilians in Russia and Russian-occupied territories, according to Russian officials, though Reuters could not independently verify these claims. Ukraine denies deliberately targeting civilians.

President Donald Trump received a hostile reception from the crowd at Madison Square Garden during Game 3 of the NBA Finals between the San Antonio Spurs and New York Knicks. When Trump appeared on the video screens during the national anthem, thousands of basketball fans responded with loud boos and jeers.

The president was shown on the jumbotron for just over eight seconds while the Star-Spangled Banner was being sung. He held a salute throughout with a smile on his face. The crowd's disapproval was immediate and audible across the arena. Moments later, when the video board switched to show Knicks players in line, the boos quickly turned to cheers.

The incident made Trump the first U.S. president to attend the NBA Finals. His attendance at the high-profile sporting event at Madison Square Garden, one of the most famous arenas in professional sports, drew significant attention beyond the game itself.

Getting into the venue required extraordinary security measures. Ticketholders faced airport-style security screening to enter Madison Square Garden, reflecting the heightened precautions taken for the event. The combination of Trump's presence, elevated security, and the championship atmosphere created an unusual environment at the arena.

The game itself turned out to be a competitive matchup. The Spurs defeated the Knicks 115-111, with San Antonio's Victor Wembanyama delivering an outstanding performance. The French center finished with 32 points, eight rebounds, six assists and three blocks, controlling stretches of the game on both ends of the floor. Stephon Castle provided crucial support with 23 points, five rebounds and five assists as a key contributor off the bench.

Despite the loss, the Knicks maintained their advantage in the series. New York still held a 2-1 lead heading into Game 4. However, the Spurs' victory signaled a shift in momentum. The Knicks struggled in the second half, managing just 47 points after halftime and receiving minimal production from key players like Karl-Anthony Towns and Mikal Bridges.

San Antonio's win made history of a different kind. The road team won the first three games of an NBA Finals for only the second time ever. The first instance occurred in 1993 when Charles Barkley's Phoenix Suns won a triple-overtime thriller at Chicago Stadium after losing the first two games at home to Michael Jordan's Bulls.

The spectacle at Madison Square Garden extended beyond Trump's appearance. The event featured movie stars along the sideline, high ticket prices reaching 10,000 dollars, and the significance of being New York City's first home NBA Finals game since 1999. The combination of political attention and championship basketball made Monday night at the Garden a memorable event for reasons both related and unrelated to the actual game being played.
