A Collin County jury found Karmelo Anthony guilty of murder for fatally stabbing Austin Metcalf during a high school track meet, resulting in a 35-year prison sentence.

The trial, which lasted one week from jury selection through verdict, drew national attention due to the racial demographics of the case. Anthony is Black while Metcalf was white. The jury consisted of 12 jurors and six alternates, none of whom were Black.

The incident occurred in April 2025 at a Frisco Independent School District track meet. Anthony, then 17 and attending Centennial High School, and Metcalf, also 17 and from Memorial High School, encountered each other when a rain shower caused confusion on the field. Some athletes remained competing while others sought shelter under team tents. When Centennial High School lacked a tent, Anthony moved toward Memorial's tent, where a confrontation broke out that resulted in Metcalf being stabbed. Metcalf died shortly after arriving at a local hospital.

Anthony's defense team argued he acted in self-defense. According to their account, Metcalf and his twin brother, both approximately 6 feet 1 inch tall and weighing around 213 pounds, confronted Anthony, who stands 5 feet 8 inches and weighs approximately 130 pounds. The defense characterized Anthony's actions as a reaction to "fear and chaos."

Prosecutors presented a different narrative. Prosecuting attorney Bill Wirskye argued that Metcalf was stabbed in a "sneak, surprise attack." He cited witness testimony indicating Anthony had told Metcalf, "Touch me and find out." Wirskye also noted that video evidence presented during trial showed other people in the tent did not turn on Anthony, suggesting the incident involved only the two teenagers.

Anthony, now 19, was tried as an adult under Texas law despite being a minor at the time of the stabbing. During the verdict reading, Metcalf's twin brother Hunter was present in the courtroom for the first time, as he had previously been excluded while listed as a witness. When the guilty verdict was announced, Hunter Metcalf leaned forward while Anthony's mother wept. Anthony broke down in tears, and his parents left the courtroom. Anthony has been remanded into custody of the Collin County Sheriff's Office.

The case highlighted broader concerns about safety at school athletic events and sparked discussions about the judicial treatment of minors charged with serious crimes.

Social Security retirement trust fund projected to run dry in 2032

The Social Security retirement trust fund will be depleted in 2032, one year earlier than previously projected, unless Congress takes action to shore up the program's finances, according to a new report. The accelerated timeline puts pressure on lawmakers to address the shortfall as tens of millions of Americans rely on the program for retirement income. The updated projection...

8:26 PM ET

The Social Security retirement trust fund will be depleted in 2032, one year earlier than previously projected, unless Congress takes action to shore up the program's finances, according to a new report. The accelerated timeline puts pressure on lawmakers to address the shortfall as tens of millions of Americans rely on the program for retirement income.

The updated projection means the program's reserves will reach critically low levels in just six years. Once the trust fund is exhausted, Social Security will only be able to pay out what it collects in payroll taxes, resulting in automatic benefit cuts for retirees if no legislative fix is implemented. The Washington Post reported that the shortfall is expected to accelerate beyond current estimates.

Social Security provides monthly payments to more than 66 million Americans, including retirees, disabled workers, and survivors of deceased workers. The program is funded primarily through payroll taxes paid by current workers and their employers. The trust fund was established to handle the gap between tax revenue and benefit payments as the population ages and the ratio of workers to beneficiaries declines.

Watchdog groups have warned that time is running out for Congress to address the funding gap. Potential solutions include raising the payroll tax rate, increasing the cap on wages subject to the tax, raising the retirement age, or reducing benefits for higher-income retirees. Each option faces political challenges, and lawmakers have been reluctant to tackle the issue in an election year.

The earlier depletion date reflects updated economic and demographic assumptions, including changes in birth rates, life expectancy, and workforce participation. Without intervention, beneficiaries could see their monthly payments reduced by roughly 20 percent once the trust fund runs dry, affecting millions of retirees who depend on Social Security as their primary source of income.

The agreement reflects a notably civil negotiation process compared to previous contract cycles in Hollywood. The DGA, which represents thousands of directors and members of directorial teams working across film and television production in the United States, secured the deal without the public disputes or work stoppages that have sometimes marked labor negotiations in the industry.

The union's board will next review the tentative agreement to determine whether to recommend it to members. Should the board approve the deal, the full membership will vote on ratification. If members accept the contract, it will govern working conditions, compensation, and employment terms for directors through the next four years.

The successful conclusion of DGA negotiations suggests relative stability in labor relations between creative guilds and the major studios this year. However, the specific gains and concessions achieved by either side remain confidential until the ratification process proceeds.

Once ratified, the four-year contract will establish standards for directors' working conditions and compensation structures through 2030. The DGA's ability to reach an agreement without extended negotiations or work stoppages distinguishes this cycle from more contentious labor disputes that have periodically disrupted Hollywood production schedules.

The DGA agreement adds to a broader pattern of labor negotiations resolving relatively smoothly across the entertainment industry. Earlier this year, other major unions have also reached tentative agreements with industry operators. The American Federation of Musicians Local 802 secured a three-year contract with the Broadway League just before a strike deadline, averting work stoppages that would have affected Broadway productions. That agreement provided musicians with wage increases and health benefit improvements while preserving contract protections that enable musicians to sustain careers on Broadway.

Similarly, Actors Equity Association reached its own tentative agreement with the Broadway League, securing protections around the Equity-League Health Fund while addressing other priorities including scheduling and physical therapy access.

These successive agreements across different sectors of the entertainment industry demonstrate a pattern of negotiations reaching resolution without major disruption. The DGA's four-year deal fits within this trend, suggesting that management and creative guilds have found ground for compromise during this negotiation cycle.

The DGA contract will now proceed through the union's internal approval processes before members determine whether to ratify the agreement and accept its terms for the coming four years.
