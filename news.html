The Seattle Times and Newsday filed a lawsuit against OpenAI and Microsoft, alleging the companies used their journalism without authorization to train artificial intelligence models. The lawsuit argues that generative AI could leave the journalism industry broken beyond repair by functioning as a system that destroys the organizations producing the content it depends on.

The complaint describes AI products like ChatGPT and Copilot as consumers that take human-authored content and deliver back copies and derivative imitations to achieve commercial objectives. The publications characterized generative AI as a snake eating its own tail.

The lawsuit follows a 2023 copyright infringement case filed by The New York Times against OpenAI and Microsoft, which other publications have since joined. The Seattle Times case is notable because Microsoft and OpenAI have funded some of the newspaper's journalism projects and fellowships.

A Microsoft spokesperson said the company is surprised by the lawsuit but is always happy to explore solutions to this type of dispute. The case adds to growing legal challenges against AI companies over their use of copyrighted material for model training.

A divided government occurs when one or both houses of Congress are controlled by Democrats while a Republican holds the White House.

"The second any president actively considers the first use of nuclear weapons, that person should be removed from office immediately," Carlson said during his podcast.

The comments represent a notable departure from typical conservative media support for Trump. Carlson framed the issue as a fundamental question of presidential fitness rather than as a partisan political attack. His remarks come amid ongoing discussions about the scope of presidential war powers and military operations in the Middle East.

The source material provided does not specify what prompted Carlson's statement or whether he possesses direct knowledge of Trump considering nuclear options. The White House has not commented on the remarks.

Recent developments in the Trump administration's foreign policy include a nuclear cooperation agreement signed with Saudi Arabia. Under the deal, the kingdom would be enabled to enrich its own uranium for nuclear reactors. At a House Foreign Affairs Committee hearing, Democratic Congressman Brad Sherman called for an oversight hearing of the agreement, suggesting the technology transfer could be as significant a mistake as arming the Shah of Iran during the 1970s.

The specifics of the Saudi Arabia agreement remain unclear until the full text is released. Initial reports suggest it could allow uranium enrichment in Saudi Arabia, potentially opening a pathway to nuclear weapons development in the kingdom. This contrasts with a similar 2009 agreement between the Obama administration and the United Arab Emirates, which included safeguards preventing the Emirates from developing nuclear weapons and committed the country to accepting International Atomic Energy Agency inspections.

Presidential authority over nuclear weapons has been an ongoing subject of legal and policy debate. Some lawmakers have proposed legislation requiring congressional approval before any first use of nuclear weapons. Currently, no such restrictions exist, leaving the decision solely with the president.

Carlson's statement adds a new dimension to political discussions as attention focuses on Trump's second term. Republican figures have largely avoided criticizing Trump on national security matters, making Carlson's position particularly notable within conservative circles. Whether his comments reflect broader concerns among conservatives or represent an isolated view remains unclear.

The Trump administration has been active on multiple fronts, including signing the defense policy bill and conducting military operations in the Middle East. The administration has also reportedly been pushing various policy initiatives affecting voting restrictions and other domestic matters.
