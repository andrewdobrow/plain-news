Yesterday, 11:47 PM ET

Oil prices extended their decline, with Brent crude falling below $80 per barrel following an announced agreement between the United States and Iran to end their conflict. The deal marked the fifth consecutive day of losses for the international benchmark, reflecting trader expectations that Iranian oil will soon return to global markets.

The framework agreement centers on reopening the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway that was effectively closed during the nearly three-month conflict that began on February 28. US President Donald Trump announced that the strait would reopen under the deal, which both sides have digitally signed and plan to formalize with a ceremonial signing in Geneva on Friday.

Market observers noted that oil prices remain significantly elevated despite the recent selloff. A barrel of Brent crude now trades around $80, compared to approximately $70 before the conflict began. The $20 price difference underscores the substantial impact the war had on energy markets.

Analysts cautioned that the market response should be measured. Warren Patterson, head of commodities strategy at ING, told Reuters: "We've been at this stage before, only for talks to break down. Therefore, the market will likely be more cautious about overreacting." Giovanni Staunovo, a UBS analyst, emphasized that physical oil flows remain the key factor to watch, noting that flows through the strait remain restricted despite the agreement.

Even with the deal in place, experts predict that a return to normal oil flows will require months. Damaged energy infrastructure in Qatar and elsewhere must be repaired before exports can reach previous levels. Barclays maintained its average Brent crude forecast of $100 for the year, though acknowledged risks it could climb higher.

The agreement's announcement sparked broader market movements. Stock markets rose on the news, with Japan's Nikkei climbing nearly 3 percent and the pan-European Stoxx 600 index gaining 1 percent. The dollar dipped 0.3 percent against major currencies, while the pound gained almost 0.6 percent to its highest level since mid-May. Gold climbed 1.46 percent to $4,574 per ounce.

Stephen Innes, an independent analyst, observed that markets responded positively to the prospect of the Strait of Hormuz reopening to normal operations. He noted that the reaction made sense given how much inflation fear and hawkish rate expectations had been built into markets during the recent energy shock.

The higher oil prices and costs for materials like fertilizer had driven inflation concerns worldwide, causing central banks to shift expectations toward rate increases rather than cuts. The agreement's announcement has prompted markets to recalibrate those economic forecasts, though analysts remain cautious about how quickly and completely normal energy flows will resume.

A Boeing B-52 Stratofortress crashed during a test flight at Edwards Air Force Base in the Mojave Desert, about 100 miles northeast of Los Angeles, killing all eight people on board. The aircraft experienced a fiery explosion that sent thick plumes of smoke into the air, and the base remained closed the following day as crews worked to secure the crash site for search and recovery teams.

The bomber made a sharp right turn after taking off, followed by a near 180-degree turn before plummeting to another runway at a rate of approximately 5,056 feet per minute. This descent rate is nearly ten times faster than a normal landing descent, according to flight tracking data.

The victims included Boeing employees, government workers, military personnel, and civilian contractors. Their names have not been publicly identified.

Aviation safety expert Jeff Guzzetti, who previously investigated crashes for the Federal Aviation Administration and the National Transportation Safety Board, suspects a flight control malfunction. "I think it was definitely a controllability issue. Now, whether that was tied to an engine failure, a flight control failure, or some new testing device failure, I'm not sure," Guzzetti said. He noted that controls could have been rigged incorrectly following maintenance, or the crash could have resulted from a catastrophic engine problem or failure of equipment being tested.

The B-52 was supporting a radar modernization program at the time of the crash. In 2025, Boeing delivered a B-52 to Edwards equipped with a modernized radar system designed to keep the bomber operational through at least 2050, nearly a century after it first entered service in the 1950s.

Edwards Air Force Base serves as a major testing facility for military aircraft. The 412th Test Wing, which operates the base, conducts developmental testing of all Air Force aircraft, weapons systems, software, and components before purchase and throughout their operational lifespans.

Col James Hayes, deputy commander for the 412th Test Wing, confirmed the aircraft's involvement in the radar modernization program. Mike Paoli, a spokesperson for the wing, said fires flared up overnight after the crash, requiring additional safety efforts before investigators could fully enter the area.

The investigation into the crash could take up to six months to complete, according to officials. Investigators will examine flight data, maintenance records, weather conditions, and other factors to determine what caused the bomber to descend so rapidly shortly after takeoff. The findings from such investigations typically lead to changes in procedures, training, or maintenance protocols designed to prevent similar accidents.

San Andreas fault reaches highest stress levels in 1,000 years, study finds

Yesterday, 7:54 PM ET

Southern California's San Andreas and San Jacinto fault systems have accumulated tectonic stress at levels not seen in the past 1,000 years, according to new research published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth. Scientists describe the region as being in a "critically loaded state" with increased potential for a major earthquake.

Liliane Burkhard, lead author of the study, explained the significance of the findings: "Our results show that stress levels on multiple fault segments are now at or above the highest values seen in the past millennium and that the region may be capable of a large through-going rupture involving both fault systems."

Researchers at the University of Hawaii at Mānoa used computer modeling to track how stress accumulates and releases along the faults. They analyzed 1,000 years of earthquake history to determine current stress levels. More than 160 years have passed since the last major rupture in the region, allowing stress to build to unprecedented levels.

A critical feature of the research focuses on Cajon Pass, located where the two fault systems meet. Scientists found that this area could function as an "earthquake gate," potentially either blocking large ruptures from traveling between the faults or allowing both systems to rupture simultaneously in a single event.

According to Burkhard, the alignment of stress levels between the two fault systems may determine whether this gate opens or remains closed. "Right now, with stress at historically high levels across the region and more than 160 years elapsed since the last major rupture, the system is in a critically loaded state," she said.

If the earthquake gate opens and both faults rupture together, the consequences could be severe across southern California. Such a joint rupture would represent a significantly larger event than a single fault system rupturing alone.

The researchers emphasize that their work does not predict when an earthquake will occur. Instead, the study provides essential information about the range of scenarios the region should prepare for. Burkhard noted: "What we can say is that the system is critically stressed, and that physics-based models like this one give us a clearer picture of the range of scenarios we should be prepared for."

California's last major devastating earthquake struck in 1994 near Northridge in Los Angeles. That event destroyed 87,000 homes and businesses and resulted in more than 60 deaths.

The findings underscore the importance of ongoing earthquake preparedness throughout southern California. Emergency management agencies and officials continue encouraging residents to develop evacuation plans and familiarize themselves with safety protocols in preparation for potential seismic activity.
