At least nine major companies receiving refunds after the Supreme Court struck down Trump-era tariffs directed portions of that money toward executive bonuses instead of consumer price relief, according to a ProPublica investigation published Monday. The investigation analyzed securities filings and earnings calls to identify companies that told consumers they had to raise prices because of tariffs but are now using refund money for leadership compensation.

Stanley Black & Decker, which makes vacuums, electric drills, and measuring tapes, is expected to receive at least $298 million in tariff reimbursements and indicated some will go to bonuses. The company told consumers it was forced to raise prices because of Trump's import fees. In financial disclosures, Stanley Black & Decker said the refund it received was partially offset by costs for variable incentive compensation, according to ProPublica.

TJX, the parent company of TJ Maxx and Marshalls, received $331 million in refunds and will accrue $112 million in year-end incentive compensation and discretionary bonuses for eligible associates globally because of the refunds, a company spokesperson confirmed to ProPublica. The spokesperson said the company has not disclosed details beyond that accrual because the fiscal year has not ended.

The Trump administration has refunded at least $100 billion to companies since August, about 60 percent of roughly $131 billion in tariffs collected, after the Supreme Court ruled that Trump's tariffs were illegal because they overstepped executive authority and should have required congressional approval. The administration has not specified which companies are receiving refunds despite requests for information. ProPublica found that almost all companies publicly planning to use reimbursements for customer refunds deal with other businesses rather than consumers directly, while some companies have remained vague about their plans for the money.

A Massachusetts judge set a March 29, 2027, trial date for Lindsay Clancy, who is accused of murdering her three children, after a jury deadlocked in her first trial in September. Judge William Sullivan rejected a defense motion last week to dismiss the case on double jeopardy grounds, clearing the way for the retrial. Prosecutors indicated they intend to seek a jury trial, which the defense interpreted as a signal that Clancy, 36, will again face first-degree murder charges, though the district attorney has not formally announced the charges.

Clancy was charged with three counts of first-degree murder in the deaths of her children, five-year-old Cora, three-year-old Dawson, and eight-month-old Callan, who were strangled at the family home in Duxbury on January 24, 2023. It was not disputed at trial that Clancy strangled the children with exercise bands in the basement shortly after she asked her now ex-husband, Patrick Clancy, to leave the house to pick up takeout food and medicine. The defense argued she should be found not guilty by reason of insanity, contending she had severe postpartum psychosis.

Judge Sullivan declared a mistrial on September 4 after the jury could not reach a unanimous verdict. Some jurors later told media outlets they had been ready to find Clancy not guilty by reason of insanity, but one juror held out. Double jeopardy protects defendants from being prosecuted more than once for the same crime after an acquittal or conviction, but the legal principle does not apply when a jury deadlocks without reaching a verdict.

The case has renewed debate over how postpartum mental illness is treated in the United States legal system. Clancy is currently held in a state-run psychiatric hospital and medical facility in Massachusetts. Prosecutors had requested a May trial date, but Judge Sullivan, who is set to retire in June, scheduled the retrial for March instead.

Chase serves approximately 94 million customer relationships in the United States through 5,000 physical branches and 15,000 ATMs.

Hotel giant Wyndham acknowledged a security incident after a researcher discovered a hardcoded password in its Android app that granted access to an internal database containing personal information for millions of rewards members. Security researcher Robert Wiggins found the credential embedded in the app's source code, which allowed access to a Salesforce-hosted database storing customer names, email addresses, member numbers, and login tokens.

The database contained millions of entries from Wyndham's 129 million enrolled rewards members, though Wiggins could not determine an exact count of exposed records. The credential was revoked after Wiggins contacted Wyndham through a journalist because the company had no public channel for reporting security vulnerabilities. A Wyndham spokesperson said the company addressed the issue and found no evidence of unauthorized access, but declined to say whether server logs existed to verify that claim.

Wyndham would not disclose how long the password was embedded in the app, whether it plans to notify affected customers, or whether it will report the incident to the Securities and Exchange Commission as required for material cybersecurity events at public companies. Hardcoded credentials are considered a critical security weakness because anyone with the software can extract and use the password, and such vulnerabilities typically receive maximum severity ratings.

This marks the second major discovery of hardcoded credentials by Wiggins in two weeks, following a similar flaw in the Applebee's app. Wiggins used artificial intelligence tools to scan decompiled app code for such weaknesses. Wyndham's cybersecurity director provided an email address for researchers to report issues, but the company declined to say whether it would establish a formal vulnerability disclosure program or update its website to publicize the reporting channel.

Wyndham experienced a separate security incident in 2024 when hotel check-in computers were found running remote-access spyware that leaked guest records to the internet. That spyware company was later hacked and shut down, and its owner was convicted of making and selling unlawful spyware.

Robert Pattinson has been confirmed as the next actor to play James Bond, Amazon MGM Studios announced Monday. The 40-year-old British actor becomes the seventh performer to portray the fictional spy in the official film series and will star in the 26th Bond movie, scheduled for release in 2028.

The film will be directed by Denis Villeneuve and written by Steven Knight, though the title and exact release date have not been announced. Pattinson said in a statement that playing Bond is "the honour of a lifetime" and called the role "a dream come true." He added that he is "deeply grateful" to the creative team for their belief in him.

The announcement ends years of speculation about who would succeed Daniel Craig, who appeared in five Bond films over 16 years before his final turn in 2021's No Time to Die. The 2028 release will mark a seven-year gap since the last Bond film, the longest in the franchise's history. Villeneuve, known for the Dune trilogy and films including Arrival and Blade Runner 2049, said Pattinson "has the timeless soul of 007 and brings a spirit that is completely new."

Pattinson rose to fame in Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire and starred in the Twilight series, which collectively earned more than $3 billion worldwide. He has since appeared in major films including The Batman and Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey, as well as smaller independent productions. He recently worked with Villeneuve on Dune: Part 3, set for release next month.

The film represents a new era for the franchise. Amazon purchased MGM for $8.5 billion in 2021, and in 2025, longtime Bond producers Barbara Broccoli and Michael G. Wilson sold creative control of the series to Amazon MGM, ending the Broccoli family's stewardship that began in 1962.

President Donald Trump has nominated Eric Ueland as comptroller general and head of the Government Accountability Office, a position empowered to sue the administration on behalf of Congress. The nomination represents a push to confirm an ally before Democrats take control of Congress in January. Ueland currently serves as deputy director for management at the Office of Management and Budget, which has led efforts to withhold and cancel federal funding.

The Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee will hold a hearing on the nomination Thursday. Lawmakers in both parties have called for the GAO to challenge the Trump administration before the Supreme Court for withholding federal funds approved by Congress. The agency has issued more than a half-dozen findings of illegal impoundment of federal dollars since the start of Trump's second term but has not yet taken legal action.

Ueland has significant Capitol Hill experience as a former staff director for Senate Budget Committee Republicans under then-Chair Michael Enzi of Wyoming and chief of staff to former Republican Senate Majority Leader Bill Frist. He served as White House director of legislative affairs during the first Trump administration, where he brokered talks between then-Treasury Secretary Steven Mnuchin and senators on a Covid-19 economic relief package.

Democrats have criticized Republicans for what they call a sham confirmation process that breaks precedent for how the comptroller general is typically nominated. Some Republican senators, particularly those leaving office in January, could question whether it is appropriate to confirm a sitting executive branch official to a position responsible for investigating that same administration. The Supreme Court suggested last fall that the GAO is the only entity with standing to sue the Trump administration for violating the Impoundment Control Act. The agency has been operating under an acting comptroller general since last December.

A Massachusetts judge set a March 29, 2027, trial date for Lindsay Clancy, who is accused of murdering her three children, after a jury deadlocked in her first trial in September. Judge William Sullivan rejected a defense motion last week to dismiss the case on double jeopardy grounds, clearing the way for the retrial. Prosecutors indicated they intend to seek a jury trial, which the defense interpreted as a signal that Clancy, 36, will again face first-degree murder charges, though the district attorney has not formally announced the charges.

Clancy was charged with three counts of first-degree murder in the deaths of her children, five-year-old Cora, three-year-old Dawson, and eight-month-old Callan, who were strangled at the family home in Duxbury on January 24, 2023. It was not disputed at trial that Clancy strangled the children with exercise bands in the basement shortly after she asked her now ex-husband, Patrick Clancy, to leave the house to pick up takeout food and medicine. The defense argued she should be found not guilty by reason of insanity, contending she had severe postpartum psychosis.

Judge Sullivan declared a mistrial on September 4 after the jury could not reach a unanimous verdict. Some jurors later told media outlets they had been ready to find Clancy not guilty by reason of insanity, but one juror held out. Double jeopardy protects defendants from being prosecuted more than once for the same crime after an acquittal or conviction, but the legal principle does not apply when a jury deadlocks without reaching a verdict.

The case has renewed debate over how postpartum mental illness is treated in the United States legal system. Clancy is currently held in a state-run psychiatric hospital and medical facility in Massachusetts. Prosecutors had requested a May trial date, but Judge Sullivan, who is set to retire in June, scheduled the retrial for March instead.
