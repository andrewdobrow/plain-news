Yesterday, 11:00 PM ET

The Commerce Department directed Anthropic to shut down its Fable and Mythos artificial intelligence models, citing national security concerns about a potential jailbreak vulnerability in the Fable 5 system. The order forced the AI company to halt commercial models that had been deployed to hundreds of millions of users.

Anthromic publicly challenged the government's decision in a blog post, stating that the company disagrees that discovering a narrow potential jailbreak should warrant recalling a widely deployed commercial model. The company expressed frustration with what it views as an overreaction to the security finding.

The directive represents a significant government intervention in the commercial AI sector. The Commerce Department's concerns centered on the possibility that the Fable 5 model could be compromised through jailbreaking techniques, which allow users to bypass safety restrictions built into AI systems. Such vulnerabilities could potentially enable misuse of the technology for purposes beyond its intended applications.

The shutdown affects Anthropic's commercial operations and raises questions about the balance between AI safety protocols and government oversight. The company had implemented its own safety warnings and monitoring systems before the government action. Anthropic's public disagreement with the federal directive suggests tension between the private sector's approach to AI safety and regulatory standards imposed by national security agencies.

The case may set a precedent for how the government addresses perceived security risks in artificial intelligence systems. The Commerce Department's authority to order the shutdown of deployed AI models demonstrates the expanding regulatory reach over technology companies as AI capabilities advance. The incident highlights ongoing debates about the appropriate level of government intervention in AI development and deployment, particularly when national security concerns intersect with commercial technology products serving large user bases.

Shalit joined the Today show in 1970 and became arts editor in 1973, serving in that role for over four decades. He remained a popular fixture in American households from the 1970s until his retirement in 2010, making him one of the longest-serving contributors to morning television.

Known for his distinctive appearance and love of wordplay, Shalit earned a reputation as a middle-of-the-road critic whose wit and intelligence made film criticism accessible to mainstream audiences. His affection for groan-inducing puns became his trademark, distinguishing him from more austere film critics of his era. This approach helped bring film commentary into American living rooms as part of the daily news cycle, democratizing a form of cultural criticism that might otherwise have remained confined to specialty publications.

Throughout his decades on the Today show, Shalit conducted interviews with major Hollywood stars and reviewed countless films. His combination of serious cultural commentary with entertainment value helped define the format of morning television news and made him a cultural icon beyond traditional film criticism circles.

Shalit's career spanned an extraordinary period in media history. His century-long life encompassed the entire arc of commercial cinema, from silent films through the digital age. He represented a generation of broadcast personalities who brought personality and accessibility to their roles, making them memorable and beloved by viewers.

His distinctive visual presentation, featuring his characteristic hair and mustache, made him instantly recognizable and contributed to his status as a television personality. Beyond his appearance, his verbal style and approach to criticism set him apart and made him a notable figure in American popular culture.

The death of Shalit marks the end of an era in television journalism and film criticism. He exemplified a particular tradition of broadcast criticism in which the critic's personality was integral to the appeal and impact of their work. His long tenure on the Today show reflected both his own durability as a personality and the show's evolution as a major platform for cultural commentary in American media.

Shalit's legacy includes his influence on how film criticism was presented to general audiences and his demonstration that serious cultural analysis could coexist with entertainment and humor. His approach influenced how subsequent critics and commentators approached their work on morning television.

Republicans are sharply divided over whether to pursue a third party-line spending package, with some lawmakers openly questioning whether such an ambitious effort can succeed during an election year. While GOP leaders and budget writers have begun laying groundwork for the measure, senior Republicans have raised serious concerns about its viability.

President Donald Trump has pushed aggressively for the package on his Truth Social platform, calling on congressional Republicans to "IMMEDIATELY advance and pass the forthcoming $350 Billion Reconciliation Bill." The proposed measure would fund defense spending at historically high levels and include the Save America Act, a package of voting restrictions that Trump's allies have repeatedly attempted to pass without success.

The budget reconciliation process allows lawmakers to bypass the Senate filibuster and pass legislation with a simple majority, avoiding the typical 60-vote threshold. Trump has emphasized urgency, stating "No games, no delays, and no weak compromises! Do this ASAP."

However, key Republican senators have expressed skepticism about the approach. Mitch McConnell, the former Senate Republican leader, stated bluntly this week that "there will not be another reconciliation bill." Susan Collins, the powerful Republican chair of the appropriations committee, agreed with that assessment.

Both senators raised concerns that using reconciliation for defense funding would create instability rather than provide reliable, sustained financing for weapons systems that require multi-year spending commitments. Collins told Air Force leaders that relying on a one-time reconciliation bill for the bulk of defense money "creates instability" compared to traditional appropriations processes. McConnell warned that major disruptions could occur if Republicans fail to reach agreement on the package.

The White House has requested $1.5 trillion in total defense spending for fiscal year 2027, with $1.15 trillion to flow through normal appropriations and $350 billion through reconciliation. The funding would support various weapons systems, including ones Trump has personally promoted.

The Save America Act appears to face even steeper obstacles. The legislation would impose new voter ID requirements, mandate that states regularly turn voter rolls over to the Department of Homeland Security, and hold election officials legally liable for improper voter registration. Legal experts question whether these provisions comply with budget reconciliation rules, which restrict bills to matters affecting spending, revenue, and the debt limit. Additionally, the measure lacks Democratic support needed to reach the 60-vote threshold for passage under normal Senate procedures.

The Senate attempted to advance the Save America Act in March, but the effort collapsed when it became clear the bill lacked sufficient support. Republican divisions over reconciliation now threaten Trump's push to revive it as part of the larger spending package, complicating GOP efforts to move forward with the president's legislative agenda before the election.
