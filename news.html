5:05 AM ET

Kevin M. Warsh took control of the Federal Reserve at a pivotal moment, with the central bank facing pressure from multiple directions as it manages inflation, employment concerns, and political scrutiny.

At the Fed's June meeting, officials voted to leave interest rates unchanged for the third time this year. The decision came despite ongoing calls from President Trump for rate cuts. Only one of the 12 voting board members dissented from the rate hold, though three members supported maintaining current rates while disagreeing with the Fed's signal that it might lower rates later this year.

The Fed cited several factors in its decision to pause. Job gains have remained subdued, the unemployment rate has stayed relatively flat in recent months, and inflation remains elevated. Energy prices have added to inflationary pressures, with global crude oil briefly reaching $119 a barrel amid Middle East tensions.

Warsh's appointment marked a significant shift in Fed leadership. His nomination was confirmed by the Senate banking committee just hours before the rate decision. Trump and his economic advisers have argued that the Fed should be cutting rates more aggressively to stimulate economic growth, though economists generally emphasize the importance of Fed independence from political pressure.

The outgoing chair, Jerome Powell, announced he will remain on the Fed's board after his term as chairman ends in May, a decision he said was necessary to oversee certain institutional matters through completion. Powell made pointed remarks about Fed independence, stating that the institution is "at risk" and facing what he called "legal assaults" and "battered" by recent events.

His comments referenced a Justice Department investigation into building renovations at the Fed that exceeded budget. Trump had suggested Powell may have acted corruptly in his role as chair overseeing the project. Powell previously called the investigation a "pretext" after the Fed resisted Trump's attempts to influence interest rates. The Justice Department dropped its investigation into Powell last week.

The broader economic picture shows mixed signals. Markets have performed well, with the S&P 500 and Dow reaching new highs. However, small businesses have faced mounting pressures from tariffs and elevated energy costs that have strained their finances.

As the new chairman, Warsh faces significant expectations about his policy direction. While some observers suggest he may be more receptive to rate cuts than Powell, Fed officials have emphasized that monetary policy decisions require board consensus. The new chairman cannot deliver cuts unilaterally, needing support from fellow board members.

Investors and economists are closely watching Warsh's communications and statements for signals about the Fed's future path. The central bank's dual mandate of maximum employment and stable prices remains its guiding framework, but decisions will depend on incoming data about inflation, labor markets, and overall economic growth as the year progresses.

President Trump warned at the G7 summit in France that he would resume military strikes against Iran if the country failed to comply with a preliminary ceasefire agreement, even as fellow leaders were celebrating the deal as a diplomatic breakthrough.

The threat came during a side meeting on Wednesday at the summit in Évian-les-Bains, where Trump told reporters that if Iran misbehaved, he would "go back to dropping bombs right smack in the middle of their head." The stark warning contradicted the optimistic tone of a joint G7 statement issued the same morning that called the agreement an "historic opportunity" to prevent Iran from acquiring nuclear weapons.

The preliminary agreement, scheduled to be signed on Friday, immediately lifts U.S. sanctions on Iran's oil exports and related industries. It establishes a 300 billion dollar reconstruction fund and reopens the Strait of Hormuz, which Iran had threatened during months of conflict with the U.S. and Israel. The deal also commits Iran to reaffirm its opposition to acquiring nuclear weapons, though it postpones negotiations on what to do with Iran's existing stockpile of highly enriched uranium.

Trump defended the agreement against mounting domestic criticism from Republican allies who question whether it achieves enough. He rejected suggestions that the United States would contribute to the 300 billion dollar investment fund, stating: "We're not putting up even 10 cents." He argued that other countries could invest if they wished, but that any contributions would likely depend on Iran's future behavior.

The president claimed the deal would prevent economic catastrophe, saying the alternative would be "a worldwide depression." He pointed to falling oil prices as evidence the market approved of the agreement, noting that Brent crude had dipped below 80 dollars per barrel.

The G7 statement, while welcoming Trump's deal, suggested it was considered incomplete. The leaders called for further negotiations addressing Iran's ballistic missile program and its support for proxy forces, issues not directly covered in the current memorandum. They proposed involving European leaders and international organizations like the International Atomic Energy Agency in follow-up talks.

The G7 also endorsed a French and British proposal for a multinational maritime task force to escort ships through the Strait of Hormuz, an idea likely to face rejection from Tehran, which has conducted negotiations exclusively with the United States.

Trump's dual messaging reflects the tension between his administration and international partners over how to manage Iranian relations. While he backed the G7 statement, suggesting unusual willingness to accommodate other leaders' concerns, his warning about renewed bombing operations introduces significant uncertainty into the diplomatic process.

The Atlantic hurricane season began earlier this month, and meteorologists had predicted an active year. The potential formation of this early system aligns with those forecasts. Residents across coastal Texas communities face threats from the developing storm as it moves through waters that could support further strengthening.

The National Hurricane Center continues to track the system and will issue updates as the situation develops.

Coastal communities along the Gulf are taking precautionary measures in preparation for what could be a busy hurricane season. Residents have been advised to monitor updates from the National Weather Service and local emergency management officials. Texas officials have not yet issued evacuation orders, but communities are implementing preparedness steps.

The tropical storm warnings indicate that conditions capable of producing sustained winds of at least 39 miles per hour are expected within the warning areas. This threshold wind speed would officially qualify the system as a tropical storm.

Multiple weather services, including FOX Weather and CBS News, are tracking the system as it moves through the Gulf. The focus on this developing storm reflects the broader expectation among meteorologists that the 2026 Atlantic hurricane season could be particularly active.

The timing of this potential first named storm comes as Gulf Coast residents and emergency officials prepare for the season ahead. The National Hurricane Center will continue providing regular updates on the system's position, intensity, and projected path.

If the system does reach tropical storm strength, it will represent the official start of the season's named storm activity. Communities along the Texas coast should remain alert for further developments and continue monitoring official weather forecasts and emergency management announcements.

The developing system demonstrates the active weather patterns expected this season and underscores the importance of preparedness along the Gulf Coast.

Yesterday, 9:31 PM ET

A new study reveals that stress levels on Southern California's San Andreas and San Jacinto fault systems have reached their highest point in approximately 1,000 years, placing the region in what scientists call a "critically loaded state." The research suggests the tectonic system is now capable of producing a major earthquake involving both fault systems simultaneously.

Liliane Burkhard, the lead researcher at the University of Hawaii at Mānoa, explained the significance of the findings. "Our results show that stress levels on multiple fault segments are now at or above the highest values seen in the past millennium and that the region may be capable of a large through-going rupture involving both fault systems," she said in a statement.

The study, published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth, used computer modeling to analyze how stress accumulates and releases across these fault systems. Researchers input 1,000 years of earthquake history into their simulations to determine current stress levels. They found that more than 160 years have elapsed since the last major rupture, allowing stress to accumulate to unprecedented levels.

A critical element of the research focuses on Cajon Pass, located at the junction where the two faults meet. Scientists suggest this location could act as an "earthquake gate" that either prevents large ruptures from traveling between the faults or allows both systems to rupture together in a single event. Burkhard noted that the conditions determining whether this gate opens depend on how closely stress levels align on both fault systems at the moment of rupture.

"Right now, with stress at historically high levels across the region and more than 160 years elapsed since the last major rupture, the system is in a critically loaded state," Burkhard said. A potential joint rupture of both the San Andreas and San Jacinto faults could have significant impacts across Southern California.

The research does not predict when an earthquake might occur, but scientists emphasize its importance for understanding regional risks. Burkhard stressed that while the exact timing remains beyond current scientific capability, understanding the range of possible scenarios is crucial for preparedness. "What we can say is that the system is critically stressed, and that physics-based models like this one give us a clearer picture of the range of scenarios we should be prepared for," she said.

California has maintained rigorous earthquake preparedness efforts for decades. The state has implemented strict building codes and emergency response systems designed to minimize damage and protect lives. The devastating 1994 Northridge earthquake in Los Angeles killed more than 60 people and destroyed 87,000 homes and businesses, underscoring why ongoing preparedness remains essential.

These findings reinforce the importance of continued earthquake readiness across Southern California, particularly in densely populated areas near active fault zones where millions of residents live and work.

Argentina's victory provides momentum heading into their next group stage match against Austria in Arlington, Texas on Monday. Coach Lionel Scaloni had downplayed expectations before the tournament began, but three points position Argentina well as they pursue another deep World Cup run.

The Trump administration's Justice Department is moving to intervene in a federal lawsuit challenging Evanston, Illinois' housing reparations program for Black residents. Officials argue the initiative violates constitutional protections and amounts to unlawful racial discrimination.

The Evanston City Council created the program in 2019 by dedicating $10 million in cannabis sales tax revenue to compensate Black residents for historical housing discrimination. The program represents the first local reparations effort of its kind in the United States, designed to address documented past policies that restricted where Black residents could live and limited their access to housing and wealth-building opportunities.

The Justice Department's intervention represents a direct federal challenge to local reparations initiatives that have gained momentum across the country in recent years. Federal prosecutors contend that while municipalities may have legitimate interests in addressing historical discrimination, distributing benefits based solely on race violates equal protection principles. The administration argues such programs fail to meet the strict constitutional scrutiny required for race-based classifications.

The lawsuit has drawn challenges from opponents who contend the race-based eligibility requirements are unconstitutional. These opponents argue that channeling resources exclusively to Black residents violates civil rights protections.

The outcome of this case could influence how other cities and counties structure reparations programs. Several jurisdictions nationwide have explored or implemented similar initiatives to compensate Black residents for the lingering effects of slavery, segregation, and discriminatory housing policies like redlining.

The central legal question involves whether race-conscious remedies designed to address documented historical discrimination can withstand constitutional challenge. Proponents of the Evanston program maintain it constitutes a narrowly tailored response to specific harms caused by past government policies that devastated Black wealth accumulation and homeownership opportunities.

The Justice Department's decision to intervene establishes a test case for how far local governments can proceed in crafting race-based remedies for historical wrongs. The federal position taken in this case will likely shape legal strategies for municipalities considering similar approaches.

Supporters of reparations programs argue they represent necessary steps toward addressing systemic inequalities rooted in slavery and segregation. The Evanston program specifically focuses on housing discrimination, one of the most consequential forms of discrimination that depressed Black wealth across generations.

The case reflects broader national debates about how communities should address historical injustices. As the case proceeds through federal court, it will determine whether the Evanston program can move forward and potentially set precedent for other jurisdictions exploring reparations initiatives.
