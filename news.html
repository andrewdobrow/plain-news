A few minutes ago

A federal judge in Boston has invalidated a Trump administration policy that imposed a $100,000 annual fee on H-1B visa applications for highly skilled foreign workers. U.S. District Judge Leo Sorokin ruled in a 42-page decision that the fee constituted an unlawful tax that violated federal administrative law and the Constitution.

The ruling came in response to a lawsuit filed by 20 Democratic state attorneys general challenging the fee Trump announced in September. The decision vacated the sweeping charge, which represented a 20-to-50 fold increase over existing H-1B visa fees.

Sorokin found that the fee amounted to a tax rather than a regulatory restriction. Since the Constitution grants Congress, not the president, exclusive power to levy taxes, Trump lacked the authority to impose the charge. The judge cited the 2026 Supreme Court case Learning Resources v. Trump, which previously undermined a key element of Trump's tariff strategy, to support his decision.

The Trump administration had attempted to justify the fee under the Immigration and Nationality Act, but the judge determined this legislation does not include taxing authority. Trump imposed the charge through presidential proclamation, arguing that the H-1B program had enabled the "large-scale replacement of American workers." Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick claimed at the time that major technology companies supported the measure and urged firms to train Americans rather than hire foreign workers.

The H-1B visa program, established in 1990, permits U.S. employers to hire skilled foreign workers in specialty occupations for up to six years. The program allocates 65,000 visas annually, with an additional 20,000 reserved for workers holding advanced degrees from U.S. institutions. Roughly two-thirds of H-1B positions are computer-related.

The ruling delivers significant relief to Silicon Valley, which depends on the program more heavily than any other sector of the economy. Amazon alone received approval for more than 10,000 H-1B visas in the first half of 2025, while Microsoft and Meta each exceeded 5,000 approvals.

Business groups and technology companies had opposed the fee as prohibitively expensive and likely to damage American competitiveness in attracting global talent. Supporters of the fee had argued it would encourage companies to prioritize hiring American workers and generate revenue for the federal government.

The H-1B program remains a subject of ongoing debate. Critics argue that some companies misuse it to replace American workers with lower-paid foreign employees, while supporters contend it addresses critical skill gaps in the U.S. workforce.

The Trump administration is widely expected to appeal the ruling to a higher court.

Both companies emphasized that their IPO filings do not guarantee immediate public debuts. Anthropic similarly noted that going public "will depend on market conditions and other factors," with the company expected to potentially go public as early as fall 2026.

The confidential filing process, which both companies are using, allows the SEC to review financial disclosures before the investor prospectus becomes public. This approach has become standard for major firms preparing for large debuts, with SpaceX also using the same method as it seeks a stock market float at a valuation of approximately $1.75 trillion.

OpenAI's corporate structure has drawn investor attention and raises questions about how its unique governance model will translate to public markets. The company operates as a capped-profit entity controlled by a nonprofit board, a structure designed to balance commercial interests with stated safety and transparency goals.

The IPO filings underscore the rising financial stakes in the AI sector. Beyond OpenAI and Anthropic, SpaceX and other major AI-focused companies are pursuing public listings this year. Market conditions, regulatory approval, and investor appetite will all influence the final timing and terms of any eventual offerings. The confidential filing process provides flexibility while these companies work through regulatory requirements and assess optimal market conditions for their debuts.

Russian satellites tested capability to jam GPS across Europe

5:56 PM ET

Tests conducted across Europe indicate that Russian satellites possess the capability to interfere with GPS signals on a continental scale, raising questions about Russian intentions and potential security implications. The discovery follows a pattern of mysterious GPS interference that has affected multiple European nations.

The interference capabilities suggest a technical advancement in satellite-based electronic warfare systems. GPS jamming can disrupt navigation systems used in civilian aviation, shipping, transportation networks, and emergency services. The continental scale of the potential interference marks a significant expansion beyond localized jamming operations that have been documented in conflict zones.

European authorities have been investigating unexplained GPS disruptions that have occurred across the continent. The tests now provide evidence linking these disruptions to Russian satellite operations. GPS systems rely on precise timing signals from satellites, and jamming technology can overwhelm or distort these signals, rendering navigation systems unreliable or completely inoperative.

The revelation adds to existing concerns about Russian activities in space and electronic warfare capabilities. Military analysts note that GPS jamming represents a form of hybrid warfare that can affect civilian infrastructure without conventional military action. The technology could be used to disrupt transportation, logistics, and communications across large geographic areas.

Recent assessments have raised additional concerns about Russian satellite capabilities in other regions. Ukrainian intelligence has assessed that Russian satellites conducted detailed imagery surveys of military facilities and critical sites across the Middle East, including US bases and other targets. According to the assessment, Russian satellites surveyed at least 24 areas across 11 Middle Eastern countries between March 21 and 31, covering 46 military and strategic objects including bases, airports, and oilfields.

The timing of these surveys appears significant. Within days of being surveyed, military bases and headquarters were targeted by Iranian ballistic missiles and drones. In one documented case, a Russian satellite imaged Prince Sultan airbase in Saudi Arabia days before Iran struck the facility on March 27, hitting a US E-3 Sentry airborne warning and control system aircraft. A Russian satellite then passed over the location again to assess the damage, the assessment claimed. Russian satellites were also actively surveying the Strait of Hormuz during this period.

Western military sources and regional security officials provided separate intelligence supporting these assessments. Russia's defense ministry did not respond to requests for comment on these claims.

Questions remain about Russian motives for developing and testing such capabilities. The continental-scale reach of GPS jamming suggests the system could be deployed for strategic purposes during heightened tensions or conflict. European defense officials are likely reassessing their reliance on GPS systems and considering backup navigation technologies that would be resistant to satellite-based jamming operations.

6:26 PM ET

The White House has relaunched negotiations with Congress to establish federal preemption of some state artificial intelligence laws, according to sources familiar with the discussions. The talks aim to trade a tech industry priority for support on other policy goals, as states increasingly pass their own AI regulations.

Sen. Marsha Blackburn is leading the negotiations, which would pair federal override authority with legislation focused on protecting children online and combating deepfake content. The discussions reflect growing pressure on the Trump administration to act as individual states move ahead with their own AI regulatory frameworks.

The negotiations represent a significant shift in the balance between state and federal authority over emerging technology. States have moved to fill what they view as a regulatory gap, passing laws that address AI deployment, transparency requirements, and consumer protections. The tech industry has pushed back against this patchwork of state regulations, arguing that a single federal standard would provide more clarity and reduce compliance costs.

The White House effort signals that the administration views federal action on AI as increasingly urgent. By linking preemption to popular measures like child safety protections and deepfake restrictions, negotiators hope to build a coalition broad enough to pass comprehensive legislation. The strategy acknowledges that neither side can advance its priorities independently in the current political environment.

The outcome of these talks could determine whether states retain the ability to regulate AI within their borders or whether Washington establishes nationwide standards that supersede local laws. Industry groups have long sought federal preemption, while consumer advocates and some state officials have defended the right of states to experiment with different regulatory approaches. The negotiations are ongoing, with no timeline announced for reaching an agreement.

The changes also restrict how learners can modify their bookings. Drivers can only switch their scheduled test to one of the three nearest centers to their original booking location. The government introduced the measures following thousands of missed appointments.
