3:17 PM ET

The Supreme Court ruled 8-1 on Monday to allow the Trump administration to proceed with border wall construction in the Big Bend region of southwest Texas, lifting a lower court injunction that had halted the project. The court found that the federal judge who blocked construction lacked authority to do so and said the administration was extremely likely to prevail on that issue. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson was the lone dissenter.

The dispute centers on three waivers issued by Homeland Security secretaries Kristi Noem and Markwayne Mullin between February and July that allow the government to bypass environmental and conservation laws for the project. The planned construction covers 341 miles in the sparsely populated area along the Rio Grande, including parts of Big Bend National Park. The administration said 89,000 people have been detained attempting to cross there in recent years.

District Judge Kathleen Cardone had invalidated the waivers in October, ruling that the Homeland Security secretary lacked such broad powers under a 1996 law. The administration argued that Congress granted it wide authority to build border infrastructure without regulatory delays. The Supreme Court said the government has compelling interests when border security policies are challenged.

The case was brought by the Center for Biological Diversity, Friends of the Ruidosa Church, and a local property owner who argued construction would permanently damage the landscape and threaten historic and cultural sites. They contended the Big Bend area has the lowest migrant apprehension rates along the southern border, making the waivers inappropriate. Government data confirms Big Bend has the fewest encounters between federal agents and migrants of any border section. The administration has said completing the wall there is necessary to prevent the area from becoming an entry point if other sections are built.

3:20 PM ET

France has formally accused Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps of orchestrating cyberattacks that sabotaged supermarket refrigeration systems across France and Europe last month, destroying tons of food and marking what French officials called an unprecedented act of sabotage. Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot announced that France had summoned the Iranian ambassador to Paris and said the government would impose sanctions on those responsible. Iran has not officially responded, though some Iranian media outlets have dismissed the allegations as baseless.

The initial attack on October 11 targeted software controlling refrigeration units in French supermarkets, causing temperatures in some stores to spike to 50 degrees Celsius overnight. More than 100 Coopérative U stores, 180 Intermarché and Netto locations, and outlets operated by Carrefour, Monoprix, and E.Leclerc were affected. The hackers compromised software provided by Danish engineering firm Danfoss that allows retailers to manage cold storage units remotely. A second wave of attacks hit more than 100 stores across Belgium, Luxembourg, Sweden, Ireland, the Netherlands, Estonia, and Finland earlier this month.

Paris prosecutor Laure Beccuau announced a criminal investigation targeting the head of an IRGC-affiliated offensive cyber unit called Shahid Kaveh, along with two deputies. The unit is believed to be part of the IRGC's electronic and cyber center. Shahid Kaveh is a name associated with a cluster of hackers thought to be directly employed by the Iranian government.

A separate incident in August involving malfunctioning refrigeration at U.S. military bases is also being investigated as a deliberate cyberattack, according to sources cited by Reuters. French authorities have not disclosed how they attributed the attacks to Iran, and experts note that attribution of cyberattacks is extremely difficult because hackers can conceal their locations. Two Russian-aligned hacktivist groups claimed responsibility last week, but few believed them.

Nvidia shares fell approximately 4 percent by close of trading Monday, erasing about $250 billion in market value, following the company's announcement that its board would conduct an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang's affair with an alleged Chinese spy. The board action came after Huang admitted in a company message that he had an affair with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based chip company Megaspeed, whom U.S. officials believe was recruited by Chinese intelligence to influence him on chip export policy. The investigation follows congressional scrutiny that began last week, when House Republican committee chairs requested a briefing from Huang and Sen. Chris Coons pledged an investigation after reports surfaced of the relationship.

Multiple news outlets reported that Alice Huang was recruited by Chinese intelligence officials to influence Jensen Huang's position on U.S. chip export policy toward China. A memo circulating among U.S. policymakers earlier this year claimed that Alice Huang had been recruited by China's Ministry of State Security to spy on Nvidia's CEO. The New York Times and Financial Times reported she was specifically tasked with convincing him to lobby U.S. officials to allow exports of advanced chips to China, and the Financial Times also reported that the two had a romantic relationship.

In his message to the company, Jensen Huang acknowledged the affair without mentioning Alice Huang by name. He wrote that he was unfaithful to his wife and that his actions hurt her and their children, adding that he had fallen short of his responsibility to the company. He said he was committed to protecting Nvidia and supporting America's national security and would cooperate with the investigation.

Members of the U.S. House Foreign Affairs Committee requested that Jensen Huang appear for a closed-door briefing next week on his interactions with Alice Huang and Nvidia's security protocols around espionage. Congressmen Brian Mast, John Moolenaar, and Rick Crawford wrote in a letter that recent reports raise significant national security concerns and that it is vital Congress determines the extent to which U.S. national security has been placed at risk.

The board said in its announcement that it has no reason to believe that either national security or company secrets were compromised. The investigation will be conducted by independent directors working with outside legal counsel. President Donald Trump called Huang an honourable guy, and Elon Musk also voiced support for the CEO to continue leading the firm. Analysts suggested the hit to the company would be short-lived, with Victoria Fernandez of Crossmark Global Investments saying investors would not abandon Nvidia because the chipmaker is so important to the AI industry. The Chinese government has denied involvement in any espionage operation.

A few minutes ago

Multiple shareholder-rights law firms announced investigations into Nvidia on Monday, the first trading day after the company's board said it would investigate Chief Executive Officer Jensen Huang over his relationship with Alice Huang, an executive U.S. intelligence believes was spying for China. The company's stock fell more than 4 percent. Schall, Brown & Schwartz, a Los Angeles firm, said it is investigating whether Nvidia made false or misleading statements or failed to disclose information pertinent to investors. Other firms including Rosen Law Firm, Levi & Korsinsky, and Bronstein, Gewirtz & Grossman announced similar investigations, with Rosen saying it is preparing a class action lawsuit.

Securities law experts say the legal theory centers on statements Nvidia made last month when The New York Times first reported on the relationship. After that initial report, a company spokesperson told The Hill the relationship was not unusual or unexpected and that meetings with customers are part of a CEO's job. Huang has since acknowledged to the board that the relationship was intimate, and The Times reports U.S. intelligence had been monitoring Alice Huang, an executive at Singapore data center company Megaspeed, for more than a year.

Robert Bartlett, a Stanford Law professor and securities law expert, said the company's earlier statement was clearly misleading from a securities perspective. Because Huang is CEO, his knowledge is legally imputed to the company, meaning Nvidia is treated as having known what its chief executive knew. Bartlett said he expects plaintiffs to bring a fraud on the market claim, in which investors argue they paid prices that reflected false or incomplete public information and lost money when the truth emerged.

Nvidia is one of the most valuable companies in the world, so a settlement could prove exceedingly expensive. Such cases typically are litigated for a long time before settling, resulting in large payments from the company and its directors and officers insurers. Bartlett said the irony is that the suit is brought in the name of Nvidia's shareholders, but shareholders ultimately pay for the settlement, directly or through insurance premiums. Under federal securities law, the first complaint triggers a public notice, giving the filing firm a head start on signing up investors with large losses.

James Gunn and Peter Safran have been removed as co-CEOs and chairmen of DC Studios after four years leading the comic book film division, throwing the future of Superman, Batman, and other major franchises into question. Marvel veteran Jeremy Latcham, who produced superhero films including Iron Man, The Avengers, and Spider-Man: Homecoming, will take over leadership of the studio.

Gunn and Safran launched a rebooted DC Universe in 2025 with Superman, starring David Corenswet as the Man of Steel, which earned $618 million worldwide. However, their subsequent films had mixed results. Supergirl bombed with $126 million against a $170 million budget, while Clayface became a low-budget success with $109 million from a $44 million budget. The duo will fulfill remaining creative commitments, most notably the Superman sequel Man of Tomorrow, which wrapped filming in late August and brings back Corenswet alongside new cast members including Lars Eidinger as Brainiac.

The leadership change leaves numerous planned projects uncertain. Gunn had been searching for an actor to play Batman in the rebooted universe, separate from Robert Pattinson's version in Matt Reeves' films. Reeves' Batman: Part II is scheduled for February 2028, but Reeves had to step away from production due to a family matter, with Steven Soderbergh stepping in to help finish the shoot. Wonder Woman has been absent from DC films since 2020's Wonder Woman 1984, though rumors suggest Adria Arjona may debut as the character in Man of Tomorrow.

Other projects in limbo include a potential second season of the Lanterns HBO series starring Aaron Pierre and Kyle Chandler, and a second season of Peacemaker starring John Cena, which has neither been renewed nor canceled. Various announced films including projects focused on Swamp Thing, Bane, and Deathstroke never received official greenlights and now appear unlikely to proceed under new leadership.

House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries said Monday he will work to include provisions in the annual defense policy bill that would tighten restrictions on advanced chip exports to China. Jeffries told reporters he strongly supports measures currently in the Senate version of the National Defense Authorization Act that would restrict chip access to China and will push to ensure those provisions make it into the final legislation.

Jeffries spoke a day after Nvidia's board announced an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang's relationship with a woman suspected of being a Chinese spy. His support gives bipartisan momentum to Senate efforts as Democrats prepare to take control of both chambers following their strong midterm performance.

The Senate legislation, called the AI Overwatch Act, would allow Congress to review licenses for selling advanced artificial intelligence chips to China and other countries of concern. It would also help track where exported chips end up and tighten restrictions on sales of equipment used to manufacture them.

Senators Elizabeth Warren and Jim Banks also called Monday for including the AI Overwatch Act in the defense bill. The senators cited reports that Nvidia had secured approval to export hundreds of thousands of advanced AI chips to China, including to companies with ties to the Chinese military. They said Huang's confirmed relationship with a suspected Chinese intelligence operative makes national security concerns more urgent and raises questions about whether those activities were connected.
