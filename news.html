Liverpool have completed negotiations with Paris Saint-Germain to sign winger Bradley Barcola in a deal worth up to £123 million. The 23-year-old French international will undergo a medical examination this weekend before finalising his move to Anfield.

The two clubs reached an agreement on the transfer structure following extended discussions, with Liverpool identifying Barcola as their primary summer target. The package is comprised of £106 million paid upfront, with an additional £17 million in add-ons dependent on individual and team performance.

Barcola has already agreed personal terms with Liverpool, with his representatives confirming the move last month. The winger will become the second-most expensive signing in the club's history, behind striker Alexander Isak, once he completes his medical and formalises the transfer.

At PSG, Barcola established himself as one of Europe's most promising attacking talents. His combination of pace and technical ability has drawn attention from elite clubs across the continent. The 23-year-old's performances for the French champions demonstrated the quality and potential that convinced Liverpool to commit significant resources to securing his signature.

The transfer reflects the current market reality for attacking players, with top-tier wingers commanding premium fees. Liverpool's decision to invest heavily in Barcola underscores their ambition to strengthen their squad as they pursue major honours domestically and in European competition.

The deal marks another significant piece of Liverpool's summer recruitment strategy. The Merseyside club have also brought in centre-back Ronald Araújo on loan from Barcelona, winger Víctor Muñoz from Osasuna, and defender Jérémy Jacquet from Rennes, whose £55 million transfer was agreed in January.

Barcola's arrival addresses a priority area for Liverpool as they prepare for the upcoming season. Manager Arne Slot will be keen to integrate the French winger into his squad, with hopes that he can make an immediate impact in the Premier League.

The medical examination scheduled for this weekend represents the final step before Barcola becomes an official Liverpool player. Once completed, the winger will join his new teammates at Anfield and begin preparations for his debut in English football.

A glacier collapse and subsequent flooding on the Nepal-Tibet border has killed at least 543 people, with more than 1,500 missing across both countries. Chinese authorities disclosed that 261 of the 558 people missing in Gyirong County, Tibet are foreign nationals from 23 countries, offering rare detail about the disaster on the Chinese side of the border.

The catastrophic flooding occurred near Mount Kailash, a sacred mountain that draws pilgrims from across Asia. Many of those affected were international visitors in the region for guided treks, work projects, or religious pilgrimages. Thousands have been rescued by helicopter, though rescue efforts have faced obstacles from difficult terrain and concerns about additional flooding.

Nepal has reported the highest death toll, with 538 deaths confirmed and 977 people missing. Nepali authorities said 3,253 people have been rescued by helicopter, and more than 150 injured have been taken to hospitals in Kathmandu and surrounding districts. The missing on the Nepali side include 113 Nepali tourists, 85 military and police personnel, and 161 residents from the Rasuwa district.

India reported 288 Indian nationals unaccounted for, including 73 people working on a power project. Many Indian tourists were on pilgrimage to Mount Kailash, considered a sacred site for Hindus.

Other countries with significant numbers of missing citizens include Ukraine with 55 people missing, the United States with 90 unaccounted for, Malaysia with 51, Australia with 39, and Britain with 33. Canada, South Korea, Singapore, Germany, Russia, South Africa, Latvia, Japan, and New Zealand each reported missing citizens in the single digits to low double digits. Smaller numbers of missing people have also been reported from France, Belgium, Spain, Italy, Kazakhstan, Portugal, Ireland, the Netherlands, and several other countries.

Rescue operations have faced challenges beyond terrain. A group of approximately 100 people became trapped in a tunnel at a hydropower project swamped by the flooding. Rescue teams had to suspend operations temporarily when a debris dam burst its banks, creating risks of fresh flooding and forcing residents to rush up hillsides to escape possible river surges.

Chinese state media has provided limited information about the disaster on the Chinese side of the border, with footage becoming difficult to find on China's censored internet. The announcement about foreign nationals represents one of the more detailed updates from Chinese officials since the flooding began. China's foreign ministry noted that nearly 100 Chinese citizens were also missing on the Nepal side of the border.

The death toll and missing numbers may vary as rescue efforts continue and data collection methods differ among various agencies and countries.

5:01 AM ET

The U.S. Postal Service is moving forward with a new regulation that would restrict mail-in voting despite court orders currently blocking its implementation. The rule, which the agency prepared to publish in the Federal Register, stems from a March executive order signed by Donald Trump.

Under the proposed regulation, states would be required to provide the federal government with lists of eligible voters and adopt new balloting procedures. The Postal Service would refuse to deliver mail-in ballots from states that fail to comply. The rule requires states to submit voter names and barcodes linked to mail-in ballots for federal elections to a federal database operated by the Department of Homeland Security.

Two federal courts, one in California and one in Massachusetts, have issued injunctions blocking the changes from taking effect. Nevertheless, the Trump administration is proceeding with implementation plans. According to the rule text, if courts lift the injunctions, the USPS intends to activate the system immediately, giving election officials time to comply before mail-in ballots are typically sent in September.

The federal government has sued 30 states and Washington D.C. for refusing to surrender voter lists. The USPS received more than 200,000 public comments during the rulemaking process on the proposed changes.

Nearly one in three Americans voted by mail in 2024. Mail voting expanded significantly during the pandemic and has remained popular across party lines. Legal experts and voting rights advocates have raised concerns about potential disenfranchisement, particularly for elderly and disabled Americans who depend on mail ballots.

Morris County in New Jersey has already announced plans to opt out of vote-by-mail services in response to the anticipated changes. The timing of the regulation has drawn scrutiny as the nation prepares for elections. Federal courts in multiple jurisdictions are weighing emergency motions and preliminary injunctions related to the rule.

The Postal Service has not publicly detailed the specific operational changes underlying the regulation or provided data on how many voters might be affected. Analysis from Vox suggests that Supreme Court precedent on election administration could influence whether the changes survive legal challenges. The Atlantic has criticized the Supreme Court's approach to evaluating factual claims in election-related cases.

The legal battle represents the latest conflict in ongoing disputes over election administration and ballot access. The outcome of the litigation could significantly reshape how Americans vote in upcoming elections. Any restrictions on mail ballot access would require millions of voters to change their voting plans, potentially creating logistical challenges for election officials managing in-person voting locations.

Yesterday, 12:01 AM ET

President Donald Trump announced Friday that his administration has reached an agreement with Venezuela's interim president Delcy Rodríguez that would give the United States majority control over 65 billion barrels of proven oil reserves in the South American country.

Trump stated the deal on his social media platform, noting that it was brokered through a partnership with private business interests. Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth led the negotiations.

Rodríguez, Venezuela's interim president, characterized the arrangement as a measure intended to help revive her country's struggling economy. The Trump administration described the agreement as historic, representing a significant shift in US-Venezuelan energy relations.

The specific terms and mechanisms of the deal remain unclear. Trump's announcement did not provide details about how oil extraction, distribution, and revenue sharing would be structured, or the timeline for implementation.

This arrangement marks a notable departure from previous US approaches to Venezuela. The agreement involves working directly with private Venezuelan business entities that maintain connections to the Venezuelan government, a strategy that represents a change in diplomatic positioning.

The deal has sparked criticism from Venezuelan opposition groups. Reports of the US attempt to secure a substantial and long-term stake in Venezuela's energy reserves have generated significant backlash, with opponents describing the move as predatory and unconstitutional. Critics have characterized the arrangement as a land grab that prioritizes American interests over Venezuelan sovereignty.

The Venezuelan opposition's concerns center on the long-term implications of ceding control over the country's most valuable natural resource to foreign interests. They argue that such an agreement undermines national autonomy and resource management.

The agreement could have substantial economic implications for both nations, depending on implementation details and whether it leads to increased oil production and exports. Access to Venezuela's vast oil reserves positions American energy interests in a significant way as global energy markets remain a priority.

The Trump administration's willingness to directly engage with Venezuelan entities connected to the government represents a notable strategic shift. Previous administrations maintained greater distance from Venezuela's government and associated businesses, particularly during periods of political tension.

The deal comes at a time when the Trump administration has been actively reshaping US energy policy. However, this Venezuelan agreement contrasts sharply with recent administration actions in other energy sectors. In March, federal officials announced they would pay nearly $1 billion in taxpayer dollars to French energy firm TotalEnergies to cancel major offshore wind projects off the coasts of New York and North Carolina. That arrangement has prompted seven states to file lawsuits challenging its legality.

The Venezuelan oil agreement now awaits implementation as both countries work to structure the practical details of how American interests will gain access to and benefit from the nation's extensive proven reserves.

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope delivered to launch pad for Sunday launch

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope and its SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket have arrived at the launch pad at Kennedy Space Center in Florida ahead of a scheduled Sunday liftoff at 7:26 a.m. ET. A few minutes ago

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope and its SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket have arrived at the launch pad at Kennedy Space Center in Florida ahead of a scheduled Sunday liftoff at 7:26 a.m. ET. The mission represents the next major step in NASA's effort to unlock fundamental mysteries about the universe.

The Roman Space Telescope is designed to search for exoplanets and investigate dark energy, dark matter, and the structure of the cosmos. With a 300-megapixel infrared camera, the telescope will offer the same sharpness as the Hubble Space Telescope but with a field of view at least 100 times larger. Each image will capture a patch of sky approximately 1.5 times the apparent size of a full moon.

The observatory will travel one million miles into deep space to reach Lagrange Point 2, where it will operate in the same orbit as the James Webb Space Telescope, though the two will remain far apart. Unlike the deep, focused observations made by other telescopes, Roman will conduct wide-field surveys that allow astronomers to study large portions of the universe simultaneously.

Named after Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first chief astronomer who played a crucial role in planning the Hubble Space Telescope, the new observatory carries a specialized instrument called a coronagraph. This device blocks glare from stars, making it easier to photograph planets around them in detail.

The telescope project represents years of development. The need was conceived at the end of the last century, with development beginning in 2016 and full project approval coming in 2020. Julie McEnery, NASA's senior project scientist for the Roman mission, explained that one key motivation stemmed from discoveries about the universe's expansion. "One of the motivations for Roman was the discovery that our universe is not just expanding, but that expansion is accelerating," she said.

The $4.3 billion project survived two rounds of proposed White House budget cuts before finally reaching the launch pad. Congress rejected a 2025 proposal to terminate the Roman project, restoring NASA's 2026 funding to $24.4 billion.

Once operational, images from Roman will arrive in early 2027 after months of calibration and testing. NASA expects to make the imagery immediately public, allowing professional researchers and amateur astronomers unprecedented opportunities for analysis. The telescope's initial five-year mission could extend to ten years.

Roman will work alongside existing telescopes to provide comprehensive views of the cosmos. While Hubble and Webb can conduct detailed follow-up observations, Roman's panoramic images will uncover interesting targets and provide context around objects observed by other telescopes.

This launch marks the final flight of SpaceX's Falcon Heavy rocket as the company phases out older rocket models to focus on Starship development.

4:24 AM ET

Country music legend Dolly Parton has died at age 80 following a battle with cancer, according to reports released this week. The singer, songwriter, actress, and philanthropist was one of the most influential figures in American entertainment, with a career that transformed country music and extended far beyond the genre.

Parton rose to prominence in the 1960s after moving to Nashville at 18 to pursue a recording career. She quickly became a songwriter for Combine Publishing before signing with Monument Records as an artist. Her early career included eight years as a television personality on Porter Wagoner's popular variety show starting in 1967, though her solo recordings initially outsold her duets with Wagoner.

Her breakthrough came after leaving Wagoner in 1974. She wrote "I Will Always Love You" as a farewell to him, and the song became a signature hit. The track gained international recognition when Whitney Houston recorded it for the 1992 film "The Bodyguard," becoming one of the best-selling singles ever made. Parton's refusal to cede publishing rights to the song, even when Elvis Presley requested them, proved to be a pivotal decision.

With a distinctive soprano voice featuring a remarkable vibrato and east Tennessee twang, Parton became Nashville's most recognized female export. Her catalog of hits included "Jolene" and numerous other songs that explored themes of love, loss, and female empowerment. Over her lifetime, she earned 10 Grammy Awards, 11 Academy of Country Music Awards, and many other accolades.

Beyond music, Parton built a substantial business empire. She starred in films including "9 to 5" and "Steel Magnolias," and established Dollywood, the theme park in Tennessee that became a major attraction.

Parton's charitable contributions defined much of her later life. She founded the Imagination Library in 1995, a program that has distributed free books to millions of children worldwide, inspired by her father's inability to read. During the COVID-19 pandemic, she donated one million dollars to vaccine research at Vanderbilt University Medical Center. She also provided aid to people affected by wildfires and hurricanes.

Known for her wit and maximalist approach to life, Parton became a cultural icon whose appeal transcended generational and genre boundaries. Her distinctive style, featuring her signature platinum hair and elaborate fashion choices, made her a fashion icon in her own right. She was one of the first country stars to achieve mainstream crossover success, playing a major role in bringing country music to wider audiences.

Tributes have poured in from across the entertainment, music, and political worlds, celebrating her lasting impact on culture and her generous spirit.

The comments represent a notable departure from typical conservative media support for Trump. Carlson framed the issue as a fundamental question of presidential fitness rather than as a partisan political attack. His remarks come amid ongoing discussions about the scope of presidential war powers and military operations in the Middle East.

The source material provided does not specify what prompted Carlson's statement or whether he possesses direct knowledge of Trump considering nuclear options. The White House has not commented on the remarks.

Recent developments in the Trump administration's foreign policy include a nuclear cooperation agreement signed with Saudi Arabia. Under the deal, the kingdom would be enabled to enrich its own uranium for nuclear reactors. At a House Foreign Affairs Committee hearing, Democratic Congressman Brad Sherman called for an oversight hearing of the agreement, suggesting the technology transfer could be as significant a mistake as arming the Shah of Iran during the 1970s.

Initial reports suggest it could allow uranium enrichment in Saudi Arabia, potentially opening a pathway to nuclear weapons development in the kingdom. This contrasts with a similar 2009 agreement between the Obama administration and the United Arab Emirates, which included safeguards preventing the Emirates from developing nuclear weapons and committed the country to accepting International Atomic Energy Agency inspections.

Presidential authority over nuclear weapons has been an ongoing subject of legal and policy debate. Some lawmakers have proposed legislation requiring congressional approval before any first use of nuclear weapons. Currently, no such restrictions exist, leaving the decision solely with the president.

Carlson's statement adds a new dimension to political discussions as attention focuses on Trump's second term. Whether his comments reflect broader concerns among conservatives or represent an isolated view remains unclear.

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope and its SpaceX Falcon Heavy rocket have arrived at the launch pad at Kennedy Space Center in Florida ahead of a scheduled Sunday liftoff at 7:26 a.m. ET. The mission represents the next major step in NASA's effort to unlock fundamental mysteries about the universe.

The Roman Space Telescope is designed to search for exoplanets and investigate dark energy, dark matter, and the structure of the cosmos. With a 300-megapixel infrared camera, the telescope will offer the same sharpness as the Hubble Space Telescope but with a field of view at least 100 times larger. Each image will capture a patch of sky approximately 1.5 times the apparent size of a full moon.

The observatory will travel one million miles into deep space to reach Lagrange Point 2, where it will operate in the same orbit as the James Webb Space Telescope, though the two will remain far apart. Unlike the deep, focused observations made by other telescopes, Roman will conduct wide-field surveys that allow astronomers to study large portions of the universe simultaneously.

Named after Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first chief astronomer who played a crucial role in planning the Hubble Space Telescope, the new observatory carries a specialized instrument called a coronagraph. This device blocks glare from stars, making it easier to photograph planets around them in detail.

The telescope project represents years of development. The need was conceived at the end of the last century, with development beginning in 2016 and full project approval coming in 2020. Julie McEnery, NASA's senior project scientist for the Roman mission, explained that one key motivation stemmed from discoveries about the universe's expansion. "One of the motivations for Roman was the discovery that our universe is not just expanding, but that expansion is accelerating," she said.

The $4.3 billion project survived two rounds of proposed White House budget cuts before finally reaching the launch pad. Congress rejected a 2025 proposal to terminate the Roman project, restoring NASA's 2026 funding to $24.4 billion.

Once operational, images from Roman will arrive in early 2027 after months of calibration and testing. NASA expects to make the imagery immediately public, allowing professional researchers and amateur astronomers unprecedented opportunities for analysis. The telescope's initial five-year mission could extend to ten years.

Roman will work alongside existing telescopes to provide comprehensive views of the cosmos. While Hubble and Webb can conduct detailed follow-up observations, Roman's panoramic images will uncover interesting targets and provide context around objects observed by other telescopes.

Parton rose to prominence in the 1960s after moving to Nashville at 18 to pursue a recording career. She quickly became a songwriter for Combine Publishing before signing with Monument Records as an artist. Her early career included eight years as a television personality on Porter Wagoner's popular variety show starting in 1967, though her solo recordings initially outsold her duets with Wagoner.

Her breakthrough came after leaving Wagoner in 1974. She wrote "I Will Always Love You" as a farewell to him, and the song became a signature hit. The track gained international recognition when Whitney Houston recorded it for the 1992 film "The Bodyguard," becoming one of the best-selling singles ever made. Parton's refusal to cede publishing rights to the song, even when Elvis Presley requested them, proved to be a pivotal decision.

With a distinctive soprano voice featuring a remarkable vibrato and east Tennessee twang, Parton became Nashville's most recognized female export. Her catalog of hits included "Jolene" and numerous other songs that explored themes of love, loss, and female empowerment. Over her lifetime, she earned 10 Grammy Awards, 11 Academy of Country Music Awards, and many other accolades.

Beyond music, Parton built a substantial business empire. She starred in films including "9 to 5" and "Steel Magnolias," and established Dollywood, the theme park in Tennessee that became a major attraction.

Parton's charitable contributions defined much of her later life. She founded the Imagination Library in 1995, a program that has distributed free books to millions of children worldwide, inspired by her father's inability to read. During the COVID-19 pandemic, she donated one million dollars to vaccine research at Vanderbilt University Medical Center. She also provided aid to people affected by wildfires and hurricanes.

Known for her wit and maximalist approach to life, Parton became a cultural icon whose appeal transcended generational and genre boundaries. Her distinctive style, featuring her signature platinum hair and elaborate fashion choices, made her a fashion icon in her own right. She was one of the first country stars to achieve mainstream crossover success, playing a major role in bringing country music to wider audiences.

Tributes have poured in from across the entertainment, music, and political worlds, celebrating her lasting impact on culture and her generous spirit.

Iceland votes against resuming EU membership talks

A majority of Icelandic voters rejected the government's proposal to restart negotiations on joining the European Union.

A few minutes ago

Iceland voted against restarting EU accession talks in a close referendum, with 52.8 percent of voters rejecting the government's proposal according to public broadcaster RUV. The result halts the current effort to resume membership negotiations with the bloc.

The vote reflects deep divisions within Iceland over the country's relationship with Europe. Those supporting EU membership cite potential economic gains and closer ties to European markets, while opponents argue Iceland can better protect its interests—particularly regarding fisheries—by remaining independent outside the EU.
