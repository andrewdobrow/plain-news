Judge orders Trump administration to restore National Parks displays

A federal judge in Massachusetts has ordered the Trump administration to reinstall displays it removed from National Parks sites over the past year as part of a crackdown on diversity, equity and inclusion content and climate change information at federal facilities.

The ruling came in response to a lawsuit filed by park advocacy organizations against the Interior Department, National Park Service, and its leadership. The judge determined that the removals set "a dangerous precedent of censorship and sanitization" and ordered officials to reinstate the materials within 21 days.

The removals followed an executive order signed by Trump in March 2025 titled "Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History." The order directed the secretary of the interior to examine monuments, memorials, and statues to determine whether they had been altered after January 2020 to represent what the administration called a "false construction of American history."

In a separate case in Pennsylvania, federal judge Cynthia Rufe ordered the National Park Service to restore a slavery exhibit that had been dismantled from Independence National Historical Park in Philadelphia. The exhibit documented the nine enslaved Africans owned by President George Washington who were brought to his official presidential residence during his terms in office. The names of these individuals were etched into a wall at the site: Oney Judge, Austin, Christopher Sheels, Giles, Hercules Posey, Joe Richardson, Moll, Paris, and Richmond.

According to Rufe's decision, historians identified the location of the President's House at the turn of the century through extensive research. The exhibit included information about how Washington rotated enslaved individuals in and out of Pennsylvania to prevent them from petitioning for freedom under state law. Two of the nine escaped their enslavement: Oney Judge fled in 1796 and eventually reached New Hampshire, while Hercules escaped after being brought to Mount Vernon.

In her ruling, Rufe opened with a quote from George Orwell's "1984" about the rewriting of historical records. She drew an explicit comparison between the novel's "Ministry of Truth" and the Trump administration's approach to historical exhibits. "As if the Ministry of Truth in George Orwell's 1984 now existed, with its motto 'Ignorance is Strength,' this Court is now asked to determine whether the federal government has the power it claims to disassemble historical truths when it has domain over historical facts," Rufe wrote. "It does not."

During a hearing, Rufe called the Trump administration's argument that a president could unilaterally order changes to national park exhibits both "horrifying" and "dangerous."

The court ordered the federal government to restore the President's House exhibit to its condition as of January 21, 2026, the day before its removal. Neither the National Park Service nor Philadelphia officials immediately responded to the ruling.
