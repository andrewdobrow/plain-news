South Korea fines Coupang over $400 million for data breach affecting 30 million customers

South Korea's Coupang Fine Becomes Flashpoint in US-Korea Relations

South Korean authorities issued a record-breaking fine exceeding $400 million to e-commerce giant Coupang following a data breach that compromised personal information from 33.7 million customers. The penalty represents one of the largest data protection enforcement actions in South Korean history, but has evolved into a significant diplomatic dispute between Seoul and Washington.

Coupang, often described as South Korea's answer to Amazon, is a US-incorporated company headquartered in Seattle and listed on the New York Stock Exchange. The company is run by Korean-American billionaire Bom Kim. In November, Coupang disclosed that a former employee had stolen an internal security key, enabling unauthorized access to millions of customer accounts.

The breach triggered an aggressive government response in South Korea. Police raided the company's Seoul headquarters, tax authorities launched a special audit, and parliament summoned executives for questioning. Kim declined to travel to Korea for hearings, citing his role as a global chief executive. Korean police requested that immigration authorities notify them if he enters the country.

The strong enforcement action has raised tensions with the Trump administration. South Korean broadcaster SBS reported that Washington signalled it would not proceed with high-level diplomatic and defence consultations unless South Korea guaranteed Kim would face no legal consequences related to the breach. South Korea's foreign ministry has not denied the report but stated that security discussions should proceed separately from the Coupang matter and that investigations would continue under Korean law.

The dispute has affected other bilateral negotiations. Reports indicate that talks on US support for South Korea's development of nuclear-powered submarines have been disrupted, with a scheduled visit from a US delegation postponed.

The issue reflects broader tensions between Seoul and Washington under the Trump administration. In January, Trump threatened to raise tariffs on South Korean goods from 15 percent to 25 percent. In April, 54 Republican members of Congress wrote to South Korea's ambassador accusing Seoul of "discriminatory actions" against US companies and launching a "whole-of-government assault" on Coupang over what they characterized as a "low-sensitivity data leak."

Five US investment firms holding Coupang shares filed notices of intent to pursue arbitration against South Korea under the US-Korea free trade agreement, claiming Seoul's enforcement response was disproportionate compared to similar cases involving Korean companies.

Jaechun Kim, a professor of international relations at Sogang University in Seoul, explained that the Trump administration's approach blurs economic and security issues into a transactional framework. This means disputes like Coupang could spill over into areas previously separated from retaliation, including nuclear cooperation agreements and defence procurement decisions.

"There is a growing sense that the US-ROK relationship may be approaching a critical threshold of strain," Kim said.
