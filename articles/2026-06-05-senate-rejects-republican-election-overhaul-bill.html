Senate rejects Republican election overhaul bill

The Senate has blocked the SAVE America Act, a comprehensive Republican election overhaul that President Trump identified as a top legislative priority for his party. The bill failed to advance through the chamber despite Trump's public push for passage.

The legislation aimed to implement significant changes to federal election procedures. Republican sponsors argued the measure was necessary to address concerns about election security and administration, packaging together multiple reforms into a single proposal.

The Senate vote represents another setback for the Trump administration's agenda in a chamber controlled by Republicans. Despite the party's majority, lawmakers have repeatedly blocked key administration priorities, and this defeat highlights ongoing difficulties in advancing legislation through the closely divided chamber.

Trump had pressed Republican lawmakers to make passage of the bill central to their election policy efforts, signaling its importance to his broader legislative goals. However, the measure fell short of the support needed for passage.

The outcome leaves the future of Republican election policy in question. Party leaders have not yet indicated whether they plan to attempt reviving the legislation in a modified form or will pursue election changes through other legislative approaches. The failure also raises broader questions about the administration's capacity to move its other priorities through the Senate.

The defeat comes as Congress remains engaged in other significant legislative battles. A government shutdown that began two weeks earlier continued as the Senate rejected a Republican plan to fund operations through November 21. The measure fell short on an eighth Senate vote with a tally of 49 to 45, well below the 60 votes required for advancement.

During the shutdown impasse, Democratic and Republican leaders continued to blame each other for the stalemate. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer criticized the Trump administration for approving a $20 billion bailout for Argentina while federal workers faced layoffs. "If this administration has $20bn to spare for a Maga-friendly foreign government, they cannot turn around and say we don't have the money lower healthcare costs here at home," Schumer said.

Senate Majority Leader John Thune countered by accusing Democrats of blocking government funding to extend health insurance subsidies under the Affordable Care Act. He suggested Democrats were using the shutdown strategically, saying their "political calculations are flawed."

House Speaker Mike Johnson refused to negotiate with Senate Democrats, instead accusing them of "playing games." The administration, facing pressure over military payroll during the shutdown, directed the Pentagon to use unspent research and development funds to pay service members, with troops scheduled to receive paychecks beginning October 15.

Democrats questioned the legality of redirecting Pentagon funds, but Johnson defended the move as appropriate. According to a federal workers union filing, more than 4,000 government employees were laid off during the shutdown.
