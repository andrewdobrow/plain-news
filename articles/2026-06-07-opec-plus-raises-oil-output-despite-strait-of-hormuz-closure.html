OPEC Plus increases oil output quotas as Strait of Hormuz closure limits exports

OPEC and its allies have agreed to increase oil production quotas by 188,000 barrels per day, marking the fourth such increase since the Strait of Hormuz effectively shut down due to regional conflict. However, experts widely acknowledge that the move is largely symbolic, as the physical blockade prevents most additional crude from reaching international markets.

The decision comes as peace remains elusive in the Middle East. Iran's blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical shipping channel through which approximately one-fifth of global oil consumption typically passes, has created severe disruptions to world oil exports. The quota increase signals OPEC Plus's willingness and capacity to supply oil globally, but geopolitical circumstances prevent the additional production from actually reaching consumers.

Previous quota increases since the strait closure have similarly failed to translate into greater oil supplies reaching world markets. The alliance, which includes the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries and major producers like Russia, typically adjusts production to manage global prices and supply stability. Current increases appear designed to demonstrate readiness to restore normal supply once the shipping disruption ends, though no timeline for reopening the strait exists.

Global oil markets face unprecedented strain. The inability to move crude through the strait has forced countries to pursue alternative supply routes and sources, driving up costs and creating uncertainty. Global oil prices have reached their highest levels in four years, exceeding $126 per barrel. The situation has particularly harmed countries dependent on strait exports for revenue.

The crisis has exposed deeper fissures within OPEC. The United Arab Emirates shocked the cartel by announcing its exit after 60 years, intending to ignore production quotas and pump as much crude as desired. The UAE's move signals potential conflict within the Gulf oil heavyweights, with Saudi Arabia likely to respond aggressively to defend its market position.

Michael Tamvakis, a commodities professor at Bayes Business School in London, warned that once oil flows resume through Hormuz and prices deflate, a race to maximize export volumes could trigger a price war. Saudi Arabia is expected to aggressively market discounted oil to Asian buyers who also rely on the UAE, while fighting to capture market share in Europe.

The potential price war mirrors previous oil market crashes in the 1980s and 2014, which caused hundreds of thousands of job losses and political instability in oil-rich economies. Economic professor Dieter Helm noted that higher prices encourage more output and suggested the world possesses abundant oil and gas reserves.

The Strait of Hormuz blockade has created opportunities for new market challengers in the Americas. As Gulf exports remain throttled, the United States, Brazil, and Guyana could increase their global market share at the Middle East's expense. Meanwhile, economies are accelerating plans to reduce fossil fuel reliance, potentially hastening the market's long-term decline.
