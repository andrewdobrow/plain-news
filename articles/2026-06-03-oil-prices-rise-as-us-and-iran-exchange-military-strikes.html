Oil prices jump and global stocks fall after Iran and Israel exchange strikes

Oil prices surged and global stock markets experienced sharp declines after Iran and Israel launched attacks against each other, threatening the stability of a fragile ceasefire that had been in place since early April. The renewed hostilities triggered immediate volatility across financial markets as investors reassessed the risks of further escalation in the Middle East.

Technology stocks suffered the most significant losses in Asian trading. Markets in South Korea and Japan were hit particularly hard, with the Kospi soaring 7.5% and the Nikkei 225 gaining more than 5% in earlier trading before reversing course. The sudden reversal highlighted how quickly geopolitical tensions can derail market momentum, as investors fled from riskier assets amid uncertainty about the conflict's trajectory.

The spike in oil prices reflected traders' immediate concerns about potential disruptions to energy supplies. Brent crude oil and US crude futures both climbed as markets priced in the possibility that escalating hostilities could threaten production or disrupt transportation through the strategically critical Strait of Hormuz. The price movements underscored how sensitive global energy markets remain to conflicts in major oil-producing regions.

According to Kathleen Brooks, a research director at XTB, markets will closely monitor whether the ceasefire holds. "Only if the US or Iran walk away from the ceasefire completely and bombing restarts do we see the oil price potentially surging back to the highs of this week above $110 per barrel for Brent crude, which could also cause stocks and bonds to slump," she said.

The market reaction demonstrated how quickly geopolitical events in one region ripple through financial systems worldwide. Military exchanges between two countries rapidly affected stock exchanges across Asia and beyond, influencing everything from technology companies to energy contracts traded globally.

Jim Reid, a markets strategist at Deutsche Bank, noted the uncertainty ahead. "Investors will be breathing a big sigh of relief that an off-ramp out of the war is being taken even as there'll be various elements to watch to see whether this leads to sustained de-escalation," he said. Key questions remained about whether the ceasefire would hold, whether it would extend to Israel's actions in Lebanon, and whether talks could lead to a permanent end to hostilities.

The renewed hostilities cast doubt on the durability of diplomatic efforts to stabilize the region. Market participants now face the challenge of assessing whether the attacks represent a temporary escalation or the beginning of a broader conflict that could sustain elevated oil prices and continued pressure on risk assets. The uncertainty is expected to keep markets volatile until the situation becomes clearer.
