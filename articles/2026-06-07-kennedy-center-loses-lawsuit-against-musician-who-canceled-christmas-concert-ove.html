Kennedy Center Loses Lawsuit Against Musician Who Canceled Christmas Concert Over Trump Name Change

A federal judge dismissed a lawsuit filed by the Kennedy Center against jazz drummer Chuck Redd, who withdrew from a Christmas Eve performance after the venue added former President Donald Trump's name to its official title.

Judge Tanya Jones Bosier ruled that Kennedy Center officials failed to establish a legally binding agreement with Redd. "There's no dispute that he did not sign the 2025 agreement," the judge said, according to the Washington Post. "I could not find a valid breach-of-contract claim here."

The judge dismissed the case under Washington D.C.'s anti-SLAPP law, which protects people from lawsuits designed to silence their speech on matters of public interest. Kennedy Center President Richard Grenell had threatened legal action soon after Redd canceled, calling the withdrawal "this political stunt" and initially seeking one million dollars in damages.

The Kennedy Center filed suit in March and offered a settlement proposal: Redd would pay seven thousand five hundred dollars, perform at this year's Christmas Eve Jazz Jam concert, and refrain from making "political commentary" about his decision to withdraw from the annual free event.

Redd canceled his performance after the White House announced Trump's name would be added to the facility. The venue, formally known as the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts, is designated by Congress as a living memorial to the 35th president.

A separate ruling came the week before the lawsuit dismissal. Another judge ordered the removal of Trump's name from the Kennedy Center's facade and website, determining that the performing arts venue cannot be renamed without an act of Congress.

Lisa J. Banks, one of Redd's lawyers, said in a statement that "the lawsuit against Mr. Redd was political retribution, pure and simple, by the Trump Kennedy Center, and the Court correctly saw it as such in dismissing the case with prejudice."

Redd told the Associated Press he was "very pleased" by the ruling. The case drew attention to tension within the arts community over the Kennedy Center's name change, with the musician's cancellation representing one of the first public protests by a performing artist against the decision.

The judge's dismissal suggests that artists may have more legal protection than previously thought when objecting to venue changes on personal or political grounds, particularly when no signed agreement exists. The ruling's grounding in anti-SLAPP law indicates the court viewed the Kennedy Center's lawsuit as an attempt to suppress Redd's political speech rather than a straightforward contract dispute.
