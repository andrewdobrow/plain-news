Total solar eclipse set to cross Europe and parts of North America Wednesday

A total solar eclipse will occur on August 12, 2026, with the path of totality crossing parts of Europe and offering partial visibility in some U.S. states. The event comes one day after multiple news outlets began tracking the eclipse's approach, providing viewers across multiple continents the opportunity to witness the celestial phenomenon.

The path of totality will primarily traverse Europe, where observers in the direct path will experience complete darkness as the moon passes between Earth and the sun. The Guardian reported that Europe will see the total eclipse, with specific guidance on the best viewing locations and safety protocols for watching the event. CNN detailed how scientists map the path of totality, the narrow corridor where observers can witness the complete blocking of the sun.

In North America, some U.S. states will be positioned to see a partial solar eclipse, where the moon will cover only a portion of the sun. Al Jazeera provided a solar eclipse tracker indicating specific times the eclipse will reach different locations on August 12. WLWT confirmed that certain U.S. states will have visibility of the partial eclipse later this week.

The event represents a significant astronomical occurrence, as total solar eclipses visible from populated land areas are relatively rare. Observers in the path of totality will experience the full eclipse effect, including the dramatic dimming of daylight and the appearance of the sun's corona, the outer atmosphere normally invisible due to the sun's brightness. Those viewing partial phases will see the moon take a bite-shaped section out of the sun's disk.

Safety experts emphasized the importance of proper eye protection when viewing any phase of the eclipse. Looking directly at the sun without appropriate solar filters can cause permanent eye damage. Special eclipse glasses that meet international safety standards are required for direct viewing, while indirect viewing methods such as pinhole projectors offer safe alternatives for observing the event.
