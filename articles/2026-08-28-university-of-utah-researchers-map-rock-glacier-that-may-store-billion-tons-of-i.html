University of Utah researchers map rock glacier that may store billion tons of ice

Scientists at the University of Utah have developed a 3D map of a previously hidden rock glacier on Mount Timpanogos that may contain up to a billion tons of ice. The discovery represents a potential significant water source for the state, with researchers estimating the glacier holds enough water to fill approximately 600 Olympic-sized swimming pools.

The rock glacier, concealed beneath layers of rock and debris, has remained largely unnoticed despite its substantial size. Using advanced mapping technology, the research team created detailed three-dimensional imagery revealing the extent of the ice formation within the mountain. The glacier's location in Utah's Wasatch Range makes it particularly valuable as a potential water resource in a region facing ongoing water scarcity concerns.

Rock glaciers differ from traditional glaciers in that they contain a mixture of ice and rocky debris, making them less visible from the surface. This characteristic allowed the Mount Timpanogos formation to remain undetected until researchers applied specialized mapping techniques. The discovery suggests that other hidden ice reserves may exist in Utah's mountain ranges, potentially altering calculations about the state's water resources.

The research has implications beyond immediate water availability. Understanding the volume and distribution of ice in rock glaciers helps scientists model how these formations respond to climate changes and what role they might play in future water supplies. The team's mapping methodology could be applied to other mountain ranges to identify additional hidden ice reserves.

The findings arrive at a time when water resources across the western United States face increasing pressure. Population growth and changing climate patterns have heightened concerns about water availability in the region. Utah's mountains may hold reserves that could help address these challenges.

The discovery also comes amid growing recognition of how climate change affects glacial systems. Mountain regions worldwide are experiencing rapid ice loss and glacial changes. In the Himalayas, for example, glaciers have retreated significantly in recent decades, with mountains in that region losing close to one-third of their ice in just over 30 years due to global heating.

The University of Utah scientists continue to analyze the data from their 3D mapping to better understand the glacier's composition, stability, and potential as a water source. As researchers work to catalog and understand water resources across western mountain ranges, discoveries like this one provide valuable insights into hidden reserves that may become increasingly important for water management and planning.

The mapping methodology developed for this project could have broader applications in identifying similar formations elsewhere, potentially revealing additional water sources in regions where water availability remains a critical concern.
