Airlines Face $100 Billion Fuel Cost Increase, Air Fare Rises Expected

Global airlines are confronting an additional $100 billion in jet fuel costs this year, a financial shock that will force carriers to raise ticket prices across the industry. The surge stems from disruptions to global oil supplies following the closure of the Strait of Hormuz in March amid conflict with Iran.

Jet fuel prices are expected to be 70 percent higher throughout 2026, leading the International Air Transport Association to project that worldwide airline industry profits will drop to $23 billion, down from previous forecasts of $46 billion. "High oil prices will inevitably mean higher ticket prices," said Willie Walsh, Iata's director general. "There's just no way to avoid that."

The financial pressure is reshaping profit forecasts across the sector, with industry leaders gathered in Rio de Janeiro to address the deteriorating outlook. Some carriers will struggle to survive the fuel price shock caused by the strait closure. Walsh described it as a "challenging and unpredictable time" with "wafer-thin margins," adding that "for a lot of airlines the increase in the fuel bill is potentially existential."

However, Walsh emphasized that concerns about fuel shortages have been resolved and the situation differs from previous crises. The industry remains profitable and is still forecasting growth, with traffic up 2 percent. He noted that outside the Middle East, conditions remain relatively positive.

Passengers should expect varying fare increases depending on route type. Sean Doyle, chief executive of British Airways, indicated that long-haul and business class travelers will likely face the bulk of increases first. "A brand like BA, which has got a lot of long haul, a lot of corporate, a lot of premium, we'd expect maybe to have more pass-through of prices than maybe a carrier who's solely competing for leisure short haul," Doyle explained.

Short-haul holiday flights, which compete heavily on price, are expected to see increases more gradually. According to Iata research, around half of passengers surveyed said they were prepared to spend substantially more on fares if prices tracked oil costs, which Walsh said "bodes well" for a strong summer season in the Northern Hemisphere.

Industry data shows more British and European travelers will fly within Europe than usual, with fewer venturing farther afield given ongoing uncertainty around Gulf hubs.

Beyond fuel costs, Iata raised concerns about Europe's new entry-exit system for border controls, which could create significant delays for summer travelers. The system, set to fully launch by September 7, will require fingerprinting and photographing of most non-EU citizens. Iata warned that processing times could stretch to 90 seconds per passenger compared to the current 20 to 25 seconds, creating potential bottlenecks at airports across the continent during peak travel season.
