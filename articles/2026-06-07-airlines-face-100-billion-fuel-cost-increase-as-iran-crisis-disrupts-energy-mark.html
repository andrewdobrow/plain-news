Airlines Face $100 Billion Fuel Cost Increase as Iran Conflict Disrupts Energy Markets

The global airline industry is bracing for an additional $100 billion in jet fuel expenses this year following the conflict in Iran and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a vital shipping channel for crude oil. The surge in fuel costs has forced carriers to slash profit forecasts and acknowledge that higher ticket prices are unavoidable.

Industry leaders gathered in Rio de Janeiro for the International Air Transport Association's 82nd Annual General Meeting to discuss the financial fallout. With jet fuel prices expected to be 70 percent higher across 2026, collective industry profits worldwide are projected to halve to $23 billion. IATA Director General Willie Walsh stated: "High oil prices will inevitably mean higher ticket prices. There's just no way to avoid that."

Jet fuel represents one of the largest variable costs for airlines, making the price surge particularly damaging to operating margins. While OPEC Plus announced plans to increase output by 188,000 barrels per day, this move offers limited relief given the massive quantities of oil prevented from reaching markets by the Strait of Hormuz closure.

For some carriers, the fuel price shock poses an existential threat. Walsh described the situation as "potentially existential" for many airlines, characterizing it as "a challenging and unpredictable time" with "wafer-thin margins." However, he noted that concerns about actual fuel shortages have diminished, and the industry remains profitable with traffic up 2 percent.

The timing of this crisis complicates an industry still stabilizing after pandemic-related disruptions. Walsh observed that "if you factor out the impact on the Middle East for the rest of the world it remains a pretty positive environment," with expectations for a strong northern summer season.

Fare increases will likely hit certain passenger categories hardest. Sean Doyle, chief executive of British Airways, suggested that long-haul and business passengers would absorb the bulk of price increases, while price-sensitive short-haul holiday flights would face smaller hikes. "If fuel goes up, fares have to go up," Doyle said.

Research from IATA indicates that roughly half of passengers are prepared to spend substantially more on fares if prices track oil costs. This willingness may help offset some impact during peak travel seasons.

Beyond fuel concerns, European airlines face additional headwinds from new border regulations. The EU's entry-exit system, scheduled for full implementation by September 7, will require biometric checks on most non-EU citizens. IATA warned that processing times could extend from 20 to 25 seconds to 90 seconds per passenger, creating significant congestion at European airports during peak travel periods. Rafael Schvartsman, IATA's vice president for Europe, cautioned that "the probability that people will be waiting in lines for a long time is very, very high," particularly affecting major Mediterranean destinations.
