Airlines face $100 billion fuel cost increase as oil prices surge

Global airlines are confronting an additional $100 billion in jet fuel costs this year following the closure of the Strait of Hormuz in March, which disrupted oil supplies during the conflict with Iran. The fuel shock has prompted the International Air Transport Association to slash profit forecasts, with collective industry profits expected to halve to $23 billion as jet fuel prices rise 70% across 2026.

The IATA gathered in Rio de Janeiro to address the mounting financial pressure on carriers worldwide, with fuel representing their single largest operating expense. Multiple carriers have already indicated that ticket price increases appear inevitable to offset the rising costs.

"High oil prices will inevitably mean higher ticket prices," said Willie Walsh, IATA's director general. "There's just no way to avoid that." Walsh acknowledged that industry polling showed passengers were now braced for higher fares and prepared to spend more, though he noted that how long travelers can tolerate the higher costs of connectivity remains uncertain.

Walsh described the situation as "a challenging and unpredictable time" with "wafer-thin margins," adding that "for a lot of airlines the increase in the fuel bill is potentially existential." However, he distinguished the current situation from previous crises, noting that concerns about fuel shortages had subsided and that the industry remained profitable overall with traffic up 2 percent.

Sean Doyle, chief executive of British Airways, acknowledged that fare increases were unavoidable, though he suggested they would affect different routes differently. Long-haul and business passengers would likely face the bulk of increases, while price-sensitive short-haul holiday flights would be the last to see higher fares, he said.

According to IATA research, approximately half of passengers indicated they were prepared to spend substantially more on fares if prices tracked oil costs, which Walsh said "bodes well" for a strong summer season. However, British and European travelers showed a preference for flying within the continent rather than venturing farther afield given uncertainty around Gulf hubs.

The airlines body also raised concerns about the EU's new entry-exit system, which requires biometric checks on most non-EU travelers. Rafael Schvartsman, IATA's vice-president for Europe, warned that the system could create substantial delays, with processing times potentially extending to 90 seconds per passenger rather than the current 20 to 25 seconds. He expressed particular concern about impacts on British travelers, who represent the largest number of tourists to Mediterranean destinations.

While Greece announced it would exempt UK nationals from the new checks, Schvartsman argued the issue affected many airports and could not be resolved through single-country exemptions, noting that American carriers were already adding extra European flights during summer months.
