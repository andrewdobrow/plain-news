Dolphins in Australia independently learn to use shells as hunting tools

Dolphins on opposite sides of Australia have developed the same sophisticated hunting technique without contact between the populations, according to new research. The dolphins chase fish into empty seashells on the ocean floor, then tip the shells to pour their trapped prey into their mouths.

The two dolphin populations are separated by thousands of kilometers, making independent invention the likely explanation for the shared behavior. Scientists captured the technique on camera for the first time, documenting what researchers describe as a remarkable example of convergent innovation in marine mammals. The behavior represents a form of tool use, adding to the growing body of evidence demonstrating dolphin intelligence and adaptability.

Researchers expressed surprise at witnessing the behavior, with one scientist reporting they were screaming upon seeing the footage. The shell-trapping method requires multiple steps: locating suitable shells, herding fish into them, and then manipulating the shells to extract the meal. This level of coordination suggests advanced problem-solving abilities.

The discovery joins other documented cases of dolphin tool use, such as using marine sponges to protect their rostrums while foraging. However, the shell technique appears to be a more complex, multi-stage process. The fact that geographically isolated groups arrived at the same solution suggests that dolphins can innovate hunting strategies in response to their environments.

The research provides insight into how intelligent species develop and transmit behaviors within their social groups. The independent emergence of identical techniques in separate populations indicates that certain environmental pressures or opportunities may lead different groups to similar solutions. Scientists continue to study how such behaviors spread within dolphin communities and whether they represent cultural traditions passed between generations.
