U.S. Gives Iran Deadline to Renounce Attacks on Ships in Strait of Hormuz

The United States has set a Saturday deadline for Iran to publicly renounce attacks on vessels in the Strait of Hormuz, according to multiple reports. Vice President JD Vance is expected to participate in negotiations resuming in Oman, where American and Iranian officials are working to de-escalate tensions over the strategic waterway.

The U.S. is seeking an Iranian pledge to stop shooting at ships traveling through the strait, a narrow passage that serves as a critical chokepoint for global oil shipments. Roughly one-fifth of the world's petroleum passes through this waterway, making it essential to international energy markets. Any disruption to shipping in the area could trigger widespread economic consequences and increase transportation costs worldwide.

The talks represent a diplomatic effort to resolve the maritime confrontation between the two nations. American officials have been working to secure commitments from Tehran that would guarantee safe passage for both commercial and military vessels through the waterway.

The negotiations come after a period of heightened tensions involving Iranian attacks on ships in the region. A memorandum of understanding signed between Iran and the U.S. last week included an agreement by Tehran to make its best efforts to ensure full freedom of navigation and to impose no fees or tolls for a minimum of 60 days.

However, complications have emerged in coordinating safe passage for vessels. The UN's International Maritime Organization proposed two temporary shipping evacuation lanes in conjunction with Oman. One route would run through mined Iranian waters to the north, while another would pass through more passable Omani waters to the south. The proposal aimed to help evacuate hundreds of ships that have been trapped in the area for months.

The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps rejected the proposed coordinates, calling alternative transit routes "unacceptable and completely dangerous." The statement emphasized that coordination with the IRGC navy would be mandatory for any transit through the strait. Iran has used control of the waterway as a negotiating lever in broader discussions about U.S. sanctions, asset relief, and its nuclear program.

Mohammad Ghalibaf, the speaker of Iran's parliament and chief negotiator, stated that the administration of the strait would not return to conditions prior to February 28. "Everyone should know that the administration of the strait of Hormuz will never go back to the way it was before the war," he said.

Iran and Oman have agreed to establish a working party to discuss the future administration of the strait. Oman is pushing for any arrangement to comply with international maritime law, while the U.S. and other nations have warned against imposing fees or tolls that would violate fundamental principles of ocean navigation.

The outcome of the Saturday negotiations could determine whether the standoff escalates further or moves toward resolution.
