Federal Reserve signals possible rate hike in 2026 under new chairman

The Federal Reserve held interest rates steady at its latest policy meeting while shifting its forward guidance to suggest rate increases could happen before the end of the year. The central bank maintained rates at a range of 3.5% to 3.75%, where they have remained since December, with unanimous support from the voting committee.

This was the first meeting overseen by Kevin Warsh, who became Fed chair in May. The decision prompted a sharp market reaction, with US stock markets dropping on Wednesday afternoon. The Dow closed 500 points lower, while the S&P 500 and Nasdaq each fell over 1.2%.

In its policy statement, the Federal Reserve's open market committee noted that "economic activity is expanding at a solid pace despite elevated uncertainty that owes, in part, to the conflict in the Middle East." The committee acknowledged that "inflation remains elevated relative to the committee's 2% goal, in part reflecting supply shocks that have driven price increases in certain sectors, including energy."

The Fed's latest projections showed a significant shift in outlook. Nine of the voting members projected at least one rate increase this year. Warsh confirmed he was the sole board member who did not contribute to the projections. This marks a dramatic reversal from March, when 12 of 19 officials had projected at least one rate cut by year's end.

Current inflation stands at 4.2%, the highest level since 2023 and well above the Fed's 2% target, driven largely by energy prices following Middle East conflicts. However, core inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy prices, has increased only modestly to 2.9%. The labor market remains relatively strong, with unemployment holding steady at 4.3%.

At his first press conference as chair, Warsh announced plans to overhaul the central bank's operations. He will establish five new taskforces to assess monetary policy broadly, including communications, the Fed's balance sheet, data, productivity and jobs. One taskforce will specifically reassess how the Fed communicates with the public, potentially including changes to news conferences, dot-graph projections, and meeting transcripts.

Warsh emphasized that monetary policy "cannot have a very significant effect on particularly prices" and that oil prices, food costs and other commodity fluctuations do not have first-order consequences for Fed policy. He stated: "The price of oil in the markets or the price of a dozen eggs does not have first-order consequences to what we're doing. But we have an important job there. And it's to make sure that those changes in oil, or beef, or eggs or milk don't broaden in the economy."

The Fed's policy statement was notably shorter than previous statements, reflecting Warsh's stated desire to tighten the central bank's public communications on future guidance.
