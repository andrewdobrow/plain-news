San Andreas and San Jacinto fault lines reach highest stress levels in 1,000 years

Two of Southern California's most dangerous earthquake fault systems have reached their highest levels of tectonic stress in a thousand years, according to new research published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth. Scientists say the region is now in a "critically loaded state" with significantly elevated earthquake risk.

Researchers at the University of Hawaii at Mānoa used computer modeling to analyze stress accumulation across the San Andreas and San Jacinto fault systems. By inputting 1,000 years of earthquake history into their model, they determined that current stress levels match or exceed the highest values recorded over the past millennium.

"Our results show that stress levels on multiple fault segments are now at or above the highest values seen in the past millennium and that the region may be capable of a large through-going rupture involving both fault systems," said Liliane Burkhard, the study's lead author.

A critical aspect of the research focuses on Cajon Pass, the location where the two faults intersect. Scientists describe this area as an "earthquake gate" that could either block large ruptures from traveling between the faults or allow both systems to rupture together in a single massive event. The conditions determining whether this gate opens appear to depend on how closely the stress levels on both fault systems align at the moment of rupture.

"Right now, with stress at historically high levels across the region and more than 160 years elapsed since the last major rupture, the system is in a critically loaded state," Burkhard said. More than a century has passed since the last significant earthquake on the southern San Andreas Fault, allowing stress to accumulate to unprecedented levels.

The research indicates that if both fault systems rupture simultaneously, the impacts could affect large areas across Southern California. However, Burkhard emphasized that the study does not predict when such an earthquake might occur.

"What we can say is that the system is critically stressed, and that physics-based models like this one give us a clearer picture of the range of scenarios we should be prepared for," she said.

The findings reinforce long-standing scientific warnings that Southern California faces significant seismic risk. The last major destructive earthquake to strike California was the 1994 Northridge earthquake in Los Angeles, which destroyed 87,000 homes and businesses and killed more than 60 people.

The research underscores why earthquake preparedness remains crucial for Southern California's millions of residents. Understanding the stress conditions on these fault systems helps scientists and emergency planners better prepare for potential seismic events and their consequences.
