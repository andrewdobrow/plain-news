San Andreas fault reaches highest stress levels in 1,000 years, study finds

Southern California's San Andreas and San Jacinto fault systems have accumulated tectonic stress at levels not seen in the past 1,000 years, according to new research published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth. Scientists describe the region as being in a "critically loaded state" with increased potential for a major earthquake.

Liliane Burkhard, lead author of the study, explained the significance of the findings: "Our results show that stress levels on multiple fault segments are now at or above the highest values seen in the past millennium and that the region may be capable of a large through-going rupture involving both fault systems."

Researchers at the University of Hawaii at Mānoa used computer modeling to track how stress accumulates and releases along the faults. They analyzed 1,000 years of earthquake history to determine current stress levels. More than 160 years have passed since the last major rupture in the region, allowing stress to build to unprecedented levels.

A critical feature of the research focuses on Cajon Pass, located where the two fault systems meet. Scientists found that this area could function as an "earthquake gate," potentially either blocking large ruptures from traveling between the faults or allowing both systems to rupture simultaneously in a single event.

According to Burkhard, the alignment of stress levels between the two fault systems may determine whether this gate opens or remains closed. "Right now, with stress at historically high levels across the region and more than 160 years elapsed since the last major rupture, the system is in a critically loaded state," she said.

If the earthquake gate opens and both faults rupture together, the consequences could be severe across southern California. Such a joint rupture would represent a significantly larger event than a single fault system rupturing alone.

The researchers emphasize that their work does not predict when an earthquake will occur. Instead, the study provides essential information about the range of scenarios the region should prepare for. Burkhard noted: "What we can say is that the system is critically stressed, and that physics-based models like this one give us a clearer picture of the range of scenarios we should be prepared for."

California's last major devastating earthquake struck in 1994 near Northridge in Los Angeles. That event destroyed 87,000 homes and businesses and resulted in more than 60 deaths.

The findings underscore the importance of ongoing earthquake preparedness throughout southern California. Emergency management agencies and officials continue encouraging residents to develop evacuation plans and familiarize themselves with safety protocols in preparation for potential seismic activity.
