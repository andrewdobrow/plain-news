California's San Andreas and San Jacinto faults reach highest stress levels in 1,000 years

Two of California's most dangerous fault systems have accumulated tectonic stress at levels not seen in over a thousand years, according to research published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth. Scientists warn that the San Andreas and San Jacinto faults are now in a "critically loaded state," significantly increasing the probability of a major earthquake in the future.

Researchers at the University of Hawaii at Mānoa used computer modeling to simulate stress accumulation across the fault systems over the past 1,000 years of earthquake history. Their findings show that stress levels on multiple fault segments have reached or exceeded the highest values recorded in the available geological record.

"Our results show that stress levels on multiple fault segments are now at or above the highest values seen in the past millennium and that the region may be capable of a large through-going rupture involving both fault systems," said Liliane Burkhard, the study's lead author.

A particularly significant finding involves Cajon Pass, the junction where the two faults meet. The research suggests this location could function as an "earthquake gate" that either blocks large ruptures from spreading between the faults or allows both systems to rupture simultaneously in a single catastrophic event. The conditions determining whether this gate opens or remains closed appear linked to how closely the stress levels on both fault systems align at the moment of rupture.

More than 160 years have passed since the last major rupture in the region, allowing stress to continue accumulating to unprecedented levels. This extended period without major seismic release has contributed to the current critically stressed condition of the system.

Burkhard emphasized that while the study does not predict when an earthquake will occur, it provides crucial information for disaster preparedness. "What we can say is that the system is critically stressed, and that physics-based models like this one give us a clearer picture of the range of scenarios we should be prepared for," she stated.

The research underscores long-standing concerns about earthquake hazards in California, a state where nearly 40 million people live in close proximity to active fault systems. The last major devastating earthquake to strike California was the 1994 Northridge quake in Los Angeles, which destroyed 87,000 homes and businesses and caused more than 60 deaths.

California officials have long emphasized the importance of earthquake preparedness through building codes, emergency planning, and public readiness measures. The new findings reinforce the critical need for continued investment in these protective strategies to reduce potential casualties and damage when major seismic events occur.
