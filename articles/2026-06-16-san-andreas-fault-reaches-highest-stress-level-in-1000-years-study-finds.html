California fault systems reach highest stress levels in 1,000 years

California's San Andreas and San Jacinto fault systems have accumulated tectonic stress to levels not seen in at least 1,000 years, according to research published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth. Scientists describe the region as existing in a "critically loaded state" with increased potential for a major earthquake.

Researchers from the University of Hawaii at Mānoa used computer models to analyze how stress builds and releases along these fault lines. They incorporated 1,000 years of earthquake history into their simulations to determine current stress levels across multiple fault segments. The findings reveal that stress is now at or above the highest values recorded over the past millennium.

"Our results show that stress levels on multiple fault segments are now at or above the highest values seen in the past millennium and that the region may be capable of a large through-going rupture involving both fault systems," said Liliane Burkhard, the lead author of the study.

One significant finding concerns Cajon Pass, located where the two fault systems meet. The research suggests this area functions as an "earthquake gate" that could either block large ruptures from traveling between faults or allow both systems to rupture simultaneously in a single event. Whether this gate opens or closes depends on how closely the stress levels on both fault systems align at the moment of rupture.

"Right now, with stress at historically high levels across the region and more than 160 years elapsed since the last major rupture, the system is in a critically loaded state," Burkhard explained. The last major earthquake on the San Andreas Fault occurred in 1857, while the San Jacinto Fault experienced its most recent significant event in 1918.

The accumulation of stress over more than a century means the region faces what scientists characterize as a heightened probability for a substantial seismic event. If both faults rupture together, the consequences could be severe across Southern California, given the densely populated areas near these fault systems.

However, the research does not predict when such an earthquake might occur. Instead, it establishes the geological conditions that make major seismic activity possible. Scientists stress that understanding these risk scenarios is essential for preparedness planning.

"What we can say is that the system is critically stressed, and that physics-based models like this one give us a clearer picture of the range of scenarios we should be prepared for," Burkhard said.

Researchers continue monitoring both fault systems using GPS networks, seismometers, and other instruments to track ground movement and identify any early warning signs. The findings underscore the ongoing seismic risks facing millions of residents living near these critical fault lines in Southern California.
