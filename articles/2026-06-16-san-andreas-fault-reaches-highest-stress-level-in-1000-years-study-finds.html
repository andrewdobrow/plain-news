California fault lines reach highest stress levels in 1,000 years, new study finds

California's San Andreas and San Jacinto fault systems have reached their highest levels of tectonic stress in at least 1,000 years, according to a new study published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth. Scientists describe the current conditions as a "critically loaded state" that could lead to a significant earthquake.

Liliane Burkhard, the lead author of the research, said in a statement that the findings show stress levels on multiple fault segments are now at or above the highest values recorded over the past millennium. "The region may be capable of a large through-going rupture involving both fault systems," Burkhard explained.

The research highlights an important geographical feature: Cajon Pass, located where the two faults meet, could act as an "earthquake gate." This natural junction may either block large ruptures from traveling between the faults or potentially allow both systems to rupture simultaneously in a single event.

More than a century has passed since the last major earthquake along these fault systems. During that time, stress has continued to build steadily and has now reached unprecedented levels. "With stress at historically high levels across the region and more than 160 years elapsed since the last major rupture, the system is in a critically loaded state," Burkhard noted.

Scientists at the University of Hawaii at Mānoa developed a computer model to understand how stress accumulates and releases along the fault systems. They input 1,000 years of earthquake history to simulate current stress levels. The research suggests that conditions at Cajon Pass could determine whether the "earthquake gate" opens or remains closed. This outcome appears to depend on how closely the stress levels on the two fault systems align with each other at the time of rupture.

The study does not predict when a major earthquake might occur, but researchers emphasize that understanding these risks is essential for preparation. "What we can say is that the system is critically stressed, and that physics-based models like this one give us a clearer picture of the range of scenarios we should be prepared for," Burkhard said.

California has been bracing for a major seismic event for decades. The most destructive recent earthquake was the 1994 Northridge quake in Los Angeles, which struck just before dawn. That disaster destroyed 87,000 homes and businesses and killed more than 60 people.

The state maintains building codes and emergency preparedness systems designed to reduce earthquake damage. However, researchers stress that despite these precautions, a major rupture along either fault system could still cause significant destruction and casualties depending on its magnitude and location. The findings underscore the importance of continued scientific research into earthquake risks and the need for ongoing preparedness efforts throughout California.
