Iran says nuclear talks to begin Friday as US-Iran ceasefire takes hold

Iran announced that negotiations on nuclear arms and sanctions relief will begin Friday, following the signing of an initial ceasefire agreement between the United States and Iran. The talks will commence immediately after both nations formalize the preliminary deal that has brought a pause to military hostilities.

Iran's foreign minister confirmed the timeline for the diplomatic negotiations, which represent a significant shift from weeks of military conflict between the two nations. The ceasefire agreement has already led to reduced fighting in Lebanon, though details of the arrangement remain disputed between the parties involved.

The terms of the ceasefire have become a point of contention. Iran maintains that the agreement requires Israeli forces to withdraw from Lebanon, a condition the country says is essential to the deal's implementation. US officials have downplayed the written text of the agreement, saying it does not fully capture commitments made through back-channel diplomatic communications. They describe the text as intentionally vague to allow for flexibility in implementation.

Hezbollah and Iran have clashed with the United States over what they characterize as unclear terms in the ceasefire deal. The ambiguity has left unanswered questions about enforcement and compliance, even as a relative quiet has settled over Lebanon. Iran's military has warned Israel of a strong response if it continues strikes in Lebanon despite the ceasefire.

The upcoming negotiations will be tested by deeper questions about Iran's willingness to engage with the United States. Protesters outside the foreign ministry in Tehran have demanded no return to talks with America, reflecting domestic skepticism about the agreement.

Sources with knowledge of previous negotiations point to significant communication breakdowns that contributed to the escalation into armed conflict. Misunderstandings about how Iran's complex nuclear programme functioned, including the purpose of key facilities and the scope of Iran's future nuclear plans, all played a role in the deterioration of talks.

The new supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, has not clarified whether the fatwa banning the use of nuclear weapons set out by his late father remains in place. This uncertainty adds another layer of complexity to the upcoming negotiations, as calls inside Iran to obtain a nuclear weapon have inevitably grown following the conflict.

President Trump said he hopes to put the Iran war in the rearview mirror, signaling a desire to move past the conflict that has dominated recent weeks. The upcoming Friday talks will test whether the ceasefire can hold long enough for both nations to reach a broader agreement on Iran's nuclear program and the lifting of economic sanctions that have strained the Iranian economy for years.

Many Iranians remain focused on practical concerns rather than questions of victory, asking whether the deal will lower prices and reduce the fear of another war.
