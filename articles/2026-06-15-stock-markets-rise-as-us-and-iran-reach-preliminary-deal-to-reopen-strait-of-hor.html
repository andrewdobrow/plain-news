US and Iran Set to Begin Nuclear Talks After Initial Agreement

The United States and Iran have announced an agreement to end their conflict, with President Donald Trump stating that the deal will reopen the Strait of Hormuz and prolong a ceasefire. However, significant details about the agreement remain unclear, and multiple parties are offering conflicting interpretations of what was actually agreed upon.

Iran's foreign minister has stated that the peace deal requires Israeli forces to withdraw from Lebanon. This condition has created confusion among the US, Iran, and Hezbollah, each offering different understandings of the ceasefire terms. The disagreements over specific military movements and territorial control in the region suggest the agreement's implementation faces considerable challenges.

The negotiations leading to this deal were characterized by unusual diplomatic practices. Donald Trump's special envoy Steve Witkoff conducted talks with Iran in Oman with minimal technical expertise on his team. Witkoff brought only Michael Anton, a political philosopher without specialization in Iran's nuclear program, while the Obama administration had sent 10 senior officials from four departments to previous talks in Vienna in 2009. When talks resumed in February, Witkoff arrived with the US military commander in the Middle East in full naval uniform, a breach of protocol that surprised Omani hosts.

The compressed timeline of negotiations raised concerns among those involved. Witkoff allocated just three and a half hours for talks with Iran on February 17, even as he was simultaneously meeting with Ukrainian officials. Because the talks were conducted indirectly at Iran's request, these brief exchanges were frustratingly limited in scope.

Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi has indicated that the truth about what happened during the final day of negotiations on February 26 would soon become known. He has been asked to publish Iran's seven-page written offer for a deal that was shown to Witkoff, despite calls from within Iran for transparency on this matter. According to Jonathan Powell, the UK national security adviser present at the talks, Iran's offer was worth pursuing.

Witkoff later claimed that Iran's refusal to allow him to keep a copy of the written offer suggested they were not genuinely interested in a deal. In response, Iranian negotiators have stated they believed the talks were a subterfuge designed to give the US time to assemble its military forces.

The conflict between the two nations began on February 28 when military operations disrupted energy production and transportation across the Middle East, causing fuel costs to jump. Oil prices have now fallen following the announcement of the agreement.

Whether this diplomatic opening can produce a lasting framework for regional stability remains uncertain. The fragile truce has left several issues unresolved, and disagreements over preliminary terms have already emerged among the parties involved.
