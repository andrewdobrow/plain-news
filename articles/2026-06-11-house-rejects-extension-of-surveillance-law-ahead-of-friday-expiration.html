House rejects extension of surveillance law ahead of Friday expiration

The House of Representatives failed to pass a short-term extension of Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act on Thursday, clearing the way for the powerful surveillance tool to expire for the first time since its enactment in 2008.

The measure fell short in a 198-218 vote after Democrats announced they would block the renewal in protest of President Trump's appointment of Bill Pulte as acting director of national intelligence. Republicans attempted to pass the extension using a fast-track process that required a two-thirds majority, but they fell well short, with 19 members of their own party joining most Democrats in opposition.

Section 702, enacted in the wake of 9/11, allows U.S. intelligence agencies to intercept foreign communications without obtaining individual court warrants. Lawmakers in both parties have long considered it one of the government's most important intelligence tools, yet the political crisis surrounding Pulte's appointment derailed efforts to extend the law.

Democrats cited Pulte's lack of qualifications for the position. "Bill Pulte has no relevant national security experience," said a statement from top House Democrats including Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries and the ranking members on the intelligence and judiciary committees. "Consequently, his appointment is in defiance of the law that requires the Director of National Intelligence to have 'extensive' national security experience."

The Democrats also expressed concern about Pulte's willingness to use government databases to search for damaging information on Trump's political opponents. "There is a path to reauthorizing Fisa, but it will require enacting meaningful reforms," the statement said.

Republican leaders in both chambers had been negotiating a compromise to extend Section 702 for three years, involving both Democrats and civil liberties-minded Republicans. Those negotiations collapsed after Trump's announcement of Pulte's appointment.

A similar effort failed in the Senate last week when all Democrats except John Fetterman of Pennsylvania opposed the extension, alongside seven conservative Republicans who had raised concerns about the law's civil liberties implications.

However, the expiration of the statute does not mean the surveillance program itself will cease operations. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court issued a year-long certification authorizing Section 702 collection through approximately March 2027. The law contains a provision allowing collection to continue under that court order even if the underlying statute lapses.

The congressional deadlock ensures Section 702 will lapse on Friday unless Congress takes unexpected action. Republican chairs of the Senate intelligence and judiciary committees have asked Secretary of State Marco Rubio to prepare the administration for a "potential significant gap in foreign intelligence collection."
