Pakistan Says U.S.-Iran Peace Deal Could Be Finalized Within a Day

Peace negotiations between the United States and Iran began in Islamabad on Friday, with senior officials from both nations meeting face-to-face for the first time since 1979. Pakistan's role as mediator has positioned the country as a critical bridge between Washington and Tehran during a period of intense conflict.

The talks brought together high-level delegations from each side. Vice President JD Vance is leading the American team, which also includes Steve Witkoff, a real estate developer serving as Trump's personal envoy, and Jared Kushner, the president's son-in-law. Iran's negotiating team is headed by Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, the parliamentary speaker, and Abbas Araghchi, the foreign minister. Pakistan's Army Chief Asim Munir, who brokered an earlier ceasefire, was present during discussions.

Pakistani state television confirmed that American and Iranian officials were "sitting directly at the same table," a significant development given their usual practice of remaining in separate locations during previous negotiations. The first round of talks lasted approximately two hours, followed by technical discussions focusing on security issues, financial matters, and control of the Strait of Hormuz. Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif hosted a dinner for the delegations at his residence.

A senior Pakistani official expressed optimism about the negotiations, stating that both delegations "have come to Islamabad with complete authority from their capitals and have stepped back from extreme positions." The official added that the talks were "in a strong position" and that "the entire world is looking for a breakthrough and an end of the war."

On arrival in Islamabad, Vance warned against any Iranian attempts at manipulation, saying the negotiating team would not be receptive if Iran tried to play games with the United States. Iranian media reported that the U.S. had agreed to unblock six billion dollars of Iranian assets frozen in Qatar, though American officials promptly denied this claim.

The preliminary discussions revealed Iranian demands for a ceasefire in Lebanon, reparations, and access to unfrozen assets as part of any peace agreement. However, the path forward remains uncertain, with significant complications continuing on the ground. Israeli forces have continued striking targets in Lebanon and southern areas, while Iranian state television threatened that any American warship crossing the Strait of Hormuz would be attacked within thirty minutes.

Additional negotiations are planned between Lebanese and Israeli ambassadors in Washington, where they will discuss ceasefire terms and the start date for direct talks between their countries. The complex nature of these discussions suggests that while optimism exists among negotiators, implementation and verification of any agreement would present substantial challenges requiring sustained commitment from all parties involved.
