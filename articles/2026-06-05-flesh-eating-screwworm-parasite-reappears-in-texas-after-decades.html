Flesh-eating cattle parasite spreads beyond Texas as USDA confirms additional cases

The New World screwworm, a flesh-eating parasite that attacks livestock, has spread beyond its initial detection point in Texas, with the U.S. Department of Agriculture confirming multiple new infections across the state and into neighboring New Mexico.

The screwworm is a parasitic fly whose larvae feed on the living tissue of warm-blooded animals, including cattle, horses, and goats. When eggs hatch, the larvae burrow through living flesh, causing a painful condition called screwworm myiasis that can kill the host if left untreated. The pest had been eradicated from the United States decades ago, but recent detections mark the first cases found in the country since the 1960s.

The initial infection was confirmed in a three-week-old calf with an umbilical lesion in Zavala County, southwest of San Antonio. The Texas Animal Health Commission then confirmed a second case, followed by the USDA's confirmation of three additional cases on Monday: two more in Texas affecting a calf in La Salle County and a goat in Gillespie County, plus one case in New Mexico.

In response to the outbreak, Texas Governor Greg Abbott issued a statewide disaster declaration and activated the state's emergency operations center. Officials are dispersing millions of sterile screwworm flies in the vicinity of where the initial infections were found to prevent wild female flies from reproducing. According to the Texas Animal Health Commission, this combination of sterile fly release, enhanced surveillance, movement restrictions, prompt wound treatment, and public education represents "a proven and effective tool for controlling and eradicating this pest."

An infested zone has been established in the affected area, and animal movement restrictions are in effect to prevent further spread. The Texas Animal Health Commission director, Dr. Bud Dinges, stated that the agency had been preparing for this resurgence for over two years and encouraged all animal owners to remain vigilant for larvae in wounds.

Governor Abbott warned that the upcoming season poses significant challenges, calling it likely to be "an extraordinarily challenging summer" for infected areas. The parasite's northward progression from Central America began in 2023 and represents a major threat to the cattle industry. The U.S.-Mexico border has been closed to live cattle imports for over a year due to the disease's advance, causing the $100 billion beef industry to contract to a 75-year low.

Canada's food inspection agency announced a temporary ban on livestock from Texas following the confirmed infections. Animals that were in Texas within 21 days before crossing the border into Canada will not be accepted. While officials noted that the colder Canadian climate makes widespread infection unlikely, they warned farmers to monitor livestock for wounds with discharge or foul odor.

The USDA confirmed that the food supply remains safe, noting that screwworms do not infest meat, fruits, vegetables, or other food sources.
