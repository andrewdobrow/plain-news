Flesh-eating cattle parasite spreads beyond Texas as new screwworm cases found

A flesh-eating parasite that infests livestock has spread beyond its initial Texas detection site, with authorities now confirming cases in both Texas and New Mexico. The New World screwworm fly, which had been eradicated from the United States decades ago, has reemerged after more than 50 years and now threatens the nation's cattle industry.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture confirmed three additional screwworm infections on Monday, including two more cases in Texas and one in New Mexico. The Texas cases involved a calf in La Salle county and a goat in Gillespie county. The initial cases were discovered in Zavala county, located southwest of San Antonio, where two infections were found approximately five miles apart.

The screwworm fly lays eggs in open wounds on animals, and the hatching larvae then feed on living tissue, causing a painful condition called screwworm myiasis. If left untreated, the infection can kill the host animal. The parasite affects cattle, livestock, and wildlife, though federal officials have confirmed that the U.S. food supply remains safe, as screwworms do not infest meat, fruits, vegetables or other food sources.

Texas Governor Greg Abbott issued a statewide disaster declaration on Friday, warning that it is likely to be "an extraordinarily challenging summer" for infected areas. Abbott said state officials were working with the federal government to slow the spread of the fly.

Authorities have launched an aggressive containment response. Officials are dispersing millions of sterile screwworm flies in the vicinity of the initial infections. These sterile male flies prevent wild female flies from reproducing, a method that has proven effective for controlling and eradicating the pest when combined with enhanced surveillance, movement restrictions, and prompt treatment of wounds.

The Texas Animal Health Commission noted that it had been actively preparing for a potential resurgence of the parasite for over two years. The director urged all animal owners and caretakers to remain vigilant for the presence of larvae in animal wounds and to immediately report any suspicions.

Animal movement restrictions are now in effect to prevent further spread of the pest. The USDA discovered the second infestation after testing a number of suspect cases, according to the agency's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service.

The parasite's arrival poses a significant economic threat to U.S. cattle ranchers. A widespread resurgence could lead to animal deaths as well as higher labor and treatment costs. The situation has already impacted border trade. The U.S. had closed its southern border to live cattle imports from Mexico for over a year as the disease moved northward, contributing to a contraction in the beef industry to a 75-year low. Additionally, Canada announced a temporary ban on livestock from Texas following the confirmed infections.
