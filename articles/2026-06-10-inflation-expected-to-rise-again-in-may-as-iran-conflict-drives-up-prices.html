Bank of Japan Raises Interest Rates to 1%, Highest Level Since 1995

The Bank of Japan raised its benchmark interest rate to 1% on Tuesday, the highest level in 31 years, defying pressure from Prime Minister Takaichi and marking a significant shift in the country's monetary policy. The decision comes amid mounting concerns over inflation driven by energy disruptions and a weakening yen, as well as pressure from the United States.

The rate increase represents the central bank's continued move away from the near-zero interest rate policy it has maintained since the mid-1990s. The Bank of Japan has been gradually raising rates since 2024, but this latest hike signals a more aggressive stance in response to mounting economic pressures. The bank also announced plans to stop reducing its bond purchases, a key component of its previous stimulus efforts.

The decision to raise rates came despite opposition from Prime Minister Takaichi, highlighting tensions between the government and the central bank over monetary policy direction. The move was influenced by multiple factors, including pressure from U.S. officials, concerns about the yen's sharp decline against other major currencies, and rising inflation stemming from global energy market disruptions.

Japan's currency has been under significant pressure in recent months, making imports more expensive and contributing to domestic inflation. Energy costs have surged amid global supply disruptions, adding to the inflationary pressures facing Japanese households and businesses. The central bank appears to have concluded that maintaining ultra-low rates was no longer tenable given these economic conditions.

The rate hike marks a turning point for Japan's economy, which has relied on accommodative monetary policy for decades to combat deflation and stimulate growth. The move to 1% represents the highest borrowing costs Japanese businesses and consumers have faced in more than three decades, potentially affecting everything from corporate investment decisions to household spending patterns. Financial markets will be closely watching how the economy responds to this shift in monetary policy direction.
