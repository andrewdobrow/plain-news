British artist David Hockney dies at 88

David Hockney, the iconic British painter whose revolutionary work shaped contemporary art across seven decades, has died peacefully at home aged 88. His death on June 11, 2026, marks the loss of one of the most important figures in modern and contemporary art.

Hockney rose to prominence during the 1960s as a leading pop artist, becoming best known for his paintings of California swimming pools that helped define the Los Angeles aesthetic. Works such as "A Bigger Splash" and "Portrait of an Artist (Pool With Two Figures)" depicted scenes of love, lust and loss unfolding beneath sun-soaked skies. These paintings brought him international recognition and made him one of the most recognizable artists of his generation.

Born in Bradford, West Yorkshire, in 1937, Hockney came from what he described as a "radical working-class family" whose parents nurtured his artistic talents. He studied art at Bradford College before enrolling at London's Royal College of Art in 1959, where he quickly developed a reputation as a uniquely talented but rebellious figure. His refusal to conform to traditional requirements nearly prevented his graduation, but the college recognized his exceptional abilities and awarded him his diploma.

Hockney's willingness to challenge conventions extended throughout his career. His early works openly depicted gay life and relationships with honesty and openness at a time when homosexuality remained illegal in Britain. With his distinctive bleach-blonde hair and thick-rimmed spectacles, he became a prominent figure in the vibrant social scenes of 1960s London and America.

After relocating to Los Angeles in the mid-1960s, Hockney's work evolved toward a more mature and restrained style while maintaining its emotional depth. His paintings gained acclaim for their ability to convey complex feelings about love and loss. In November 2018, his masterpiece "Portrait of an Artist (Pool With Two Figures)" sold for 90.3 million dollars at auction, setting a world record for a living artist at that time.

Throughout his career, Hockney continuously reinvented himself. He created stage designs for opera, experimented with photo collages, and in later years explored digital media and iPad painting to create large-scale landscapes. Even after suffering a stroke in 2012, he remained remarkably productive.

The artist maintained studios in London, Los Angeles, and Normandy, France. He received the Order of Merit from Queen Elizabeth II in 2012, one of Britain's highest civilian awards. His 2012 Royal Academy exhibition attracted over 600,000 visitors, making it one of the institution's most attended shows.

UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer said Hockney's "vivid, instantly recognisable work influenced generations of artists." The Tate Gallery described him as an "endlessly inventive artist" whose loss represents an immense blow to the art world. The institution plans major retrospective exhibitions next year, ensuring his extraordinary legacy continues to inspire viewers worldwide.
