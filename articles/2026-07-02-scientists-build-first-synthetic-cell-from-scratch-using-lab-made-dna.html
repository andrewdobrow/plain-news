Scientists build first synthetic cell with complete life cycle

Researchers have created what they describe as the first fully synthetic cell capable of completing an entire life cycle, including growth and division. The achievement, which scientists have named SpudCell, represents a significant advance in synthetic biology and biological engineering.

The synthetic cells were constructed from chemical compounds rather than material extracted from existing living organisms. Built from the ground up in laboratory conditions, they are believed to be the first to demonstrate the complete cell cycle of growth, genetic replication and splitting to produce the next generation.

Dr Kate Adamala, who led the research at the University of Minnesota, explained the significance of the work. "It is not as robust, as fast, or as good at most of its functions as a natural cell, but it is proof of principle that molecules can reconstitute behaviours that up until now we only associated with natural living cells," she said. "If we want to be able to engineer biology, we really have to understand exactly the blueprint, every component of it, so we know what we're changing."

Adamala's team built SpudCells starting with tiny water-filled spheres called liposomes, which measure a few thousandths of a millimetre wide. They added synthetic DNA to provide basic cellular functions. The name evokes both Sputnik and the space age, though Adamala, who is Polish, also chose it as a personal reference to potatoes.

SpudCells function only within a liquid rich in vital chemicals such as ATP, the main energy-carrying molecule that living cells normally manufacture from nutrients. To grow, SpudCells fuse with smaller "feeder" liposomes that contain molecules, enzymes and ribosomes needed for protein production. The synthetic genome carries instructions for copying itself and dividing.

However, SpudCells have significant limitations. They cannot build their own protein-making machinery, control their metabolism or clear their waste. They rely completely on the substances and components in the surrounding liquid. Additionally, they often pass on incorrect amounts of DNA when dividing and typically cease functioning after a few generations.

Prof Tom Ellis from Imperial College London called the work probably the field's "biggest breakthrough in recent times." The achievement provides a fully understood system for testing biological circuits and computer models of cellular life while helping scientists understand the minimum requirements for life.

The development raises the prospect of artificial organisms designed and built to produce drugs, foods, fuels and other materials. Adamala and others are launching an institution called Biotic to advance SpudCells into something more capable. The goal, according to co-founder Prof Drew Endy from Stanford University, is to build "an operating system for life" made from genes and biochemistry.

The study was released as a preprint before peer review, allowing other laboratories to examine the work without delay.
