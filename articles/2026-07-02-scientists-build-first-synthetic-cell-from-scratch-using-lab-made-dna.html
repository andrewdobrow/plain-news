Scientists build first fully synthetic cell capable of complete life cycle

Researchers have created the first synthetic cell built entirely from laboratory components that can feed, grow, and reproduce, marking a significant milestone in bioengineering. The achievement represents a fundamental shift in how scientists approach building cellular life.

The synthetic cells, nicknamed SpudCells, differ fundamentally from previous efforts that modified existing organisms. Earlier projects, including efforts to create minimal bacterial genomes, still relied on inserting synthetic DNA into existing cellular machinery. This new work was built from the ground up to ensure every component was known and understood.

Dr. Kate Adamala, who led the research at the University of Minnesota, explained the rationale: "If we want to be able to engineer biology, we really have to understand exactly the blueprint, every component of it, so we know what we're changing."

SpudCells are tiny, water-filled spheres called liposomes, just a few thousandths of a millimetre wide, containing synthetic DNA and other essential components. The cells demonstrate the complete cycle of growth, genetic replication, and division to produce offspring. Adamala named them SpudCells to evoke Sputnik and the space age, but also acknowledged a personal connection: "I'm Polish. I'm mostly made of potatoes."

The synthetic cells function in a specially prepared liquid brimming with vital chemicals, including ATP, the main energy-carrying molecule that natural cells manufacture from nutrients. To grow, SpudCells fuse with tiny "feeder" liposomes containing molecules, enzymes, and ribosomes needed to make proteins. The SpudCell genome carries instructions to copy itself and divide. Researchers demonstrated how SpudCells with genetic advantages spread through populations, outcompeting original variants.

However, SpudCells remain far from being true living organisms. They are completely dependent on the substances in their surrounding liquid and cannot build their own protein-making machinery, control their metabolism, or clear waste. When they divide, they often pass on incorrect amounts of DNA and typically fail after a few generations.

Professor Tom Ellis from Imperial College London called the work probably the field's "biggest breakthrough in recent times." The achievement helps scientists understand the minimum requirements for life and how it might have emerged from chemistry, while also providing a fully understood system for testing biological models.

Adamala and others are now launching an institution called Biotic to develop SpudCells further. The goal is to build "an operating system for life" made from genes and biochemistry. While the cells could eventually be designed to produce drugs, foods, fuels, and other materials, significant work remains before they become practical for real-world applications.

The research was released as a preprint, allowing other laboratories to scrutinize the work before formal peer review.
