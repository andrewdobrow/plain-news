US-Brokered Lebanon Cease-Fire Stalls as Hezbollah Rejects Terms

A United States-led cease-fire agreement between Israel and Lebanon has failed to stop fighting, with Hezbollah continuing attacks despite the deal reached on April 17. The Iranian-backed group has rejected the terms, stating they do not consider themselves bound by negotiations conducted without their participation.

Since the cease-fire began, Hezbollah has launched drone attacks against Israeli soldiers in south Lebanon on a nearly daily basis. On Tuesday, the group conducted several drone strikes using fiber-optic-guided aircraft that have evaded Israeli air defenses. An Israeli soldier was killed and six others wounded in a Hezbollah drone attack on Sunday. The armed group claims its Tuesday attacks injured additional Israeli soldiers, though the Israeli military has not confirmed casualties beyond reporting that it fired interceptor missiles.

Israel has responded with continued airstrikes and new displacement orders. On Tuesday alone, Israeli forces ordered residents of 16 villages in south Lebanon to evacuate northward. Over the weekend, Israeli airstrikes killed 18 people and wounded 88 more in Lebanon, according to the Lebanese health ministry. Since fighting began on March 2, at least 2,534 people have been killed and 7,863 wounded by Israeli airstrikes in Lebanon.

The cease-fire agreement permits Israel to conduct strikes in self-defense, language identical to a previous 2024 cease-fire during which Israel struck Lebanon more than 15,000 times. Israel has established a "yellow line" in south Lebanon comprising at least 55 villages, where Israeli troops remain active and have continued demolishing homes.

Hezbollah leadership has explicitly rejected the agreement. Naim Qassem, the group's head, called direct negotiations between the Lebanese government and Israel a "grave sin" that would plunge the country into instability. He stated that the group will not disarm, a key Israeli demand. The Lebanese government responded that "real betrayal" comes from those who drag their country into war for foreign interests.

The collapse of cease-fire enforcement reflects the limited authority of the Lebanese government over Hezbollah. While Lebanese officials negotiate in Washington, they cannot control the armed group's military operations. This dynamic has prompted US Secretary of State Marco Rubio to suggest the United States could help Lebanon develop specialized army units to confront Hezbollah directly, a prospect that has alarmed Lebanese officials concerned about sparking civil conflict.

Israeli officials continue applying pressure, with Defense Minister Israel Katz accusing the Lebanese government of taking cover under Hezbollah rather than acting forcefully against the group. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu stated that Hezbollah retains about 10 percent of its original missiles and promised continued strikes on what he termed Hezbollah infrastructure both inside and north of the security zone.

The United States asked Israel to ensure any response to Hezbollah attacks remained "calculated and limited."
