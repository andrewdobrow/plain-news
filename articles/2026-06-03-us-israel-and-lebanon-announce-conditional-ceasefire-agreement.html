Israel and Lebanon Renew Cease-Fire in Effort to End Hostilities

Israel and Lebanon have agreed to implement a new cease-fire aimed at ending hostilities between the countries, with the Trump administration announcing the deal after negotiations in Washington. The agreement requires the Iran-backed Hezbollah armed group to stop all attacks against Israel and withdraw its forces south of the Litani River in southern Lebanon.

The renewed cease-fire represents an attempt to address one of the largest obstacles to reaching a broader agreement to end the war with Iran. However, significant challenges to the agreement's success have already emerged. Despite the deal being announced, Israeli warplanes launched dozens of strikes across southern Lebanon on the day after the agreement was reached. Near the city of Sidon, rescuers recovered the bodies of six family members, including two children and a woman, following an Israeli strike. The Israeli military also issued new evacuation warnings for the southern city of Nabatiyeh before conducting additional strikes.

The Israeli military accused Hezbollah of violating the cease-fire, saying the group fired two projectiles overnight toward the northern Israeli city of Safed. Hezbollah has not claimed responsibility for recent strikes into Israel, instead stating it attacked Israeli troops who have advanced into Lebanon to establish a security zone between five and ten kilometers wide.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu cited what he called Hezbollah's "repeated violations" of a cease-fire officially in place since April 17 when he ordered strikes on Beirut's southern suburbs, a densely populated Hezbollah stronghold. Trump reportedly intervened in the situation, with some accounts suggesting he called Netanyahu over the strikes on Beirut.

Mahmoud Qmati, a senior Hezbollah official, told reporters that the group "will not accept a partial ceasefire" and warned that "any aggression against the suburbs of Beirut could lead to a deeper and stronger response" from the organization.

Hezbollah operates as both a political party in Lebanon and a military organization with significant armed capabilities. The group receives backing from Iran and maintains considerable influence in Lebanese politics and security matters. Because Hezbollah was not a party to the cease-fire negotiations, uncertainty remains about whether the organization will honor the agreement's conditions.

The recent escalation marked the most serious intensification since the supposed cease-fire took effect. Over the weekend, Israeli troops raised their flag over Beaufort Castle, marking their deepest incursion into southern Lebanon since the end of the 1982 to 2000 occupation. Lebanese and Israeli delegations have begun a new round of talks in Washington, marking the fourth round of negotiations between the two countries, which have no diplomatic relations.

The renewed cease-fire reflects broader tensions in the region involving Iran, Israel, and proxy forces. Both Israel and Lebanon have expressed an interest in preventing further escalation, but the arrangement's durability will depend on compliance from armed groups operating with varying degrees of independence from state control.
