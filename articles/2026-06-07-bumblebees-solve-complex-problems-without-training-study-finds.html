Bumblebees solve complex problems without training, study finds

Bumblebees can solve problems spontaneously despite their tiny brains, according to new research that demonstrates their remarkably advanced cognitive abilities. The study shows the insects performing a task previously documented only in large-brained animals like chimpanzees, elephants, and crows.

Researchers gave bumblebees an adapted version of a classic experiment from a century ago that first showed chimpanzees could retrieve an out-of-reach banana by stacking boxes. In the latest version, bees were tested on their ability to roll a polystyrene ball to a specific location and climb on top of it to access an artificial flower positioned on a low ceiling. The bees had never encountered this type of challenge before and received no training to perform it.

In the basic test, 75 percent of the bees successfully reached the flower. "This is essentially an insect version of the classic 'box-and-banana' problem," said Dr Olli Loukola, a behavioural ecologist at the University of Oulu, Finland, and senior author of the research. "What stands out about the result is that this kind of spontaneous problem solving is now demonstrated in an insect."

The findings challenge the long-held assumption that insects operate purely on instinct and mindless trial-and-error learning. To verify that the bees were genuinely solving the problem rather than simply enjoying rolling objects, scientists created increasingly complex versions of the challenge. In the final setup, bees explored two chambers before the test began, one containing the artificial flower. When scientists illuminated the chamber with red light to hide the blue flower from view and then introduced the ball, 23 out of 30 bees successfully positioned it beneath the flower's location based on memory alone.

"We are not claiming that bees think like humans," Loukola said. "But our findings show that miniature brains can generate flexible solutions to novel problems in ways we are only beginning to understand."

The research contributes to growing evidence that insect intelligence has been significantly underestimated. Previous studies have shown bees can count, recognize faces, and communicate through dance, but spontaneous tool use represents a notable leap in cognitive flexibility.

Prof Lars Chittka, a behavioural ecologist at Queen Mary University of London who was not involved in the research, emphasized the importance of these findings. "There's a general perception that intelligent behaviour requires big brains because we are big-brained and relatively intelligent among animals," Chittka said. "Bees are a model of how much intelligence you can squeeze into a small nervous system."

The study, published in the journal Science, raises fundamental questions about how intelligence evolved across different species and what minimal neural systems can achieve.
