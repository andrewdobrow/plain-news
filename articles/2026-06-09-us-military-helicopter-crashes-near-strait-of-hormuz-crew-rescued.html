U.S. and Iran sign agreement to end war and reopen Strait of Hormuz

The United States and Iran have signed a memorandum of understanding to end their conflict and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, with the agreement taking effect immediately. President Trump signed the deal remotely, and the signing was confirmed by two U.S. officials and Trump himself. The signing happened ahead of a scheduled ceremony in Switzerland on Friday, as both sides moved to implement the agreement sooner than originally planned.

The accord represents the conclusion of two months of negotiations focused on halting hostilities, reopening the critical shipping lane, and stabilizing global energy markets. Trump presented his case for the deal during a press conference, though observers noted he appeared to lower his previous expectations for success. He warned that he could authorize military strikes against Iran again if nuclear negotiations fail.

The agreement has drawn strong criticism from Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who characterizes it as a strategic and political disaster. Netanyahu had promised Israelis total victory against Iran but instead faces Trump's memorandum of understanding along with frequent presidential criticism, all occurring four months before an Israeli election. Netanyahu remains isolated internationally in his opposition to the deal, as even traditionally hawkish Gulf states like the United Arab Emirates have joined the regional consensus supporting the agreement.

Reopening the Strait of Hormuz presents immediate practical obstacles. An estimated 5,000 mines were placed in the waterway during the conflict, and clearing these will delay the restoration of normal shipping traffic. Trump acknowledged that U.S. military personnel were already conducting mine-hunting operations, but extensive sweeping will be necessary before commerce can resume fully.

The 14-paragraph memorandum includes provisions for ending fighting and a commitment that Iran will never develop nuclear weapons. The agreement also includes a 300 billion dollar redevelopment package for Iran.

Some critics argue that the deal demonstrates that the war itself was fundamentally a costly mistake. By making concessions simply to return to the status quo, these observers contend, the conflict failed to achieve its objectives. The war disrupted global energy markets, causing fuel price spikes and economic strain across multiple continents. Germany halved its 2026 growth forecast to 0.5 percent, while Greece announced 500 million euros in emergency aid for households and farmers facing higher fuel and food costs.

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz, through which about 20 percent of the world's oil and liquefied gas had passed during peacetime, created a global humanitarian crisis. Approximately 20,000 seafarers and 2,000 ships became stranded in the Gulf during the blockade.

Trump has defended the economic benefits of the ceasefire while maintaining that military force remains available if Iran fails to cooperate in upcoming nuclear negotiations.
