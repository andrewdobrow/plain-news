US and Iran sign agreement to end hostilities and reopen Strait of Hormuz

The United States and Iran have signed a 14-point memorandum of understanding that took effect immediately after both countries finalized the agreement. President Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian signed the document on Wednesday, according to Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif, who served as the lead mediator in the negotiations.

The agreement marks a significant development in US-Iran relations, bringing an end to armed conflict and reopening the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway for global energy supplies. Roughly one-fifth of the world's oil and liquefied fossil gas passes through the narrow shipping channel during peacetime. The deal follows months of military engagement and tension between the two countries, with Pakistan playing a central role in facilitating the talks.

The 14-paragraph memo includes provisions ending the fighting and addresses Iran's nuclear program. The agreement also includes a $300 billion redevelopment package for Iran. President Trump has emphasized that the deal ensures Iran will never buy, develop, or produce a nuclear weapon.

Trump publicly defended the agreement, framing it as necessary to stabilize the region and restore normal shipping routes through the strait. The closure of the waterway had created serious economic consequences worldwide, with fuel price increases and supply disruptions affecting countries dependent on Gulf oil imports. Germany halved its 2026 growth forecast, while Greece announced additional economic aid for households and farmers struggling with increased energy costs.

The humanitarian impact of the conflict has been substantial. According to the International Maritime Organization, approximately 20,000 seafarers and 2,000 ships have been stranded in the Gulf since the strait's closure. The UN maritime agency has appealed for assistance for the stranded workers.

Despite the agreement, significant challenges remain unresolved. The text of the agreement regarding Iran's nuclear program falls short of Trump's public claims about the deal's guarantees. Critics argue that the most difficult aspects of US-Iran relations remain unaddressed, and the longer-term issues will require future negotiations to resolve. Some observers characterize the agreement as a return to conditions existing before the conflict began, rather than a comprehensive resolution of the underlying disputes.

Both sides have committed to the agreement's terms by signing the document, signaling their intention to move forward with implementation. The immediate effect means that provisions regarding the cessation of hostilities and the reopening of the strait are now in force. Pakistan's Prime Minister Sharif stated that the signing demonstrated genuine commitment from both Washington and Tehran to follow through on the outlined steps, though he acknowledged that talks scheduled in Islamabad did not proceed as originally planned.
