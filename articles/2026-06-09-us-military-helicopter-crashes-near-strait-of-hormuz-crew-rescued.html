U.S. and Iran sign agreement to end war and reopen Strait of Hormuz

The United States and Iran have signed a memorandum of understanding to end their three-month war and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, according to U.S. officials. President Trump signed the agreement remotely, and it is now in effect. The signing occurred ahead of a scheduled ceremony planned for Friday in Switzerland.

The agreement concludes more than three months of conflict that began when the U.S. and Israel launched military operations against Iran. The war disrupted global shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, a critical waterway for international oil transport. About 20 percent of the world's oil and liquefied fossil gas passed through the strait during peacetime, making the closure devastating to global energy markets and supply chains.

According to a diplomat from a mediating country, discussions had been underway to move up the signing date. Both parties wanted to open the strait sooner than Friday. Political pressure on the White House to release the text of the memorandum may have also accelerated the timeline.

The conflict has created severe economic consequences worldwide. Germany halved its 2026 growth forecast to 0.5 percent, while Greece announced additional aid for households and farmers struggling with fuel costs. Countries in Asia dependent on Gulf oil have faced particularly acute shortages of fuel, fertilizer and raw materials.

The disruption to shipping has stranded approximately 20,000 seafarers and 2,000 ships in the Gulf, according to the International Maritime Organization. The head of the U.N. maritime agency has appealed for assistance for these seafarers.

The deal has received mixed reactions in Washington. Top House Democrats, including the ranking members of committees overseeing foreign affairs, defense, and intelligence policy, have demanded that Secretary of State Marco Rubio provide an immediate briefing. However, they welcomed the administration's shift toward diplomacy and its decision to end what they called a war of choice.

Robert Malley, who served as President Biden's envoy on Iran and negotiated the 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action, expressed support for the Trump administration's deal. He stated the memorandum is "far preferable to any of the alternatives" and that he does not see much value in other available options.

The agreement follows months of stalled peace negotiations. Pakistan had acted as mediator, preparing a luxury hotel in Islamabad for talks, but they never materialized. Iran had initially suggested that a wide gap remained between the sides regarding key terms.

Trump has insisted the deal ensures that Iran will never buy, develop or produce a nuclear weapon. However, the text of the agreement reportedly falls short of that comprehensive guarantee, leaving many key issues to be negotiated in future discussions.
