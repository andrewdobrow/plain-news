U.S. and Iran to sign agreement ending war, reopening Strait of Hormuz

The United States and Iran are preparing to electronically sign a memorandum of understanding on Sunday that will extend their ceasefire by 60 days, reopen the Strait of Hormuz, and begin discussions about Iran's nuclear program. The virtual signing will include Pakistani and Qatari mediators who helped negotiate the deal.

The agreement comes after nearly three months of negotiations between the U.S. and Iranian governments, with assistance from Pakistan, Qatar, Egypt, and Turkey. The deal aims to end armed conflict in the region and provide stability after months of escalating tensions.

President Trump announced the signing plans on Truth Social, stating that "the deal is scheduled to get signed tomorrow." He highlighted that the U.S. relationship with Iran under his administration differs from previous approaches, though he did not elaborate on specific terms beyond the framework outlined by mediators.

The reopening of the Strait of Hormuz carries major economic consequences worldwide. This waterway handles between 10 and 25 percent of global oil and gas supplies. Its closure had disrupted energy markets and increased fuel prices internationally. Interior Secretary Doug Burgum addressed gasoline prices on Friday, attributing variations across states primarily to state policies and taxes rather than the Strait situation.

The 60-day ceasefire extension provides a negotiating window for Iran's nuclear program, which has long been a source of tension between Washington and Tehran. The virtual format reflects the sensitivity of the agreement and the ongoing diplomatic challenges between the two nations.

More than 40 countries have coordinated efforts to pressure Iran on the strait issue. UK Foreign Secretary Yvette Cooper said after chairing a virtual summit that coordinated action was essential. She emphasized that Iran's actions were affecting nations worldwide, influencing petrol prices, mortgage rates, jet fuel availability, fertilizer shipments to Africa, and gas supplies to Asia.

Britain's Permanent Joint Headquarters will convene a military discussion next week to explore options for clearing sea mines and assisting trapped ships. The UN is also examining whether a humanitarian shipping corridor can be established to ensure fertilizer reaches poorer nations and prevent food shortages.

World Bank predictions suggest continued blockage could push 9 million people into food insecurity while driving unsustainable increases in oil and food prices affecting households and businesses globally.

Not all political figures have supported Trump's approach. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch stated that if Trump started the conflict, he should not abandon resolving it, citing the principle that "if you break it, you own it." Reform UK leader Nigel Farage expressed difficulty understanding the president's motivations at times, while Liberal Democrat leader Ed Davey urged Prime Minister Starmer to strengthen efforts to reopen the shipping route.
