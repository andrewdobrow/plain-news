Oil Prices Drop as U.S.-Iran Agreement to Reopen Strait of Hormuz Takes Effect

Oil prices fell sharply after President Trump confirmed a U.S.-Iran peace deal that would reopen the Strait of Hormuz to tanker traffic. The agreement marks a turning point following more than 100 days of disruption to global energy supplies, with Brent crude tumbling to $82 a barrel and wholesale gas prices dropping around 6 percent.

The strait, which handles roughly 20 percent of the world's oil and liquefied gas during normal times, had been closed as the conflict between the U.S. and Iran intensified. The blockade created one of the most severe disruptions to energy markets on record, driving fuel costs upward worldwide and creating shortages across multiple industries.

Financial markets reacted positively to the news. Stock indices climbed as investors welcomed the prospect of stabilized energy supplies returning to global markets. The Nasdaq prepared to rebound, while major indices including the Dow and S&P 500 posted gains. Analysts noted that optimism around the Iran deal helped offset concerns about hawkish signals from the Federal Reserve.

American consumers saw immediate relief at the pump. Gasoline prices fell below four dollars per gallon for the first time in months, representing a significant decline from the elevated costs that followed the conflict's start in February. The drop came as supply fears eased with the strait's reopening to tanker traffic.

The reopening also had broader diplomatic effects. Britain dropped its "do not travel" advisory for Dubai, where thousands of British travelers had been stranded when the conflict erupted. The Bank of England held interest rates steady, maintaining its December rate cut as the central bank continued monitoring the impact of high energy prices on the economy.

However, analysts cautioned that full recovery would take time. Energy markets noted that oil and gas prices were unlikely to return to pre-war levels for months, as buyers rushed to refill depleted emergency crude stockpiles. About 20,000 seafarers and 2,000 ships remained stranded in the Gulf, according to the International Maritime Organization, though the reopening should eventually clear these backlogs.

The economic toll of the disruption had spread globally. Germany, Europe's largest economy, halved its 2026 growth forecast to 0.5 percent, while Greece announced 500 million euros in extra aid for households and farmers facing elevated costs for fuel and other essential goods.

Asian markets prepared for mixed trading as investors weighed the positive supply news against lingering uncertainty around Federal Reserve policy and longer-term economic impacts from the extended energy crisis. The reopening of the strait represented a significant diplomatic and economic milestone, though full normalcy in global energy markets remained months away.
