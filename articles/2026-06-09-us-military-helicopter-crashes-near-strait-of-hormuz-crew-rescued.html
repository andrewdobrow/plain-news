U.S. and Iran sign agreement to end war and reopen Strait of Hormuz

The United States and Iran have signed a memorandum of understanding to end their military conflict and reopen the Strait of Hormuz. President Trump signed the agreement remotely ahead of its originally scheduled signing ceremony in Switzerland, with the deal now in effect.

The 14-paragraph memo includes provisions to end fighting, establish that Iran will never develop nuclear weapons, and allocate a $300 billion redevelopment package for Iran. Trump confirmed the signing during a press conference at the G-7 summit in France, where he defended the agreement while appearing to lower his earlier expectations.

The accord aims to stabilize global energy markets and restore shipping through a critical waterway through which roughly 20 percent of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas passed before the conflict. For two months, Trump pursued this deal to address the severe economic consequences of the war.

However, significant obstacles remain. An estimated 5,000 mines that Iran placed in the strait during the conflict will delay reopening shipping lanes. Trump said the U.S. military was already searching for some of these mines. The continued presence of extensive mining will complicate efforts to restore normal traffic quickly.

The global economic impact has been severe. About 20,000 seafarers and 2,000 ships remain stranded in the Gulf. Germany halved its 2026 growth forecast to 0.5 percent, while Greece announced 500 million euros in additional aid for households and farmers. Asian countries dependent on Gulf oil have faced shortages of fuel, fertilizer, and raw materials.

Peace negotiations faced significant delays. Pakistan had attempted to mediate talks in Islamabad, but Iran never publicly accepted an invitation and a U.S. delegation led by Vice President JD Vance never left Washington. Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf had stated that reopening the strait would be "impossible" while U.S. and Israeli actions continued, citing a U.S. naval blockade and other violations of the ceasefire.

Trump has warned he could authorize military strikes against Iran again if nuclear negotiations fail. Critics argue the deal simply returns conditions to their pre-war state, questioning whether the conflict's costs were justified. Trump had initially promised total victory over Iran but ultimately agreed to a negotiated settlement rather than the decisive outcome he initially sought.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu opposed ending the conflict through negotiation, calling instead for continued military action. He had promised the Israeli public "total victory" but had to settle for Trump's memorandum. However, even the United Arab Emirates, previously the most hawkish Gulf state, joined the regional consensus supporting the deal.

The agreement represents a significant shift in U.S. policy toward Iran following weeks of military confrontation and comes as Trump faces pressure to demonstrate concrete achievements from the military action.
