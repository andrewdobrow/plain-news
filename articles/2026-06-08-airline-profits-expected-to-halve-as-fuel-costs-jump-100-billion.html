Airline profits expected to halve as fuel costs jump $100 billion

Global airline profits are projected to drop by half this year as jet fuel costs have surged due to Middle East conflicts disrupting oil supplies. The International Air Transport Association reports that fuel expenses have climbed significantly over the past year, placing severe financial pressure on carriers worldwide.

Airlines are already taking concrete action to manage the crisis. Two million airline seats have been cut from May schedules as carriers redraw their operations in response to soaring jet fuel prices. About 13,000 fewer flights will operate globally next month, according to aviation analytics company Cirium, representing less than 2 percent of global aviation capacity. Istanbul and Munich have recorded the biggest drops in flights, with Turkish Airlines and Lufthansa making substantial cuts. Lufthansa has eliminated 20,000 short-haul flights operated by its CityLine subsidiary.

Budget airlines face particularly acute challenges from the fuel price spike. These carriers operate on thin profit margins and have less flexibility to absorb cost increases compared to larger legacy airlines. However, some budget operators have committed to maintaining full summer schedules. EasyJet and Wizz Air have pledged to operate their summer schedules despite pressure on the unhedged portion of their fuel bills. Most major short-haul airlines operating from the UK are well hedged on jet fuel, meaning they do not expect immediate cost increases.

The industry has limited options to hedge against sharp price movements, and the increases arrive at a time when many carriers were still recovering from previous economic disruptions. Fuel typically represents one of the largest operating expenses for airlines, often accounting for a quarter or more of total costs.

The UK government has introduced contingency measures to manage potential summer disruptions. The transport secretary agreed with the aviation industry on unusual measures including consolidating schedules on routes with multiple daily flights to the same destination. Airlines can now cancel flights with fewer seats sold without losing valuable operating rights for the following season. UK refineries have been asked to maximise jet fuel production under contingency planning, though ministers have resisted industry requests to cut taxes or reduce environmental rules.

International agencies have predicted that Europe faces jet fuel shortages if Middle East disruptions continue. Analysts at Goldman Sachs warned that the UK is particularly vulnerable as Europe's largest net importer of jet fuel, with low inventory levels and reduced domestic refining capacity. UK stocks could fall to critically low levels, potentially requiring rationing measures.

The projected profit decline will be felt across the airline industry, though impacts will vary based on individual carriers' fuel hedging strategies, route structures, and ability to pass costs to consumers through higher ticket prices. The combination of rising fuel costs and operational constraints raises questions about whether some carriers, particularly smaller operators, will sustain operations if fuel prices remain elevated.
