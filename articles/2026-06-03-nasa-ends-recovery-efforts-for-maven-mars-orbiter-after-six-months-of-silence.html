NASA declares Mars orbiter MAVEN mission over after months of silence

NASA has officially ended the MAVEN mission after the spacecraft lost contact with Earth following an orbital anomaly. The orbiter, which has studied Mars for more than a decade, stopped communicating with ground stations over a weekend and has not resumed contact despite extensive recovery efforts.

MAVEN, which stands for Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution, launched in 2013 and reached Mars orbit in 2014. According to NASA, all subsystems were working normally before the spacecraft passed behind Mars. When it reappeared on the other side of the planet, the spacecraft had stopped transmitting data back to Earth.

"Telemetry showed all subsystems working normally before it orbited behind Mars," NASA said in a statement. "The spacecraft and operations teams are investigating the anomaly to address the situation."

Throughout its operational life, MAVEN studied the upper Martian atmosphere and its interaction with the solar wind. The research helped scientists understand a fundamental question about Mars: why the planet lost most of its atmosphere over billions of years. Scientists determined that the sun itself was responsible, as solar wind gradually stripped away much of the planet's air. This atmospheric loss transformed Mars from a potentially wet and warm world capable of supporting life into the cold and dry planet it is today.

Beyond its primary research mission, MAVEN served an important secondary role as a communications relay for NASA's two Mars rovers, Curiosity and Perseverance. The spacecraft transmitted data from these surface rovers back to Earth, supporting their ongoing exploration and scientific discoveries.

The loss of MAVEN leaves NASA with fewer active orbiters around Mars. However, the space agency still maintains two other spacecraft in Mars orbit: the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, launched in 2005, and Mars Odyssey, launched in 2001. Both continue to operate and study the planet.

Mission operators expressed the emotional impact of losing the long-lived spacecraft. The loss was compared to losing a loved one, reflecting the deep connections that develop between teams and the spacecraft they operate over many years of continuous work.

Despite the end of active operations, MAVEN's contributions to science will continue to benefit researchers. The extensive data collected by the orbiter throughout its years of operation will inform scientific understanding of Mars and planetary atmospheres for years to come. NASA has not announced immediate plans for a replacement mission with similar atmospheric research objectives focused on studying the Martian upper atmosphere and its interaction with solar wind.
