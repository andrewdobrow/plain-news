NASA ends Mars MAVEN mission after orbital failure

NASA has confirmed the end of its Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution mission after losing contact with the spacecraft. The orbiter stopped communicating with ground stations over the weekend after operating normally before passing behind Mars. When it reappeared from behind the planet, the spacecraft remained silent.

"Telemetry showed all subsystems working normally before it orbited behind Mars," NASA said in a statement. The spacecraft and operations teams investigated the anomaly, but ground controllers were unable to restore contact or functionality. NASA declared MAVEN in an unrecoverable state after attempting to reestablish communications for six months.

MAVEN launched in 2013 and reached Mars the following year to begin its mission studying the upper Martian atmosphere and its interaction with the solar wind. During its more than a decade in orbit, the spacecraft collected extensive data about how Mars loses atmospheric gases to space. Scientists used MAVEN's findings to understand a fundamental question about the planet's history.

The research helped explain how Mars transformed from a world that may have been wet and warm to the cold and dry environment observed today. The sun itself played a crucial role in this transformation. Solar wind and radiation drove processes that stripped away most of the planet's atmosphere over billions of years, fundamentally changing Mars' climate and surface conditions.

Beyond its primary scientific mission, MAVEN served an important operational function. The spacecraft acted as a communications relay for NASA's two Mars rovers, Curiosity and Perseverance. These rovers have conducted extensive exploration of the planet and made numerous scientific discoveries that depend partly on MAVEN's relay capabilities.

The loss of MAVEN reduces NASA's fleet of active Mars orbiters but does not leave the agency without atmospheric monitoring capabilities. Two other NASA spacecraft continue to operate around Mars. Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, launched in 2005, and Mars Odyssey, launched in 2001, remain active and continue studying the planet and supporting ongoing surface missions.

The mission significantly exceeded its original planned lifespan, continuing to transmit valuable scientific data well beyond initial expectations. MAVEN completed thousands of orbits around Mars during its operational period and made key discoveries about atmospheric escape processes.

Most NASA spacecraft missions at Mars end through planned decommissioning rather than sudden anomalies. The unexpected loss of MAVEN after so many years of successful operation marked an unusual conclusion to the long-running mission. Despite the loss, NASA continues to develop future Mars exploration missions as part of its broader planetary science program, maintaining its commitment to understanding the red planet and supporting ongoing surface exploration efforts.
