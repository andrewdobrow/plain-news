Atlantic ocean current system shows signs of approaching tipping point

New research has significantly increased concerns that the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, or AMOC, is heading toward a critical collapse. Scientists say the findings are "very concerning" because a breakdown of this major ocean system would have catastrophic consequences for Europe, Africa, and the Americas.

The AMOC is already at its weakest point in 1,600 years due to climate change. It functions like a conveyor belt, carrying warm tropical water north to Europe and the Arctic, where it cools and sinks to form a deep return current. The system plays a crucial role in regulating global climate patterns and influences everything from weather systems to fish populations across multiple continents.

A mysterious cold blob in the North Atlantic has served as a visible sign of the circulation's decline. Scientists have also detected hidden warming deep beneath the Atlantic surface, a pattern consistent with a weakening current system.

The new analysis, published in Science Advances, combined real-world ocean observations with climate models to identify which predictions are most reliable. Previous climate models produced widely varying results, ranging from no further slowdown by 2100 to a 65% deceleration. By narrowing down to the most realistic models, researchers found an estimated slowdown of 42% to 58% by 2100, a level almost certain to trigger collapse.

Dr Valentin Portmann, who led the research, stated: "We found that the AMOC is going to decline more than expected compared to the average of all climate models. This means we have an AMOC that is closer to a tipping point."

Professor Stefan Rahmstorf from the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research said: "This is an important and very concerning result. It shows that the pessimistic models, which show a strong weakening of the AMOC by 2100, are, unfortunately, the realistic ones."

Rahmstorf, who has studied the AMOC for 35 years, expressed growing concern about the timeline. He warned that "we may well pass that AMOC shutdown tipping point, where it becomes inevitable, in the middle of this century, which is quite close." He previously argued the risk was too high even when estimated at 5%, but now believes it exceeds 50%.

The AMOC is slowing because Arctic air temperatures are rising rapidly due to global heating. Warmer ocean water is less dense and sinks more slowly, while increased rainfall freshens surface waters, creating a feedback loop that further slows the circulation.

A collapse would shift tropical rainfall patterns that billions of people depend on for food production, plunge Western Europe into extreme cold winters and severe droughts, and add 50 to 100 centimeters to rising sea levels around the Atlantic.

Rahmstorf noted that the slowdown could be even more severe than current predictions because climate models do not account for additional freshwater from melting Greenland ice.
