Construction begins on border wall at Big Bend National Park

Bulldozers have begun construction on border security infrastructure at Big Bend National Park in Texas, drawing fierce opposition from environmental groups, local business leaders, and elected officials. The project marks the first time the Department of Homeland Security has used its waiver authority to install border security infrastructure inside a national park.

The Trump administration waived numerous environmental and historical preservation laws to allow construction to proceed. The waived protections include the National Environmental Policy Act, the Endangered Species Act, the Clean Water Act, and the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, among others.

The current plan calls for a 17-mile vehicle barrier system in four separate locations along the park's river frontage, composed of steel rails and posts measuring 4 to 6 feet tall. The project also includes 205 miles of roads up to 24 feet wide equipped with detection technology, utility poles, lighting, and surveillance cameras. However, the broad waiver gives Customs and Border Protection the authority to build virtually any security infrastructure it wants, including a potential 30-foot steel bollard wall.

The 800,000-acre park draws around half a million visitors annually who come to hike, camp, stargaze, and float the Rio Grande. The area is home to several endangered species, a struggling bighorn sheep population, and extensive Native American rock art and petroglyphs.

U.S. Representative Lloyd Doggett, a Texas Democrat, criticized the project as wasteful in a region where unauthorized crossings are already rare. "Billions of taxpayer dollars are being wasted on this unnecessary project, as Big Bend's rugged mountains make illegal crossings nearly impossible, with crossings in the area accounting for under half a percentage point of all illegal border crossings nationwide last year," Doggett said.

Bob Krumenaker, former Big Bend National Park superintendent and head of the advocacy group Keep Big Bend Wild, expressed concern about the massive environmental impact. "You're looking at some of the most remote parts of a remote national park," he said, describing it as massive destruction.

Krumenaker also raised alarms about the waiver's implications. "Waiving the law undermines all credibility and makes them completely unaccountable to anyone. They don't care about the impact on the environment. If they have, say, a fuel spill, they're not subject to any laws." The waiver casts aside Clean Water Act and Clean Air Act protections, he noted.

Customs and Border Protection said in a statement that its infrastructure plans "in the areas adjacent to the Big Bend National Park and State Park are still in the planning stages, while CBP focuses on other higher priority locations."

Local businesses have organized a coordinated response, with owners pledging to refuse service to contractors involved in the wall project.
