Trump Cancels Next Wave of Strikes on Iran, Says Deal Close

President Trump announced he was canceling planned missile strikes against Iran after claiming the two countries were on the verge of signing a peace agreement. The announcement came after two consecutive days of U.S. military attacks on Iranian targets that threatened to collapse a fragile ceasefire established in April.

Trump made the announcement through social media posts but provided no details about what progress had been made or what terms were being discussed. Iranian leadership quickly disputed his claims, with the foreign ministry saying reports of a deal were "speculative" and that no final decision had been reached.

The timing of Trump's announcement followed escalating military exchanges between the two countries. The U.S. strikes targeted radar and air defense facilities along the Strait of Hormuz in response to the downing of an American Apache helicopter gunship. U.S. officials said the helicopter collided with an Iranian drone, though it remained unclear whether the collision was intentional. The two crew members were rescued in what the military called an unprecedented operation using an unmanned drone boat.

Iran responded to the initial U.S. strikes with its own wave of attacks on Wednesday morning, launching long-range missiles and drones at targets in Kuwait, Bahrain, and Jordan. Iranian officials claimed they struck the U.S. al-Azraq base in Jordan and targeted F-35 fighter jet hangars and command centers. U.S. officials told Reuters that nearly all the missiles and drones were intercepted, with Jordan's armed forces confirming they shot down five missiles before impact.

Trump downplayed the helicopter incident in comments to the Wall Street Journal, calling it "not a big deal" and stating the pilot was fine. He had previously characterized the downing as intentional, blaming Iran and stating the United States could not allow such an action to go unanswered.

U.S. Central Command described the strikes as "a proportional response to unjustified Iranian aggression," signaling the administration was not seeking to escalate toward full-scale war.

Trump had expressed optimism about negotiations earlier in the week, claiming there was "a good chance" of reaching a deal within two or three days. Over the past two months since the April ceasefire, he has repeatedly predicted an agreement was imminent without providing specifics. Vice President JD Vance suggested the conflict could conclude in a week or several months.

Iran's chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, responded to Trump's overtures by stating Iran preferred diplomatic language but spoke "other languages far more fluently," appearing to reference military options.

The conflict has significantly impacted global energy prices and food costs since military operations began in February. Officials have struggled to convert the April ceasefire into a permanent agreement, particularly as Israel continues expanding military operations against the Iranian-backed Hezbollah militia in Lebanon.
