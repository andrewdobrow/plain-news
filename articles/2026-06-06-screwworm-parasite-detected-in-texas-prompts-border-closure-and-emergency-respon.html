Screwworm parasite detected in Texas prompts border closure and emergency response

A flesh-eating parasite that had not been confirmed in the United States for nearly 60 years has been found in a calf in south Texas, about 50 miles from the Mexico border. The discovery of the New World screwworm fly has triggered an emergency response from federal and state agricultural authorities and prompted neighboring countries to restrict cattle imports.

Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins announced the detection late on Wednesday, marking the first confirmed case in Texas since 1966. The parasite, whose larvae feed on the blood and tissue of warm-blooded animals, was eradicated from the United States in the 1970s after causing tens of millions of dollars in losses. Federal and Texas officials had warned livestock owners for more than a year about the parasite's progression through Mexico, where it advanced more than 1,100 miles northward.

The discovery has raised significant concerns for the cattle industry at a time when beef prices are at record highs. While the flies pose no direct threat to food safety, officials worry that an established population could cause shortages if the infestation spreads among cattle and poultry operations. A second infected calf was subsequently identified, and Texas declared a state of disaster over the outbreak.

Canada has responded by banning cattle imports from Texas in response to the outbreak. The movement restrictions reflect concerns about the parasite's potential to establish itself in North America if containment efforts fail.

Rollins stated that "there is no threat of mass infestation" and expressed confidence that the detection would not lead to widespread establishment of the pest in the country. However, Texas Agriculture Commissioner Sid Miller criticized the USDA's response strategy, noting that despite the release of billions of sterile flies, the screwworm had continued advancing toward Texas. Miller called for increased deployment of the screwworm adult suppression system, which uses attractants, bait, and targeted insecticides to reduce adult fly populations before sterile fly releases complete the eradication effort.

The USDA is leading containment efforts with support from the Army Corps of Engineers. In March, the agency announced construction of a new sterile fly production facility at Moore Airbase in Edinburg, Texas, to boost the response. The sterile fly approach has proven effective in previous eradication campaigns, as female screwworm flies mate only once in their lifetime, and eggs from sterile males do not hatch.

This detection comes after an isolated case confirmed in a Maryland resident who had traveled to El Salvador in August 2025, though that individual recovered with no further transmission. The last significant outbreak occurred in the Florida Keys in September 2016, mostly affecting wild deer, and was contained early the following year without spreading further.
