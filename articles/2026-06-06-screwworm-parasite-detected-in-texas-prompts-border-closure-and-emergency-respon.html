Screwworm outbreak disrupts US cattle trade as Canada halts Texas imports

A flesh-eating parasite has triggered emergency measures across North America after a second case of New World screwworm was confirmed in Texas. The discovery prompted Canada to ban livestock imports from the state, marking a significant escalation in response to the disease that had been absent from the United States since the 1960s.

The New World screwworm is a parasitic fly whose larvae burrow through living flesh of warm-blooded animals, including livestock and humans. The condition, known as NWS myiasis, can be fatal if left untreated. The parasite represents the first detection in the US since the disease moved northward from Central America in 2023.

Both confirmed cases occurred within five miles of each other in Zavala County, southwest of San Antonio. The first infection was identified in a three-week-old calf with an umbilical lesion, confirmed by the Texas Animal Health Commission on Wednesday. The USDA confirmed the second case after testing multiple suspect animals.

Texas Governor Greg Abbott declared a statewide disaster on Friday, warning that the region faces "an extraordinarily challenging summer" ahead. Officials are implementing aggressive containment measures, including the release of millions of sterile male screwworm flies in the affected area. When sterile males mate with wild females, they prevent reproduction, a proven method for controlling the pest.

Animal movement restrictions are in place to prevent further spread. The Texas Animal Health Commission stressed that enhanced surveillance, prompt wound treatment, and public education will be critical alongside the sterile fly program. The agency noted it had been preparing for a potential resurgence for over two years.

Canada's food inspection agency announced the temporary livestock ban on Friday, refusing entry to cattle and horses that were in Texas within 21 days before crossing the border. Canadian officials acknowledged that the colder climate makes widespread establishment unlikely, but urged farmers to monitor livestock for wounds accompanied by discharge or foul odor.

The economic impact extends across the border. For more than a year, the US-Mexico border has been closed to live cattle imports as the disease moved northward. This closure has contracted the 100 billion dollar US beef industry to a 75-year low. The US formerly imported more than one million cattle annually from Mexico, representing 4 to 5 percent of all cattle sold for beef production.

The USDA has assured the public that the US food supply remains safe, emphasizing that the screwworm does not infest meat, fruits, vegetables, or other food sources. Texas officials have not announced a timeline for resolving the outbreak or reopening trade channels with Canada and other affected partners.
