Second screwworm infection confirmed in Texas cattle as border closure continues

Texas authorities have confirmed a second case of New World screwworm in cattle, escalating concerns about the parasitic outbreak that has disrupted livestock trade across North America. The infections were discovered about five miles apart in Zavala County, southwest of San Antonio, marking the first detections of the disease in the United States since the 1960s.

The first case was identified in a three-week-old calf with an umbilical lesion. Days later, the U.S. Department of Agriculture confirmed a second infection after testing suspect cases in the area. The New World screwworm is a parasitic fly whose larvae burrow through living flesh, causing a painful condition called myiasis that can be fatal if untreated.

Governor Greg Abbott issued a statewide disaster declaration on Friday and warned that the region faces "an extraordinarily challenging summer" as warmer months increase the risk of disease spread. State and federal agencies are implementing aggressive containment measures, including the release of millions of sterile male screwworm flies in the affected area. These sterile insects prevent wild females from reproducing, a proven eradication strategy that helped eliminate the parasite from the United States decades ago.

The Texas Animal Health Commission has established an infested zone and implemented animal movement restrictions to prevent further spread. Officials are urging ranchers and animal owners to remain vigilant, inspecting livestock for larvae in wounds and reporting any suspected cases immediately. The USDA has assured the public that the food supply remains safe, as the screwworm does not infest meat, fruits, vegetables, or other food products.

The outbreak's economic impact extends beyond Texas borders. Canada's food inspection agency announced a temporary ban on livestock from Texas, prohibiting cattle and horses that were in the state within 21 days of crossing into Canada. However, Canadian officials noted the disease is unlikely to become established there due to the colder climate.

The screwworm's reappearance has compounded existing challenges for the U.S. cattle industry. The U.S.-Mexico border has been closed to live cattle imports for more than a year as the disease moved northward from Central America. This closure has severely impacted the beef industry, which contracts to a 75-year low, as the United States formerly imported more than one million cattle annually from Mexico, representing about 4 to 5 percent of all cattle sold for American beef production.

Texas Animal Health Commission Director Dr. Bud Dinges said the agency has spent over two years preparing for a potential screwworm resurgence and is now implementing those preparations. The combination of sterile fly releases, enhanced surveillance, movement restrictions, prompt wound treatment, and public education represents the established approach to controlling and eradicating the pest.
