Inflation Rises to 4.2 Percent, Highest Rate in Three Years

Consumer prices climbed 4.2 percent over the past year, marking the fastest pace of inflation in three years, according to the latest Consumer Price Index data. The surge represents the third consecutive monthly increase since the start of the Iran conflict, driven primarily by rising energy costs stemming from the closure of the Strait of Hormuz.

Inflation had stood at just 2.4 percent in February, before the Middle East conflict began. It then accelerated to 3.3 percent in March and 3.8 percent in April, before jumping to the current 4.2 percent level as oil prices remained elevated.

The report showed that companies remain reluctant to pass the full burden of higher costs onto consumers. Wage growth has not kept pace with price increases, leaving many households facing diminished purchasing power. The squeeze on consumer spending power comes as businesses navigate elevated input costs across multiple sectors.

Airfare prices jumped 27 percent compared to a year earlier, representing one of the sharpest increases in the inflation report. Energy prices, including surging gasoline costs, contributed significantly to the overall acceleration.

The inflation data arrives just before Federal Reserve Chair Kevin M. Warsh's first policy meeting in his new role. The elevated price growth reduces the likelihood the Fed will cut interest rates in the near term, as policymakers typically raise or maintain higher rates to combat inflation. Markets reacted negatively to the report, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average falling 650 points as traders absorbed both the inflation numbers and escalating tensions in the Middle East related to U.S. military involvement in the region.

The combination of stubborn inflation and geopolitical uncertainty has created a challenging environment for both policymakers and investors. The Fed faces pressure to balance inflation control against economic growth concerns, while consumers confront higher prices without corresponding wage gains to offset them.

The global impact of the conflict extended beyond U.S. markets. In the United Kingdom, government borrowing costs reached their highest levels since 2008, with the 10-year gilt yield climbing to 5 percent. Financial markets began pricing in up to three interest rate increases for 2026. Stock markets on both sides of the Atlantic declined as investors digested reports of increased U.S. military involvement in the Middle East, including potential plans related to Iran's Kharg Island.

The FTSE 100 index fell 1.44 percent, while Germany's DAX dropped 1.9 percent and France's CAC fell 1.7 percent. In the U.S., the S&P 500 and Nasdaq were down approximately 1 percent in afternoon trading on Wall Street. The pound also weakened, falling nearly 1 percent against the dollar.
