Fusion startup Helion raises $465 million to build power plant for Microsoft

Helion, a fusion energy startup supported by Sam Altman, has secured $465 million in funding to advance construction of a power plant intended to deliver electricity to Microsoft. The investment provides the company with crucial resources as it works toward completing the facility by 2028.

Fusion energy technology attempts to generate power by forcing atomic nuclei together, mirroring the energy production process of the sun. This approach differs fundamentally from nuclear fission, which splits atoms and generates radioactive waste. Fusion technology promises cleaner energy with reduced environmental impact, though the field has struggled for decades to achieve sustained commercial-scale energy production.

Helion has entered into an agreement with Microsoft to supply power from its planned fusion plant. The 2028 completion target is ambitious for an industry where similar projects typically require years or decades to reach operational status. The company must successfully demonstrate that its fusion technology operates reliably, generates electricity at scale, and can deliver power to Microsoft's facilities.

The funding round reflects increasing investor appetite for alternative energy solutions as organizations seek carbon-free power sources. Technology companies face mounting pressure to lower their environmental footprint while managing rising electricity consumption from data centers and artificial intelligence infrastructure. Microsoft has announced aggressive climate commitments and is investigating multiple clean energy pathways, including nuclear and renewable options.

Helion operates within a growing field of fusion startups attempting to bring the technology to market. Other companies pursuing fusion development have secured backing from major investors and government support, though none has yet successfully operated a fusion facility that produces more energy than it consumes on a sustained basis. The $465 million funding round positions Helion to advance its technological approach, despite the company still facing substantial technical and regulatory obstacles before achieving power delivery to Microsoft by 2028.

The broader energy sector is experiencing renewed interest in fusion technology as demand for clean electricity accelerates. Large technology and AI companies including Google's parent Alphabet and OpenAI have invested in fusion ventures. This wave of investment comes as the artificial intelligence industry strains existing power grids, creating urgency around developing new energy sources.

The Trump administration has also signaled support for fusion development. Since taking office in January, the government has pursued deregulation of the nuclear energy industry and promoted technology advancement, including executive orders designating fusion development as a national security priority. The Department of Energy has established a new Office of Fusion aimed at preparing the technology for commercial deployment as rapidly as possible.

Despite investor enthusiasm and government backing, fusion energy remains commercially unproven. No utility-scale fusion plant currently operates, and some scientists remain skeptical about achieving commercial viability. Helion's aggressive timeline and substantial funding position it as a significant contender in the race to commercialize fusion energy technology.
