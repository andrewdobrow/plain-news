Fusion startup Helion raises $465 million to build Microsoft power plant

Helion, a fusion energy company backed by Sam Altman, has secured $465 million in funding to advance construction of a power generation facility for Microsoft. The startup is working toward completing the facility by 2028 as part of an agreement with the technology company.

Fusion energy represents an approach to nuclear power fundamentally different from the fission reactors built since the 1940s. Rather than splitting atoms to release energy, fusion works by forcing atomic nuclei together under extreme heat and pressure to replicate reactions occurring in the sun. Proponents view fusion as a potential source of clean, abundant electricity without the radioactive waste associated with traditional nuclear power.

Helion's technology uses powerful magnets to compress plasma to temperatures and pressures capable of causing hydrogen isotopes to fuse and release energy. The company has constructed seven prototype reactors throughout its history and is now moving toward building a commercial-scale facility. The Microsoft commitment marks one of the first major corporate agreements to purchase electricity generated from fusion technology.

The fresh capital will fund the construction and development necessary to meet the aggressive 2028 deadline. Altman, who serves as chief executive of OpenAI, has been a significant investor in Helion and a vocal proponent of fusion energy as a way to meet the enormous power requirements of artificial intelligence data centers. Microsoft itself is expanding its data center capacity to support AI applications and has investigated several alternative energy sources.

The 2028 target represents an ambitious timeline within the fusion industry. Most fusion energy projects have experienced delays and technical obstacles throughout their development. Critically, no fusion plant has yet achieved sustained net positive energy output suitable for commercial purposes. Success by Helion would demonstrate whether private fusion companies can overcome challenges that have confronted government-funded research programs for decades.

The funding announcement occurs amid broader industry momentum. Demand for new energy sources has intensified as artificial intelligence deployment strains electrical grids globally. Tech companies including Alphabet and other major enterprises are investing in multiple fusion startups to develop commercially viable reactor technology. The convergence of AI energy demands and private sector interest has accelerated timelines across the fusion sector.

Helion's approach competes with other fusion technology platforms being developed by various companies. While fusion energy remains in early commercial stages and some scientists question whether it can achieve economically viable production at scale, the combination of corporate demand for clean power and substantial private capital investment has created unprecedented momentum. Whether Helion and competitors can deliver functional fusion plants within projected timelines will significantly shape the future of global energy infrastructure and AI infrastructure development.
