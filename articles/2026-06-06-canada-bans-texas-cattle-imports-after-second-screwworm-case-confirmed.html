Canada bans Texas cattle imports after second screwworm case confirmed

Canada has implemented an import ban on cattle from Texas after a second case of screwworm was confirmed in the state. The discovery prompted Texas to declare a state of disaster as officials work to contain the outbreak.

Screwworm is a parasitic fly larva that burrows into the flesh of warm-blooded animals, including livestock and humans. The parasite can cause severe tissue damage and death without treatment. The United States successfully eradicated screwworm in the 1960s through a sustained campaign, making any reappearance a serious concern for agricultural and public health officials.

The identification of a second infected calf in Texas triggered the state's disaster declaration. This action allows authorities to mobilize additional resources and implement emergency measures to contain the outbreak and prevent further spread. The appearance of a second case suggests the parasite may have established a foothold in the state, raising concerns about broader contamination.

Canada's import ban protects its own cattle industry from the parasite. The restriction will impact Texas ranchers who export cattle across the border, adding economic pressure to an industry already facing containment costs and potential losses. Live cattle trade between the United States and Canada represents a significant portion of cross-border agricultural commerce.

Federal and state veterinary officials are working to determine the source of the infestation and identify how many animals may have been exposed. The response includes quarantines, inspections of nearby herds, and treatment protocols for affected animals. The speed and effectiveness of containment efforts will determine whether the outbreak remains localized or spreads to other parts of Texas and neighboring states.

Screwworm has been absent from the United States for decades due to eradication efforts. Its reappearance in Texas marks a significant development for the livestock industry, which depends on disease-free herds to maintain trade relationships with major trading partners like Canada. The parasite typically enters livestock through open wounds or natural body openings.

The economic consequences of the outbreak extend beyond individual ranchers. Trade restrictions can cascade through the supply chain, affecting processors, exporters, and consumers. Both countries have established protocols for disease management, but the sudden discovery of multiple cases has triggered precautionary measures.

Veterinary teams are collecting samples and conducting epidemiological investigations to understand how the parasite entered Texas. The findings will inform whether additional restrictions are necessary and when trade may resume. Officials anticipate that aggressive containment and treatment efforts will be essential to controlling the outbreak and restoring confidence in Texas cattle exports.

The screwworm outbreak represents a test of cross-border agricultural coordination between the United States and Canada. Both nations maintain strict animal health standards to protect their industries and ensure food safety.
