Flesh-eating screwworm detected in South Texas, first US breach in decades

The USDA confirmed the first case of New World screwworm in South Texas, marking the parasite's first breach of the US-Mexico border in decades. The detection has triggered emergency protocols and renewed concerns about the parasite's potential spread across the country.

The screwworm is a parasitic fly whose larvae feed on living tissue in warm-blooded animals. Female flies lay eggs in open wounds, and when the eggs hatch, hundreds of larvae use sharp mouths to burrow through flesh. Left untreated, the infestation can kill the host. The parasite primarily affects livestock but can also infect pets, wildlife, and humans with open cuts or sores.

The case follows a Maryland patient diagnosed with screwworm infection in August after traveling to El Salvador. While experts say the risk to public health in the US remains low, the case highlighted growing concerns about the parasite's resurgence in Central America and the Caribbean.

"This is not an instantaneous infestation," said Heather Walden, an associate professor of parasitology at the University of Florida College of Veterinary Medicine. "This takes time, this is not like a bacteria or a virus that spreads quickly."

The screwworm was eradicated from the United States in 1966 through an innovative program that released millions of sterile male flies to disrupt breeding cycles. Scientists had successfully pushed the parasite south for decades, establishing a barrier zone in Central America to protect North America from reinfestation.

That progress has now been threatened. Around 2022, the screwworm began moving northward again, likely aided by cross-border livestock trade. About 89,000 cases were detected in animals over three years in Central America, and the parasite now exists only a few hundred miles south of the US border.

"It could infest animals and come to the US and cause serious economic damage to our livestock production here, specifically areas like in Texas, where they have a very large cattle population," said Timothy Schell, acting director of the Center for Veterinary Medicine at the FDA.

The US Department of Health and Human Services declared a public health emergency in August, enabling officials to test existing anti-parasitic medications like ivermectin for controlling screwworm in animals. There are currently no vaccines or established medications to address screwworm infestations.

In response, the USDA reopened a Texas breeding facility for sterile flies and announced an 850 million dollar plan to fight the parasite in August. Federal agencies are expanding monitoring programs to trap flies and continuing inspections of livestock crossing the border.

The new detection underscores the importance of international cooperation. Experts warn that recent budget cuts to scientific research and foreign aid could further imperil the decades-long fight against the screwworm.
