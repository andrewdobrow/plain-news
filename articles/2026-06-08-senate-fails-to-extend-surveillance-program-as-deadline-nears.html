Senate Fails to Extend Surveillance Program as Deadline Nears

The Senate failed to extend a key intelligence surveillance program, leaving lawmakers just days to prevent its expiration. Seven Republicans joined Democrats in blocking the extension, creating uncertainty about the future of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act authorities.

The failed vote came after President Trump's appointment of Bill Pulte as acting director of national intelligence threw bipartisan renewal efforts into disarray. Pulte, a major Republican donor and heir to a home construction fortune with no intelligence experience, replaced Tulsi Gabbard in the role. Senior Democrats immediately warned that the appointment could collapse a fragile bipartisan agreement needed to renew section 702 of FISA before the June 12 deadline.

Mark Warner, the Democratic vice chair of the Senate intelligence committee, directly criticized Pulte's qualifications during a hearing. "What qualifications from my standpoint does Mr Pulte bring to the office? Well, he has shown that he is willing to do anything that President Trump wants, legal or otherwise," Warner said. In an NPR interview, Warner stated that Pulte's appointment had "upended what he described as an already difficult path to renewal of section 702," adding "I do not have the confidence I had yesterday."

Democratic sources indicated privately that a bipartisan deal could collapse if Trump refused to reverse the appointment. Warner reportedly asked Republican Senate Majority Leader John Thune to use his influence with the White House to block the nomination.

Even within Republican ranks, significant opposition emerged. Thune offered a notably cool response to his own president's pick, warning that Pulte would face "a lengthy road ahead of him" if nominated permanently and stating that "we don't need a weaponized" national intelligence director. Former Senate Majority Leader Mitch McConnell said that "anyone performing this role of such immense public trust must have the extensive national security experience required by statute, and no nominee who falls short of this requirement will earn my vote."

Other senior administration officials notably failed to defend the appointment. Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent both declined to endorse Trump's decision during separate congressional hearings.

Chris Murphy, a Democratic senator from Connecticut, expressed concern about potential misuse of intelligence authorities. "The very nature of our collection is now going to be put in the hands of somebody who has a history of seeking out private information for political gain," Murphy said.

Section 702 permits US intelligence agencies to collect communications of foreign targets operating outside the country without a warrant. The program has long attracted criticism from civil liberties advocates concerned about privacy protections, particularly after the FBI used it to investigate Black Lives Matter protesters in 2020.

With Congress facing a compressed timeline to negotiate renewal, lawmakers must bridge significant differences between members supporting the program's extension and those demanding reforms or opposing renewal altogether.
