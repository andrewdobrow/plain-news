SpaceX goes public, shares close up 19% and create world's first trillionaire

SpaceX completed its initial public offering on Friday, with shares closing 19% above the company's $135 IPO price. The debut delivered the world's first trillionaire, as founder Elon Musk's net worth surged past the trillion-dollar threshold following the listing.

The company began trading on the Nasdaq stock exchange with a valuation of approximately $2.1 to $2.2 trillion. According to the Bloomberg rich list, Musk is now worth $1.11 trillion, marking a historic milestone in personal wealth accumulation.

SpaceX had planned to sell 555.6 million shares and raise $75 billion from the sale. The IPO was billed as the biggest stock market launch in history, with up to a quarter of the shares reserved for individual investors rather than institutional funds and banks, which is a larger allocation than is typical for major IPOs.

The space exploration and satellite company had been one of the most anticipated public offerings in years. Investors showed strong enthusiasm for the company's dominance in commercial spaceflight and its Starlink satellite internet network. The robust first-day performance reflected confidence in SpaceX's prospects across space transportation and telecommunications sectors.

Nasdaq rules were adjusted to allow SpaceX to join the index on a fast-track basis. This decision meant the company would benefit from "forced buying" by tracker funds and index funds, providing additional purchasing pressure on the shares. Richard Hunter, head of markets at interactive investor, explained that this guaranteed significant additional buying interest, though it remained uncertain whether SpaceX would have a disproportionate effect on the index weighting.

The company has evolved from its early struggles to become the dominant player in commercial spaceflight. It regularly launches astronauts to the International Space Station and has deployed thousands of Starlink satellites. Its reusable rocket technology has substantially reduced the cost of space access, making it a central player in both government and commercial space operations.

Musk retained 82.4% of the company's voting power and did not sell any of his shares during the IPO, ensuring he maintained significant control over the company's direction.

For UK-based investors, platforms including AJ Bell and Hargreaves Lansdown offered access to the IPO. In the United States, Charles Schwab, Fidelity, Robinhood, SoFi Technologies, and E*Trade facilitated share purchases. Some UK investment trusts, including Edinburgh Worldwide and Baillie Gifford US Growth, already held stakes in SpaceX before the public listing.

The successful debut marked a significant shift for a company that had remained private for over two decades. The IPO validates the commercial spaceflight business model, demonstrating that private companies can build profitable operations in an industry once dominated entirely by government agencies.
