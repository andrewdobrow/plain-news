NASA names four astronauts for Artemis III lunar mission training

NASA announced the crew for its Artemis III mission on Tuesday, revealing the next phase of the agency's efforts to return humans to the Moon. The space agency selected four astronauts who will launch in 2027 for a two-week test flight in low Earth orbit, paving the way for a planned crewed lunar landing in 2028.

The crew includes Randy Bresnik of NASA as mission commander, Luca Parmitano of the European Space Agency as pilot, and NASA mission specialists Frank Rubio and Andre Douglas. NASA also named Bob Hines as a support crew member. Bresnik is a Marine colonel and former commander of the International Space Station with more than 7,000 hours of flying time. Parmitano is a fighter pilot in the Italian air force who previously experienced a near-fatal incident during a 2013 spacewalk when his helmet filled with liquid. Rubio, an Army Black Hawk helicopter pilot and flight surgeon, holds the record for the longest single spaceflight duration by a NASA astronaut at 371 days. Douglas, a systems engineer and Coast Guard reserve officer, has no previous spaceflight experience but trained as a backup crew member for Artemis II.

Unlike the Artemis II mission that looped around the Moon, the Artemis III crew will remain in low Earth orbit to test critical systems. They will practice docking procedures and evaluate life support systems for two competing lunar landers from Blue Origin and SpaceX ahead of the actual Moon landing.

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman described the announcement as marking the creation of "Earth's first starfleet," emphasizing the international scope of the program. He highlighted the numerous spacecraft that will operate simultaneously, including vehicles from NASA, Russia, China, Blue Origin, and SpaceX.

The announcement also addressed recent setbacks facing the program. Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket, designed to carry its lunar lander, was destroyed in a launchpad explosion on May 28. John Couluris, Blue Origin's senior vice-president of lunar permanence, acknowledged the incident but expressed confidence that the company would rebuild and meet the 2027 launch timeline.

Jeremy Parsons, NASA's acting deputy associate administrator for the Moon to Mars program, confirmed NASA's commitment to supporting its partners through technical challenges. "While we recognize there are questions about how Blue Origin's recent anomaly impacts our plans, setbacks are a learning opportunity," he said.

The Artemis program represents an expansion of lunar exploration through international collaboration. The selection of Parmitano marks the first time a European astronaut will participate in a NASA Artemis mission, demonstrating how multiple nations are contributing to humanity's return to the Moon more than 50 years after Apollo 17 in 1972.
