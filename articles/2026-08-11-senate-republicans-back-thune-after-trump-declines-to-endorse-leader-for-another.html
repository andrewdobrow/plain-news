Senate Republicans back Thune after Trump declines to endorse leader for another term

Senate Republicans are publicly backing Majority Leader John Thune after President Trump declined to endorse the South Dakota Republican for another term leading the chamber. GOP senators say they will support Thune's continued leadership, even if Trump decides to back a challenger.

The disagreement between Trump and Thune centers on the legislative filibuster, a procedural rule that requires 60 votes to advance most bills in the Senate. Trump has pushed for eliminating the rule to allow Republicans to pass legislation more quickly, while Thune and other Senate Republicans have resisted the change, arguing it protects minority party rights and the long-term function of the chamber.

The public rift marks a significant moment in Republican Party dynamics, testing whether institutional loyalty within the Senate can withstand presidential pressure. Thune's refusal to abolish the filibuster has put him directly at odds with Trump, who views the rule change as necessary to efficiently advance his legislative agenda.

Senate Republicans' decision to openly support Thune despite Trump's opposition demonstrates that some members of the conference are willing to assert independence from the White House. Their backing suggests that legislative experience and institutional relationships carry meaningful weight against presidential influence, at least within the Senate Republican conference.

Recent events have shown the tension between Trump and Senate Republicans on other issues as well. When some GOP senators voted with Democrats on war powers measures related to Iran, Trump criticized them harshly during a Capitol Hill lunch. Senator Bill Cassidy of Louisiana, who voted with Democrats initially, faced particularly heated criticism from the president, who had previously endorsed a rival candidate against him in a primary.

After receiving a White House briefing on Iran from Vice President JD Vance and Trump's special envoy Steve Witkoff, Cassidy changed his position and voted against a second war powers resolution. Senator Rand Paul of Kentucky also shifted his vote, saying he wanted to give the president space to negotiate. Trump later thanked both senators and Thune for the outcome, indicating he was satisfied with their reversal.

The contrasting situations highlight the complicated relationship between Trump and Senate Republicans. While some senators like Cassidy have been willing to adjust their positions after direct pressure and White House engagement, Thune appears to have maintained his position on the filibuster despite Trump's opposition.

Whether Trump will follow through on supporting a primary challenger to Thune remains uncertain. If he does, it could set up a significant battle over the future of Senate leadership and define how much influence a president can exert over the upper chamber. The outcome could reshape how Senate Republicans balance loyalty to the president against their own institutional priorities and traditions.
