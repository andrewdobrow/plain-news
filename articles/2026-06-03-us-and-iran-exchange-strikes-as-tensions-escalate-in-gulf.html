US and Iran exchange fire in Gulf as tensions test fragile ceasefire

The United States military and Iran conducted strikes against each other in the Persian Gulf on Saturday, marking the latest escalation in a series of flare-ups threatening a recently established ceasefire between the two nations.

US Central Command said it shot down four Iranian attack drones heading toward the Strait of Hormuz and struck Iranian coastal radar installations in response. American military officials characterized the Iranian drones as posing "an immediate threat to regional maritime traffic" and described the strikes on radar sites as defensive measures to "defend against further attacks."

Hours later, Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps announced it had targeted "enemy bases" in the Gulf region. Iran fired seven ballistic missiles toward Kuwait and Bahrain, according to Centcom, which said six were intercepted and one failed to reach its target. The US military reported no casualties among American personnel and dismissed Iranian claims of damaging the US Navy's 5th Fleet headquarters in Bahrain as false.

Bahrain confirmed it came under attack, with air raid sirens alerting residents to move to safe locations. Kuwait's military said it was intercepting drones and missiles launched at the country. Kuwait's foreign ministry condemned the strikes as a "serious escalation" and a "flagrant violation of its sovereignty," reserving the right to defend itself.

The exchange of fire tests a ceasefire agreement that both nations have struggled to maintain. The Strait of Hormuz serves as a critical corridor for global oil and natural gas shipments, and the military confrontation has already sent energy prices climbing. The World Food Programme warned that millions of people were being pushed into hunger due to consequences of the regional conflict, citing soaring energy and food prices.

US and Iranian negotiators have been working for weeks to extend the ceasefire by 60 days and begin new talks on Iran's nuclear program. However, both sides continue to demand changes to any agreement, with neither appearing willing to compromise.

President Donald Trump told reporters on Friday that the situation "seems to be going quite well," predicting a quick resolution either through negotiation or military force. He stated that fertilizer prices would fall significantly once the crisis ended. However, Trump expressed frustration with the pace of negotiations, calling it "a very hard thing" for Iran.

Trump also claimed Iran still possesses approximately 21 to 22 percent of its missiles, a higher figure than the 18 percent he cited the previous month. His administration has emphasized confidence in degrading Iran's military capabilities, though the continued attacks suggest Tehran retains significant capacity.

The latest incident underscores the precarious nature of regional stability and raises questions about whether diplomatic efforts can prevent further military escalation in one of the world's most strategically important waterways.
